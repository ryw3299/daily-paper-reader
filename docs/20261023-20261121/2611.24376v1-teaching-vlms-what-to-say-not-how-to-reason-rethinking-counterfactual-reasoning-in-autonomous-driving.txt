# Teaching VLMs What to Say, Not How to Reason: Rethinking Counterfactual Reasoning in Autonomous Driving 

Hayeon Oh and Donghwan Lee 

Department of Electrical Engineering Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology 

{hahao5252, donghwan}@kaist.ac.kr 

Abstract 

End-to-end autonomous driving systems have been shown to suffer from shortcut learning and causal confusion, limiting their ability to generalize robustly under distribution shift. To mitigate these limitations, recent works based on Vision-Language Models (VLMs) have explored incorporating linguistic reasoning ca-pabilities into driving decision-making, reporting improvements in driving per-formance. In particular, counterfactual (CF) reasoning has been proposed to en-able self-reflective, high-level causal understanding. However, it remains unclear whether the language-based reasoning mitigates shortcut learning or merely shifts it from visual representations to linguistic patterns, creating an illusion of causal understanding. Moreover, existing approaches often conflate intervention-based reasoning with CF reasoning, leaving the effects of genuine CF reasoning unex-plored. In this work, we examine what CF reasoning actually induces in VLM-based autonomous driving and observe that it shifts the action distribution to-ward more conservative behaviors while increasing uncertainty in reasoning and decision-making. This effect persists under reinforcement fine-tuning, suggest-ing that it is not merely a transient artifact. Furthermore, visual attention analysis shows that CF reasoning does not alter visual grounding, while causal intervention experiments reveal that masking causally relevant and non-causal factors in acci-dent scenarios results in comparable decision change rates. Overall, these results suggest that CF reasoning does not induce genuine causal reasoning grounded in visual evidence. Instead, it acts as a linguistic shortcut that reshapes the action distribution, teaching models what to say but not how to reason, highlighting a gap between language-based reasoning and causal understanding in autonomous driving. 

1 Introduction 

Autonomous driving based on the conventional end-to-end paradigm has been consistently shown to suffer from inherent limitations in causal reasoning due to issues such as shortcut learning and causal confusion. To address these challenges, recent studies have explored Vision-Language Mod-els (VLMs), where language-based intermediate reasoning steps such as chain-of-thought (CoT) [1, 2] and chain-of-causation (CoC) [3, 4] have been reported to improve driving performance. In particular, counterfactual (CF) reasoning [5, 6, 7, 8] has been regarded as a promising approach for enabling higher-level causal understanding through self-reflective reasoning, thereby strengthening the expectation that VLMs can acquire genuine causal reasoning capabilities. However, according to the formal definition in [9], CF reasoning defined in prior works [5, 6] often does not correspond to retrospective reasoning that reconstructs past events and reasons about al-

ternative causes. Instead, it is more commonly limited to intervention-level reasoning that considers how actions might change under the current observation [10]. In other words, rather than reasoning over unobserved alternative worlds, it is closer to action adjustment conditioned on the current input. Due to this discrepancy, it remains unclear whether the reported performance gains truly originate from causal reasoning. This raises the fundamental question: what effects does genuine retrospective CF reasoning induce in VLMs for autonomous driving (RQ1) ? More specifically, does it induce genuine causal reasoning, or does the introduction of language as an additional modality instead act as a shortcut that reshapes the action distribution (RQ2) ?In this study, we address these questions by analyzing how CF reasoning, as an intermediate step prior to decision-making, influences model behavior and whether it leads to genuine causal un-derstanding. Accordingly, evaluating these aspects requires scenarios with explicit causal factors. Furthermore, single-frame settings are insufficient to capture key causal cues, such as motion dy-namics, trajectory evolution, and temporal risk accumulation, making temporal context essential. Accident video datasets provide a suitable setting for this evaluation, as they contain identifiable causal relationships and temporally ordered event progressions. Moreover, they enable retrospective counterfactual queries, such as "if the underlying cause had not been present, the accident would not have occurred," making them a suitable testbed for assessing genuine CF reasoning. Beyond scenario design, an appropriate action space and evaluation metrics are required to examine how CF reasoning influences decision-making. Conventional trajectory-based evaluations primarily focus on driving performance, making them less suitable for our objective of analyzing changes in the model’s decision-making behavior and its underlying action distribution. In particular, a trajec-tory lies in a continuous action space, whereas a meta-action represents a discrete driving behavior. Moreover, trajectory is less semantically interpretable than meta-actions, making it difficult to in-terpret changes in the likelihood of semantically equivalent decisions. Consequently, meta-action provides a more interpretable basis for analyzing changes in reasoning exploration. Therefore, we adopt meta-action as the decision space for analysis, following prior work[11, 12, 6, 13, 14]. This enables consistent evaluation across likelihood-based analysis using perplexity and direct exami-nation of changes in distributional diversity through policy entropy. Accordingly, we construct a testbed dataset by integrating existing accident video datasets [15, 16, 17] with explicit causal fac-tors, temporal context, and meta-action annotations. Further details of the dataset are provided in Appendix C. In conclusion, we reveal three key findings. First, we observe that CF reasoning induces a con-sistent shift toward more conservative action distributions, accompanied by increased entropy in language-based decision outputs, indicating that the model generates more diverse linguistic ex-planations while its decisions become biased toward conservative directions.The action distribution shift persists even after reinforcement learning fine-tuning (RFT), suggesting that it is not merely a training artifact. Second, we examine changes in visual attention with and without CF reasoning and find no meaningful differences in attention patterns, suggesting that the reasoning process operates independently of visual evidence. Third, in causal intervention experiments, VLMs exhibit similar levels of decision change regardless of whether causal or non-causal objects are masked, unlike hu-mans selectively change their decisions only when causal factors are masked. This indicates that CF reasoning does not enable the model to selectively identify causal factors. Overall, these results indicate that CF reasoning primarily reshapes action distributions rather than improving the grounding of causal reasoning in visual evidence. We further examine whether this effect extends to general driving scenarios and low-level decisions, as in prior work reporting per-formance gains from CF reasoning[5]. An ablation on CF reasoning data reveals that models trained with CF reasoning consistently exhibit lower rates of lateral maneuvers than those trained without it, suggesting that this shift also manifests under existing CF reasoning paradigms. Given the rel-atively low prevalence of lateral maneuvers in the evaluation trajectories, this shift may align with the dataset’s action distribution, potentially explaining the reported performance gains. Taken to-gether, our findings indicate that CF reasoning may not induce causal reasoning capabilities, but rather functions as a linguistic shortcut that shifts the existing policy distribution. 22 Preliminaries 

2.1 Limitation of Driving Metrics for Evaluating Reasoning 

Metrics such as displacement error between predicted and expert trajectories [18] and overall driving success rates [19, 20] are widely used for evaluating autonomous driving systems. However, while these metrics are effective for quantitative performance comparison, they do not directly reflect the underlying reasoning process behind model decisions. Recent studies further show that strong driving performance does not necessarily imply improved visual understanding or causal reasoning ability. In particular, models can achieve competitive results using only non-visual inputs such as ego status and navigation signals [21, 22], and similar behaviors have also been observed in VLM-based approaches [5, 23]. These findings suggest that current performance gains may stem from spurious correlations or shortcut patterns in the data rather than genuine causal reasoning, making it difficult to isolate the effect of CF reasoning using standard driving metrics alone. Consequently, it remains unclear whether performance gains reflect improved reasoning or dataset-induced shortcuts, making it difficult to isolate the effect of CF reasoning. To address this issue, we adopt evaluation metrics designed to quantify latent reasoning behavior. Detailed definitions are provided in Appendix B. 

2.2 Metrics for Reasoning Capacity and Exploration Pass@k. It measures whether a correct answer is included within the top-k generated outputs, and is commonly used to evaluate a model’s potential capability to solve a given problem [24, 25, 26]. Unlike single-prediction metrics such as precision and recall, Pass@k evaluates multiple candidates per input, allowing us to distinguish between generation capability and selection behavior. In par-ticular, Pass@1 reflects the model’s final decision, while larger values of k approximate the set of feasible decisions that the model can generate. This allows us to determine whether CF reasoning introduces new decisions or instead reshapes the distribution of existing actions. 

Perplexity. If the distribution of decision outcomes shifts under CF reasoning, it is necessary to determine whether such changes merely reflect a redistribution of probabilities or arise from substan-tive intervention in the decision generation process. To this end, we additionally analyze perplexity, which measures the likelihood assigned by a model to a given output sequence, and is defined in Appendix H.2. This quantifies how naturally a model generates a given output sequence under its conditional distribution. This aims to distinguish whether the observed action distribution shift is simply a change in output probabilities or a consequence of CF reasoning acting as a conditioning factor in decision making. Specifically, if CF reasoning is merely an auxiliary process unrelated to decision making, both models are expected to assign similar likelihoods to the same Y. In contrast, if CF reasoning substantively contributes to decision generation, the model generating response with CF reasoning may assign a lower likelihood to the same output when evaluated without conditioning on the reasoning process, which would be reflected as higher perplexity. 

RFT as a Verification Framework. The pass@k and perplexity analyses presented above are con-ducted on supervised fine-tuned (SFT) models that are instruction-tuned to perform CF reasoning prior to decision making. However, it remains unclear whether the observed behavioral patterns are merely artifacts. To address this question, we employ reinforcement fine-tuning (RFT) as a diagnos-tic framework. In autonomous driving, RFT enables policy optimization through explicitly designed reward functions that align learning objectives with driving performance metrics [27, 12, 28, 4]. In this work, we adopt GRPO [29] and its variant GDPO [30], both of which generate G outputs per query and update the model based on relative performance within each group. If CF reasoning indeed acts as an inductive bias that expands the model’s decision-making exploration, it should consistently manifest as higher entropy in CF models, in alignment with the observed action distribution shifts and perplexity changes [35]. 

3 Problem Formulation and Experimental Setup 

We define each sample in the dataset as Y = ( a, e, d, r, y ), where a ∈ { 0, 1} denotes accident oc-currence, e ∈ { 0, 1} indicates ego-vehicle involvement, d represents the scene description extracted from the video, r denotes the CF reasoning component, and y corresponds to the final meta-action decision. Furthermore, we define three longitudinal meta-actions: Stop , Decelerate , and Keep .These span the full range of longitudinal responses from immediate braking to speed maintenance and are mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive with respect to longitudinal behavior in ac-cident scenarios, making them appropriate for analyzing how CF reasoning affects the action dis-tribution. To analyze the effect of CF reasoning, we fine-tune VLMs to generate responses in the following structured format: accident occurrence, ego involvement, scene descriptions, CF reason-ing, and final decision. This ordering reflects the causal structure of retrospective reasoning: the model first reconstructs what happened and why, then determines the appropriate response, encour-aging it to condition its final action on CF reasoning. As a baseline, we fine-tune a model with the same structure but without the CF reasoning component, enabling a controlled comparison of the effects of CF reasoning on decision making. 

4 Analysis of Counterfactual Reasoning 

4.1 Counterfactual Reasoning Induces Behavioral Shifts 

In this section, we evaluate the impact of CF reasoning on VLMs during the supervised fine-tuning (SFT). To verify whether the observed findings consistently generalize across different VLMs, we extend our evaluation to a diverse set of models, including InternVL2-1B, InternVL2-2B, InternVL2.5-1B, Qwen2.5-VL-3B, and VideoLLaMA2-7B, with detailed selection criteria provided in Appendix F. 

Action Distribution Shift. Pass@1 reflects the model’s single-shot decision behavior, capturing how the most confident action changes depending on the presence of CF reasoning. Across most models, CF reasoning consistently shifts predictions toward more conservative actions such as Stop 

and Decelerate , while reducing the proportion of Keep , suggesting a directional bias in decision preference rather than a simple increase in output variability. Extending this observation, Pass@16 approximates the broader set of reachable decisions under multi-sampling and similarly exhibits a shift toward conservative actions. This indicates that the effect is not limited to stochastic single-generation behavior, but instead reflects a systematic reorganization of the model’s underlying action distribution. Overall, these results suggest that CF reasoning induces a structurally embedded and persistent change in the model’s decision space, which becomes more pronounced in larger models, implying stronger internalization of the induced reasoning structure.

However, it remains unclear whether the observed distribution shift is a surface-level artifact caused by the co-occurrence of CF reason-ing tokens or whether it reflects a structural intervention of CF reasoning in the decision generation process that reshapes the model’s action distribution. Policy Entropy as a Dynamic Probe of Exploration. Pass@k and perplexity are metrics that evaluate the resulting action distribution after training convergence; however, they do not directly capture how the policy is formed during learning or how the exploration space evolves over time. To address this limitation, we log and analyze policy entropy throughout the entire RFT process. Since RFT incrementally updates the policy under a KL-divergence constraint with respect to the SFT reference model, it provides a suitable setting for dynamically tracking changes in the action distribution over the full training trajectory. Empirically, models incorporating CF reasoning con-sistently exhibit higher policy entropy throughout RFT. This indicates that CF reasoning alters the exploration structure of the policy during training. In particular, when considered alongside the shift toward conservative actions such as Stop and Decelerate observed in Pass@k, this increase in en-tropy does not reflect a mere rise in randomness. Instead, it can be interpreted as a redistribution of probability mass from a previously over-concentrated preference on Keep toward a more balanced allocation over multiple valid actions. 4.3 CF Reasoning Decouples from Visual Grounding 

In summary, we indirectly examined the impact of CF reasoning on decision generation by focusing on the model’s generated text outputs and their probabilistic characteristics. However, the metrics used in the previous analysis are suitable for studying latent behavioral reasoning, but they do not directly evaluate alignment with causal structure. Therefore, we conduct a more direct investiga-tion to determine whether the observed effects arise from genuine causal reasoning induced by CF reasoning or from linguistic bias in this section. 

Token Activation Map Analysis. If CF reasoning couples with perceptual awareness, the model’s decisions should rely more strongly on the visual causes of accidents, with attention correspond-ingly focusing on relevant risk-related regions. To examine whether CF reasoning induces decision-making bias through genuine visual risk perception, we conduct a visual attribute analysis using Token Activation Maps (TAM) [36], which isolate token-level activations to estimate the visual re-gions on which each output token depends. Unlike methods affected by inter-token interactions, TAM provides a more stable measure of the visual cues used by specific reasoning tokens. Based on this, we compute three metrics: (i) token-level IoU ( IoU tok ), which measures how precisely in-7dividual causal keyword tokens align with accident-causing objects; (ii) aggregated IoU ( IoU agg ), which evaluates whether attention remains consistently focused on the target object over the entire reasoning process; and (iii) grounding F1 ( F 1gnd ), which jointly accounts for precision and recall to balance the completeness and accuracy of attention localization described in Appendix B.6. As shown in Table 3, IoU values vary across models but remain largely unaffected by prompt variations, indicating consistent spatial grounding patterns. However, no clear alignment between attention and accident-causing objects is observed, suggesting that the CF-induced action distribution shifts may occur independently of visual evidence as shown in Figure 14.

Causal Intervention Analysis. Previous results suggest that the action distribution shift induced by CF reasoning may be independent of visual evidence. To test this, we conduct a causal interven-tion study on pre-collision frames, where we remove different visual elements and measure changes in model decisions. Specifically, we consider three masking settings: causal masking (removing the accident-causing object), non-causal masking (removing irrelevant objects), and random masking (removing background regions), and compute the decision change rate by comparing predictions with the original video. As shown in Table 4, human evaluation exhibits the highest sensitivity un-der causal masking, while non-causal and random masking yield much lower changes, indicating strong reliance on true causal factors. In contrast, most VLMs show no such selectivity, with similar change rates across all masking conditions and this behavior persists regardless of CF reasoning or reinforcement learning. Overall, these results suggest that the action distribution shifts are unlikely to reflect genuine causal reasoning, but are more consistent with visual-agnostic linguistic biases.

Investigating Semantic Priors as a Cause of the Action Shift. Furthermore, to investigate whether the conservative shift arises from hazard-specific linguistic priors rather than visual and causal grounding, we conducted a conflict test. We injected either adversarial hazard-related text or safety-neutral text into normal driving videos without accidents and measured the false positive rate (FPR) of Stop and Decel. predictions. We define *hazard specificity* as the difference in FPR between hazard-related and safety-neutral text injections, where a positive value indicates a stronger response to hazard-related text. As shown in Table 6, models with CF reasoning already predict Stop and Decel. in 61–69% of cases, compared with 12–25% for models without CF rea-soning, confirming their conservative bias. Under text injection, No-CF models exhibit near-zero or weakly positive hazard specificity ( +0 .71 to +5 .00 pp), whereas CF models exhibit negative hazard specificity across all three training settings ( −6.50 to −14 .21 pp), indicating that they respond more strongly to safety-neutral text than to hazard-related text. This pattern runs counter to what would be expected if hazard-specific semantic priors were driving the conservative shift. Nevertheless, the elevated FPR of CF models under both types of injection suggests that their predictions are sensi-tive to injected text regardless of its safety-related content. Taken together, these results suggest that hazard-related semantic priors alone cannot account for the observed conservative shift.

Shortcut Learning and Causal Confusion. Ensuring robust performance under distribution shift is a central challenge in autonomous driving. Thus, causal reasoning is regarded as a particularly promising solution due to its invariance to environmental changes compared to spurious correlations. However, existing works based on imitation learning, which learn by mimicking expert demonstra-tions, are inherently vulnerable to shortcut learning [38, 39, 40]. This phenomenon, also referred to as causal confusion, arises when models rely on statistical correlations in the data rather than underlying causal structure. As a result, models tend to depend on superficial patterns instead of causal reasoning. A representative example is the observation that competitive driving performance can be achieved even using only ego-status and navigation information [21, 22]. To mitigate such issues, several studies have proposed explicitly disentangling causal factors [41, 42, 43]. However, these approaches still struggle in complex scene understanding and contextual reasoning scenarios, indicating that shortcut learning remains an open challenge in end-to-end autonomous driving. 

Illusion of Causal Reasoning in VLM for Autonomous Driving. To address limitations of conventional end-to-end approaches, recent studies have explored VLMs for autonomous driving, leveraging language as a coarse semantic representation to better capture complex scene contexts and achieving improved performance in closed-loop settings [10, 44, 27]. Building on this, prior work has introduced intermediate linguistic reasoning steps such as chain-of-thought [1], chain-of-causation [3], and counterfactual reasoning [6], leading to consistent performance gains and an implicit assumption that such reasoning reflects genuine causal understanding. However, it remains unclear whether these improvements stem from causal reasoning or from shortcut learning induced by the language modality, as standard driving metrics are confounded and insufficient for analyzing internal reasoning processes [23]. Recent evidence from CausalDriveBench [45] further highlights this limitation, showing that causal QA accuracy is uncorrelated with trajectory prediction accuracy across driving VLAs, indicating that conventional driving metrics alone cannot assess whether a model’s linguistic reasoning reflects genuine causal understanding. To address this, we introduce a framework to evaluate the behavioral effects of CF reasoning by quantifying the induced shifts in a discrete meta-action space and validating whether these shifts persist under reinforcement learning, thereby ruling out training artifacts. This enables a systematic analysis of the gap between linguistic shortcut and genuine causal understanding. 

7 Conclusion and Limitations 

In this study, we investigate the effect of counterfactual (CF) reasoning in VLMs for autonomous driving. Our results indicate that CF reasoning consistently induces an action distribution shift, and that these changes primarily originate from language-level reasoning rather than genuine causal reasoning grounded in visual observations. This finding suggests that CF reasoning can guide the model in determining what to say, but does not necessarily teach the model how to reason. In other words, the behavior shift is more plausibly attributed to the learning of a language-induced spurious correlation, rather than the internalization of genuine causal reasoning. However, this study focuses on open-source VLMs that allow access to internal probability distribu-tions and learning dynamics. Consequently, the generalizability of our findings to closed-source or specialized VLMs [4, 6] remains uncertain, as our analyses based on perplexity and policy entropy require access to internal model probabilities. Therefore, our conclusions are limited to settings where such internal signals are observable, highlighting the need for future work to extend CF rea-soning analysis beyond these constraints. We further discuss potential causes of the observed action distribution shift and outline future directions in Section A.

Why Does CF Reasoning Induce Behavioral Shifts Without Visual Grounding? A key factor is that CF reasoning in current VLM-based autonomous driving systems is primarily learned at the language level. During supervised fine-tuning, supervision is provided only in the form of textual reasoning outputs, without explicit alignment between these texts and the corresponding visual evi-dence. As a result, CF reasoning is optimized as a language modeling objective rather than a visually grounded causal reasoning, leading to a natural decoupling between linguistic reasoning and visual causal structure. This interpretation is consistent with our observations of invariant attention patterns and non-selective responses under causal intervention. These findings further challenge the implicit assumption that performance improvements from language-based reasoning necessarily reflect gen-uine causal understanding. In practice, both LLMs and VLMs exhibit fundamental limitations in stable causal inference, particularly under distribution shift in long-tail scenarios [46, 47, 48]. 

Is Visual Grounding Sufficient for Causal Decision-Making? To address the limitation, prior work has proposed leveraging counterfactual consistency to encourage reliance on visual evidence rather than purely linguistic patterns [49] and further explored integrating LLM-based agents with causal world models, extending decision-making beyond next-token prediction toward structured en-vironments that support intervention and counterfactual reasoning [50]. These directions highlight the importance of developing CF reasoning as a learning signal that is aligned with physical ground-ing, rather than relying solely on linguistic supervision. However, visual grounding alone does not necessarily ensure that the resulting reasoning is effectively reflected in downstream decision-making. In other words, establishing a correspondence between reasoning and visual evidence is distinct from ensuring that such reasoning appropriately guides the model’s actions [51, 52]. 

Future Directions. Despite their ability to capture rich correlational structures and derive coarse semantic representations of scenes in autonomous driving through large-scale pretraining [53], VLMs still lack reliable causal reasoning capabilities. Therefore, the implicit assumption that language-based reasoning enables VLMs to perform causal reasoning in autonomous driving, and that improvements in driving performance necessarily reflect genuine causal understanding, war-rants reconsideration. This calls for evaluation paradigms that go beyond conventional driving per-formance metrics to explicitly examine the gap between causal understanding and downstream driv-ing behavior. In particular, evaluating the alignment between reasoning and action could provide insights into whether the model’s reasoning genuinely informs its decisions, rather than merely ac-companying behavioral improvements. Furthermore, given the substantial challenges involved in endowing VLMs with explicit causal rea-soning capabilities, an alternative research direction is to pursue more tractable objectives that mit-igate the influence of causal confounders, such as linguistic shortcuts. Rather than directly solving the broader problem of causal reasoning, identifying and suppressing such shortcuts may offer a practical step toward more reliable and causally grounded decision-making [42, 41, 43].

C.2 Automated Annotation Pipeline 

To address these limitations, we introduce a multi-stage automated annotation pipeline to con-struct a dataset for analyzing whether counterfactual reasoning truly affects safety-relevant performance from existing accident driving videos. Drawing inspiration from the visual question answering methodology proposed in [66], we leverage the advanced multimodal capabilities of Qwen2.5-VL-32B-Instruct to generate structured captions [44, 6]. Our annotation pro-cess produces six key components for each video: accident occurrence, ego-vehicle involvement, scene description, counterfactual question, counterfactual answer , and driving decision . The pipeline consists of three sequential steps: • Step 1: Scene Recognition and Description. First, we feed the raw accident video into 

Qwen2.5-VL-32B-Instruct to extract fundamental scene information. The model is prompted to identify whether an accident occurred ( accident occurrence ) and whether the ego-vehicle was involved ( ego involvement ). Simultaneously, it generates a detailed textual explanation of the traffic scenario ( description ), focusing on the behavior of road agents and environmental conditions. • Step 2: Counterfactual Reasoning Generation. Based on the description obtained in Step 1, we guide the model to generate a counterfactual question and its corresponding 22 Figure 4: The automated annotation pipeline for the dataset. 

counterfactual answer . This step aims to augment the dataset with reasoning capabilities by asking "what-if" scenarios (e.g., "what would have happened if the ego-vehicle had not braked?" ), thereby enhancing the model’s understanding of causal relationships in safety-critical situations. • Step 3: Decision Making. Finally, we synthesize the decision for the original video. The model aggregates all prior information—accident status, ego-involvement, scene description, and coun-terfactual reasoning—to deduce the optimal driving maneuver (e.g., stop, decelerate, keep). This ensures that the final decision is logically grounded in both the observed reality and potential alternative outcomes. 

C.3 Quality Assurance 

Figure 5: Diagnosis tool for quality assessment. To ensure the reliability of our dataset while maintaining the scalability of the construction process, we implemented a rigorous quality assurance protocol. Unlike traditional datasets that rely on labor-intensive manual labeling, we leverage the zero-shot reasoning capabilities of 23 Qwen2.5-VL-32B-Instruct to establish an automated pipeline, which we then statistically val-idate through a detailed human audit. 

Verification Protocol. We randomly sampled 100 videos from our dataset for a formal verifica-tion. Independent annotators evaluated each sample in terms of factual correctness (i.e., the accuracy of the scene description relative to the visual content), counterfactual plausibility (i.e., the logical and physical soundness of the hypothetical scenarios), and decision appropriateness (i.e., the safety and optimality of the predicted driving actions).

H.1 Policy Entropy 

def calculate_policy_entropy(logits, completion_mask, temperature=1.0): """ Computes the length-normalized policy entropy for generated completions. Args: logits (Tensor): Shape (Batch, Seq_Len, Vocab_Size) completion_mask (Tensor): Shape (Batch, Seq_Len). 1 for completion, 0 for prompt. temperature (float): Scaling factor for logits. Returns: float: Average entropy per token (invariant to sequence length). """ # 1. Temperature Scaling # Adjusts the sharpness of the distribution. logits = logits / temperature # 2. Compute Entropy Per Token # H(t) = - sum(p(x) * log p(x)) probs = softmax(logits, dim=-1) log_probs = log_softmax(logits, dim=-1) # Shape: (Batch, Seq_Len) per_token_entropy = -sum(probs * log_probs, dim=-1) # 3. Apply Completion Mask # Filter out prompt tokens (entropy = 0 for prompt positions) masked_entropy = per_token_entropy * completion_mask # 4. Length Normalization (Critical Step) # Calculate effective length of the generated completion sequence_length = sum(completion_mask, dim=-1) # Divide total entropy by sequence length # This ensures the metric reflects ’uncertainty density’ rather than total uncertainty. policy_entropy = sum(masked_entropy, dim=-1) / clamp(sequence_length, min=1.0) return mean(policy_entropy) # Average over batch

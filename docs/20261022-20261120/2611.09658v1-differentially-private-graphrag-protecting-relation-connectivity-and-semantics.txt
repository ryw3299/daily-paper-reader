## Abstract 

Graph Retrieval-Augmented Generation (GraphRAG) connects in-formation across documents through entity relationships to sup-port relational reasoning. However, black-box access to GraphRAG systems can enable adversaries to reconstruct knowledge graphs and recover both structural connectivity between entities and the semantics of their relationships. Existing defenses lack formal pri-vacy guarantees and provide limited protection against such re-construction attacks. We propose RelGuard , the first differentially private framework for GraphRAG, which provides formal privacy guarantees by mitigating connectivity leakage and protecting re-lations and their semantic meaning through relation removal. We formalize relation differential privacy (relation-DP), which bounds the distinguishability of mechanism outputs according to the se-mantic distance between alternative relations while accounting for relation removal. Building on this definition, we develop a four-step defense mechanism that sanitizes source documents before GraphRAG indexing by perturbing relation predicates or removing their associated connectivity using the exponential mechanism. Experiments across three GraphRAG systems and two targeted re-construction attacks demonstrate that RelGuard provides stronger protection against reconstruction attacks than four existing defense mechanisms while better preserving downstream utility.

## 1 Introduction 

Graph Retrieval-Augmented Generation (GraphRAG) integrates knowledge graphs, which represent entities and their relationships, with unstructured text corpora to support knowledge retrieval, domain-specific question answering, and multi-hop reasoning in Web services [9, 14, 25]. By modeling relationships among entities, GraphRAG enables information scattered across documents to be connected and retrieved through graph structures, enhancing the relational reasoning capabilities of large language models (LLMs). However, the relational information that enables these capabil-ities also creates privacy risks. We consider two complementary dimensions of relation privacy: (1) structural connectivity , which reveals whether two entities are connected in the knowledge graph, and (2) relation semantics , which reveals the nature of the rela-tionship between connected entities. An adversary who infers con-nectivity can discover previously unknown associations between entities, while recovering relation semantics can further reveal the specific nature of those associations. Recent studies have demon-strated that adversaries can recover such information by recon-structing knowledge graphs through black-box access to GraphRAG systems [13, 21, 26, 29]. Although existing defense mechanisms at-tempt to mitigate these reconstruction attacks [13, 26, 32], they lack formal privacy guarantees, leaving the extent of information leakage difficult to characterize and control. To address this limitation, we propose RelGuard , the first dif-ferentially private framework for GraphRAG, protecting relation semantics while mitigating connectivity leakage. Our key insight is that relation protection must account for both the existence of a relationship and its meaning when present. Simply suppressing edges can protect connectivity but may unnecessarily impair down-stream reasoning, whereas replacing relation predicates without accounting for edge removal does not provide a unified treatment of both privacy dimensions. First, we formalize relation differential privacy (relation-DP) ,a metric-based privacy definition that calibrates output indis-tinguishability to the semantic distance between alternative relation predicates while also accounting for the possibility of relation removal. This formulation bounds the distinguishability of released relation semantics, while relation removal helps protect connectivity. Second, we develop a four-step defense mechanism that sanitizes private documents before they are indexed by GraphRAG. Specifi-cally, RelGuard : (1) extracts canonical entity–relation triples from the private documents; (2) generates candidate relation predicates for each extracted relation; (3) perturbs each relation by sampling a replacement predicate or a null outcome, which suppresses the corresponding connectivity; and (4) reconstructs sanitized docu-ments by consistently updating relation expressions or removing sentences associated with null outcomes. GraphRAG then builds its index from the sanitized documents and uses the resulting index for query answering. This design integrates formal relation privacy protection into the GraphRAG pipeline while seeking to preserve the information needed for accurate question answering.

We evaluate RelGuard on three GraphRAG systems and two real-world datasets, comparing it against four existing defense mecha-nisms. RelGuard achieves the lowest relation recovery F1 across all the settings and the lowest connection recovery F1 in most settings. In particular, it reduces relation recovery F1 by 26.98%– 44.35% compared with the strongest baseline. Meanwhile, RelGuard 

achieves the best privacy–utility trade-off, limiting the reduction in question-answering F1 to just 2.98%–8.97%. These results demon-strate that RelGuard provides stronger protection against relation reconstruction while preserving high utility for downstream ques-tion answering. In summary, we make the following contributions: 

• We introduce relation differential privacy (relation-DP) , a metric-based privacy framework for formally protecting relation se-mantics and mitigating connectivity leakage in GraphRAG. 

• We design a relation-DP-based defense mechanism that sanitizes source documents before GraphRAG indexing, jointly protecting relation semantics and connectivity through relation substitu-tion and removal. 

• Our extensive empirical evaluation demonstrates the advantages of RelGuard over existing defenses in terms of both privacy protection and utility preservation. 

## 2 Background 2.1 Graph Retrieval-Augmented Generation 

Graph Retrieval-Augmented Generation (GraphRAG) uses graph-structured knowledge to retrieve connected evidence for LLM gen-eration [9, 14, 25]. Systems either retrieve from existing knowledge graphs or construct graph indexes from documents [25]. We focus on document-based GraphRAG, which extracts entities and rela-tions from source text and retains associated passages as evidence.

## 3 Related Work 

Privacy inference in GraphRAG. RAG systems are vulnerable to text extraction [6, 19] and membership inference [20]. GraphRAG attacks further recover entity connections and relation semantics from generated answers. Untargeted attacks seek broad graph recov-ery: AGEA uses graph memory and novelty-guided exploration [29], while GraphSteal expands entity neighborhoods through breadth-wise traversal [13]. Targeted attacks recover relations around a spec-ified entity. GraphSteal uses depth-wise search, whereas GRASP frames extraction as structured relation processing with record identifiers and adaptive queries [26]. We evaluate both connection and semantic relation recovery under targeted attacks. 

Privacy defenses for GraphRAG. Existing defenses modify gener-ation instructions or retrieved contexts. Protective Prompt restricts disclosure of retrieved content [13], while Abstractive requires query-relevant paraphrasing [21, 26]. GRASP’s ID Alignment col-lapses record identifiers, and Decoy adds misleading endpoint fields to disrupt structured extraction [26]. These defenses aim to reduce extraction but provide no formal privacy guarantees. In contrast, our framework provides relation-DP guarantees through document sanitization before indexing. 

Metric-DP on text. Prior work applies metric-DP to word embed-dings [11], sentence representations [2], and LLM-generated sub-stitutes [1]. Pr 𝜖𝜖 mpt combines metric-DP with format-preserving encryption for sensitive prompt values [5], while TDP substitutes sensitive entities in LLM contexts [31]. These methods protect tex-tual units rather than explicitly addressing both the semantics and existence of relationships between entities. Our framework targets

relation-level privacy, combining semantic replacement and rela-tion deletion to protect relation meaning and mitigate connectivity leakage.

## 4.2 Relation Differential Privacy 

While conventional DP [7, 8] provides a rigorous framework for limiting the information revealed about private data, its standard formulation typically assigns the same privacy bound to all neigh-boring inputs. This uniform treatment does not distinguish between relation substitutions that preserve similar semantics and those that substantially change the meaning of a knowledge graph fact. On the other hand, metric-DP generalizes conventional DP by incorporat-ing a distance function over inputs, allowing the privacy guarantee to vary with the distance between them. Motivated by this flexibility, we introduce relation differential privacy (relation-DP) , which in-stantiates the metric-DP principle for relations by defining privacy in terms of semantic distances between relations. ## 4.3 Threat Model and Defense Goal 

Attack goal. Given a private document 𝐷 and a target entity 𝑠 target 

appearing in it, the adversary aims to infer sensitive information associated with 𝑠 target from GraphRAG answers. Specifically, the attack targets two types of information: 

• Connectivity leakage : The adversary aims to identify which object entities in 𝐷 are directly connected to 𝑠 target .

• Relation semantics leakage : The adversary aims to infer the se-mantic meaning of the relationship between 𝑠 target and each directly connected object entity. An inference is considered successful if the inferred relation is semantically equivalent to the true relation, even if its exact representation differs. 

> 3

Figure 1: Threat model. Adversary capabilities. We consider a strong, knowledge-rich adversary who knows the identities of all object entities appearing in the private document 𝐷 , but has no prior knowledge of the relationships among them. This assumption reflects scenarios in which entity identities are publicly available or already known to the adversary. It also establishes a conservative threat model in which privacy protection does not rely on concealing the entity set, but instead focuses on protecting sensitive relationships among known entities. Although the adversary is strong in terms of prior knowledge, we assume that it interacts with GraphRAG as an external user and therefore has only black-box access to the service. This setting reflects practical deployments in which the underlying document, graph index, retrieved context, and model parameters are not ex-posed to users. The adversary can submit natural-language queries, observe generated answers, and adapt subsequent queries based on information accumulated from previous responses. However, it can-not directly inspect the private document or the system’s internal components, nor can it modify the document or index. Document poisoning and system compromise are outside our threat model. Figure 1 illustrates the threat model. Suppose a private docu-ment states that Alice works for Company B, where Alice is the target entity 𝑠 target . The adversary knows the identities of Alice and Company B, as well as all other object entities appearing in the private document, but has no prior knowledge of the relationships among them. Through black-box queries to the GraphRAG system, the adversary may infer whether Alice is connected to Company B and, if so, the semantic meaning of their relationship. For example, an answer describing Alice as an employee of Company B reveals both the Alice–Company B connection and its employment seman-tics, even without explicitly reproducing the predicate works_for .By issuing adaptive follow-up queries, the adversary may attempt to identify all object entities directly connected to Alice and infer the semantic meaning of each corresponding relationship, thereby reconstructing Alice’s entire one-hop neighborhood. 

Defense objective. We aim to design a defense mechanism that mitigates the disclosure of sensitive relations involving 𝑠 target along two complementary dimensions: structural privacy , which limits the ability to infer whether 𝑠 target is connected to other entities, and 

semantic privacy , which limits the ability to infer the meanings of these relations. At the same time, the defense should preserve the utility of the protected document for downstream question answer-ing (QA). To achieve these objectives, we develop a relation-level perturbation mechanism guided by the relation-DP guarantee (Defi-nition 3), with the goal of protecting sensitive relational information while retaining sufficient semantic and structural information for accurate QA. 

## 5 Methodology 

Given a private document 𝐷 and privacy budget 𝜖 , RelGuard gen-erates a sanitized document e𝐷 for GraphRAG indexing and query answering. Figure 2 shows four steps: (1) extract canonical entity– relation triples and record their occurrences; (2) generate type-conditioned candidate relations; (3) perturb relations using these candidates and null ; and (4) reconstruct e𝐷 from the perturbed triples. We describe each step below. 

## 5.1 Relation Extraction 

The first step of RelGuard is to extract subject–relation–object triples from the private document 𝐷 . We use a large language model (LLM) for this task [18, 23]. The LLM extracts only rela-tions supported by the document, excluding unsupported, negated, or hypothetical relations. The extraction prompt is provided in Appendix B. To ensure consistent representation, entity aliases and unam-biguous coreferential mentions are mapped to the same entity iden-tity, and semantically equivalent relation expressions are normal-ized into snake_case predicates while preserving subject–object direction. For example, both Alice works at Company B and Al-ice is employed by Company B are normalized to the same triple, 

(‘Alice ′, works_for , ‘Company B ′).

## 5.2 Relation Candidate Generation 

Next, RelGuard generates candidate relations for perturbing each extracted triple. The key idea is to construct candidate pools based on the types of the subject and object entities, rather than the orig-inal relation. Specifically, RelGuard follows two steps to generate the candidates: (1) Entity typing : it assigns each entity a type from a fixed inventory; (2) Candidate generation : it generates a pool of candidate predicates for each observed relation based on the types of its subject and object entities. All triples with the same subject and object entities use the same candidate pool. This design makes generated relation candidates independent of the extracted ones and avoids generating a separate pool for every triple. Next, we explain the details of the two steps.

## 6 Experiments 

We organize our evaluation around three research questions: 

• RQ1: How does RelGuard compare with existing defenses in protecting GraphRAG against reconstruction attacks on connec-tivity and relation semantics? 

• RQ2: What trade-off does RelGuard achieve between privacy protection and downstream question-answering (QA) utility? 

• RQ3: How do different parameter settings affect the privacy protection and QA utility of RelGuard ?

## 6.1 Experimental Setup 

We run experiments on a server with an NVIDIA A100 GPU with 40 GB of GPU memory. We implement the algorithms in Python using PyTorch and report the mean over three trials. 

Data, models, and GraphRAG systems. We evaluate privacy pro-tection and utility on HotpotQA [30] and 2WikiMultiHopQA [17]. Their source documents form the protected corpus, while valida-tion questions and reference answers are used for QA evaluation. We evaluate three mainstream GraphRAG systems: LightRAG [14], PathRAG [4], and HippoRAG 2 [16]. The victim generators are two closed-source models, GPT-4o-mini [24] and Gemini-2.5-Flash. Results for Gemini-2.5-Flash appear in Appendix C. We use GPT-5-mini to perform relation extraction and candidate generation in the DP-protected document reconstruction pipeline. We encode relation predicates using nli-roberta-base-v2 . Unless otherwise specified, we use 𝐾 = 10 and 𝜆 = 1.0. 

Recovery Attacks. We employ two state-of-the-art black-box re-construction attacks against GraphRAG systems: GRASP [26] and 

GraphSteal [13]. Both attacks recover both entity connections and relation semantics within the target entity’s one-hop subgraph. Appendix D provides the attack settings. 

Privacy metrics. We measure Connection Recovery (Conn.) and 

Relation Recovery (Rel.) , following the distinction between endpoint and relation matching [26]. Conn. checks whether the attacker iden-tifies the correct directed entity pair, regardless of the predicted predicate. Entity aliases are mapped to canonical names before comparison. Rel. additionally uses GPT-4o-mini to judge whether the predicted predicate captures the true relation’s meaning. Ap-pendix E defines the matching rules and precision, recall, and F1 calculations. 

Utility metrics. Following prior graph-based RAG evaluations [15, 16], we measure QA utility using token-level F1, the harmonic mean of token precision and recall. We follow each benchmark’s

## 7 Conclusion 

We proposed RelGuard to mitigate connectivity and relation se-mantics leakage through GraphRAG answers. RelGuard replaces sensitive relations based on semantic distance or deletes them, then consistently updates documents before indexing for use with GraphRAG systems. RelGuard provides relation-DP guarantees over relation values, and mitigates connectivity leakage through deletion. Experiments on two datasets across three GraphRAG sys-tems show that, compared with four existing defenses, RelGuard 

substantially reduces relation recovery with limited QA utility loss in most settings, while also reducing connection recovery.

## D Attack Details 

We use GRASP and targeted GraphSteal to evaluate the two attack objectives in Section 4.3: connectivity inference, which identifies entities directly connected to the target, and relation semantics recovery, which identifies how these entities are related to the target. 

GRASP. GRASP [26] anchors retrieval on the target entity and frames extraction as a structured relation-processing task, request-ing records with an instance identifier, source entity, relation predi-cate, and destination entity. The endpoints support inference of the target’s one-hop connections, while the predicates support recov-ery of relation semantics. Instance identifiers associate fields within each record to reduce cross-record mixing. We preserve relation direction and restrict predictions to target-incident relations. New predictions are identified against the accumulated triples; instance identifiers are excluded from scoring.

## E Privacy Metric Details 

Connection Recovery (Conn.) checks whether the attacker identifies the correct directed entity pair, regardless of the predicted predicate. Entity aliases are mapped to canonical names before comparison. 

Relation Recovery (Rel.) additionally checks whether the predicted predicate captures the true relation’s meaning. GPT-4o-mini accepts semantically matching predicates or broader predictions entailed by the true relation. For example, if Alice is Bob’s spouse, predicting that Alice is Bob’s family member counts as relation recovery; predicting a spouse relation from a true family-member relation does not. Predictions without predicates are evaluated only for connection recovery. Precision measures the fraction of predictions that are correct; recall measures the fraction of true items recovered. ## F Defense Baseline Details 

Abstractive. Abstractive [21] instructs the generator to select query-relevant evidence and express it concisely in its own words. We use the system-prompt implementation evaluated by Song et al. [26]. The instruction supplements the base prompt and returns 

NO_RELEVANT_CONTENT when the context is irrelevant. 

ID Alignment. ID Alignment [26] assigns the same displayed identifier (e.g., 1) to every retrieved relation record before passing the context to the generator. Endpoints, relation descriptions, and row order are preserved. Collapsing identifiers removes the record-level distinction used by structured extraction and can induce cross-record mixing. The transformation applies during context serialization, leaving the stored graph unchanged. 

Decoy. Decoy [26] adds misleading src_entity and dst_entity 

columns to retrieved relation records while retaining the true src 

and dst fields. The added values differ from the true endpoints. A system instruction identifies the added fields as internal metadata to ignore in answers. This presentation can steer extraction toward incorrect endpoint assignments while preserving the true evidence for QA. Decoy is evaluated separately from ID Alignment. 

Protective Prompt. Protective Prompt implements the Protective System Prompt mitigation in GraphSteal [13]. It prepends a protective instruction to the generator’s system prompt, directing the LLM not to disclose content retrieved from the knowledge base. The source corpus, stored graph, and retrieved evidence remain unchanged. The instruction applies when answering both benign and attack queries; attack predictions are extracted from the resulting answers.

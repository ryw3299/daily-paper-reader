Abstract 

Large language models (LLMs) read knowledge-graph facts ranked by relevance, leaving open whether they suffice or conflict. Our checks use relation knowledge, written by hand or learned from training questions labelled with their relation paths. It selects a compact context of facts yielding an answer, and derives it; a derivability check tests whether an answer follows, a consistency check whether facts conflict, and a validation-chosen routing gate decides where the derived answer replaces the LLM’s. Seven open LLMs read the compact context better in exact match than relevance-ranked facts; on ComplexWebQues-tions (CWQ) they match SubgraphRAG’s facts at a quarter of the prompt tokens, and stronger LLMs read SubgraphRAG’s better. Where the routing gate passes, ours leads SubgraphRAG’s in exact match in all 53 LLM, benchmark and prompt settings. A precision gate, returning the derived answer only when the check passes with exactly one derived entity, answered 18 and 5 percent of held-out WebQSP and CWQ questions in a pre-specified test, right for 90 and 96 percent. The consistency check judges facts by path evidence only where another path joins their entities, true of 2.5 percent of named-entity facts in our compact WebQSP and CWQ contexts. 

1 Introduction 

Large language models (LLMs) often answer questions from facts that a retriever selects from a knowledge graph (Baek et al., 2023; He et al., 2024; Edge et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025). Such an answer deserves trust only when the facts are sufficient , so that combining them yields the answer, and consis-tent , so that no fact contradicts another. Relevance ranking alone establishes neither. Retrievers keep the most relevant facts (Baek et al., 2023; Li et al.,

2025), and path generators keep the paths a fine-tuned model plans (Luo et al., 2024). Explorers let the LLM judge when its facts suffice (Sun et al., 2024), but LLMs often answer even from contexts that lack a necessary fact (Joren et al., 2025). We study what checks outside the LLM establish about both properties and where they fail. The checks rest on explicit relation knowledge , knowl-edge of what relations mean and how they combine. It takes the form of rules, such as “the father of one’s sibling is one’s own father”, or of a mapping learned from training questions. Such a mapping learns, for instance, that “the ethnicity of X’s wife” asks for the relations spouse and ethnicity. A set of facts is sufficient when this knowledge derives an answer from it. In Figure 1a, two of the eleven facts suffice to tell how t is related to s; they form a compact sufficient context . A selector searches the graph for such a context, and the answer the knowledge derives from it is the derived answer . A 

derivability check tests whether an answer follows from a context. A consistency check tests whether the context’s facts conflict. Gates are decision rules fixed on validation questions. From the check results and the number of derived answers, a gate returns the derived answer, the LLM’s answer or an abstention (Figure 1b). open LLMs that answer without a reasoning phase, as well as GPT-5 (OpenAI, 2025) and LLMs that reason before answering. Section 5 asks whether a compact sufficient context helps LLMs read facts. Section 6 asks what the checks catch and miss, and Section 7 asks who should answer. Answering by following in the graph the relation chain of similar training questions, that is, executing it as a query, and falling back on an LLM is not new (CBR-KBQA, Das et al., 2021; DecAF, Yu et al., 2023; Section 2). What is missing is evidence on when each part helps; we contribute three findings. (1) We show that facts selected by relation knowledge are easier for LLMs to read: on PathQuestion, MetaQA and WebQSP, the seven open LLMs answer more accurately in exact match from our compact contexts than from relevance-ranked facts, with about a tenth to a third of the prompt tokens of SubgraphRAG’s facts. (2) We show that a passing derivability check is a precision signal, not a guarantee, and turn it into a precision gate that answers without an LLM. On WebQSP and CWQ questions set aside in advance, 81.5 and 62.1 percent of the derived answers that pass the check are right; also requiring exactly one derived answer raised this to 89.8 and 95.9 percent, on 17.6 and 4.9 percent of the questions (Tables 2 and 10). (3) We identify when a consistency check outside the LLM can work: it judges a fact by path evi-dence only where a second path in the context joins the fact’s two entities. It detects 83 to 100 percent of the contradictions we inject, most of which have such a path by construction, and we find that only 2.5 percent of the fact mentions between named en-tities in our compact WebQSP and CWQ contexts have one, whereas larger contexts often do. 

2 Related work 

Selecting graph facts. Most pipelines rank facts by relevance and keep as many as a budget allows. They rank by similarity to the question (Baek et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2024), gather facts into a connected subgraph or a community summary (He et al., 2024; Edge et al., 2024), or score triples with a model or with attention (Li et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2026). Explorers let the LLM judge after each step whether its facts suffice (Sun et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024a), TRACE (Wang et al., 2026) 2steers relation choice by earlier explorations, and Self-RAG (Asai et al., 2024) trains a critic of its retrieval. We keep the first setting, an LLM reading selected facts, add the two checks outside the LLM and count how often LLMs answer without a needed fact (Appendices I and K). 

Relation paths and semantic parsing. Relation paths are generated by a fine-tuned model (Luo et al., 2024), retrieved per sub-question (Kim et al., 2023) or scored against the question (Liu et al., 2024a; Zhang et al., 2024). WebQSP parsers execute a parsed query (Yih et al., 2016), rank generated logical forms (Ye et al., 2022) or adapt those of similar questions (Das et al., 2021), as our chains do; DecAF (Yu et al., 2023) combines executed queries with generated answers, as our routing does. SELF-KBQA (Tian et al., 2026) gen-erates a query from retrieved subgraphs and, when it fails to execute, runs the query of the top-ranked subgraph. We share the executed query and the fallback and add two findings: a query can execute and still misread the question, and on human-written questions the size of the derived answer set can beat the check as a confidence signal. 

Error detection and context effects. Knowl-edge graph refinement (Paulheim, 2017) judges single facts against a whole graph by embeddings, local or path evidence or implying paths (Bordes et al., 2013; Xie et al., 2018; Melo and Paulheim, 2020; Lao and Cohen, 2010; Das et al., 2017; Galárraga et al., 2013). We instead test a fact against the rest of a context and calibrate the test on consistent contexts, as conformal anomaly detection does (Laxhammar and Falkman, 2015; Bates et al., 2023). We find that calibrating false alarms to 1 percent over all contexts leaves them far above 1 percent in the few contexts where the check has evidence to judge a fact. Irrelevant facts, needed facts in a bad position and the order of premises lower accuracy (Shi et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2024; Yoran et al., 2024; Cuconasu et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024b; Chen et al., 2024b; Saparov and He, 2023); we measure these effects on contexts known to be sufficient. Knowledge conflicts usually pit the context against the model’s training data (Longpre et al., 2021; Xie et al., 2024); ours lie inside the context. Joren et al. (2025) label context sufficiency with an LLM-based evaluator and analyse how answering models behave under those labels. We instead test whether a predicted relation query executes on the context, much as justification finding derives the axioms that entail a consequence (Schlobach and Cornet, 2003; Kalyanpur et al., 2007; Horridge et al., 2008; Baader and Peñaloza, 2010; Peñaloza and Sertkaya, 2017). Such a query can execute even when it misreads the question, which Section 6 measures. 

3 Method 

Setting and relation knowledge. A knowledge graph G is a set of triples (s, p, o ), each stating that 

o is the p of s. A question asks for the relation between two entities, (s, ?, t ), as in CLUTRR, or for the entities reached from a topic entity by a se-quence of relations; a context is a subset of G writ-ten into the prompt. Rules form an ontology: a hi-erarchy mapping surface relations to base relations, such as sister to sibling; inverse and symmetry ax-ioms; and composition rules p◦q → r, a p step then a q step being an r step; CLUTRR has such rules. 

Learned mappings. The other benchmarks have no rules, so their relation knowledge is learned from training questions and the relation paths an-notated for them. A learned mapping outputs, for a question, the relations it asks for and, on WebQSP and CWQ, also answer constraints, such as male for “brother”. The selector then keeps the paths from the question’s topic entity that realise these relations, and their ends form the derived answer. For “who did vladimir lenin marry?”, the predicted relations spouse_s, from a person to a marriage record, and spouse, from the record to each partner, lead from Vladimir Lenin to Nadezhda Krupskaya, the derived answer (Figure 1e). No step reads a test question’s answer or annotation. On PathQuestion and MetaQA, whose questions follow templates, the mapping is a lexicon that links question words to relations. The lift of a word for a relation is how many times as often the relation lies on the anno-tated paths of training questions containing the word as on those of all training questions. A word is linked to a relation when the lift is at least 1.3, a threshold chosen on held-out training questions (Appendix A). On PathQuestion’s two-hop ques-tions, ethnicity lies on the paths of all 15 training questions containing “ethnicity” but of 2.5 percent of all training questions, a lift of 40.1 (Figure 1d). Each word names the linked relation that lies most often on the paths of the training questions contain-ing it. A path passes when its relations are exactly those the question’s words name (Figure 1d). MetaQA also gets a classifier , a naive Bayes model 3over words and word pairs that predicts the ordered relation sequence. On WebQSP and CWQ, whose questions people wrote, each training question has an annotated chain , the relations and constraints of its annotated SPARQL query. For a test question we find the 10 most similar training questions by MPNet sentence embeddings (Song et al., 2020), with the topic’s name removed. Each of them votes for its own chain with a weight equal to its similarity (Figure 1e). The winning chain must hold 60 percent of the votes, and a constraint is applied when it carries at least half of that chain’s votes. CWQ uses 20 neighbours, 80 percent and a constraint share of 30 percent, all chosen on validation questions (Appendices F and G). 

Compact sufficient contexts. A context C is suf-ficient for a question when the relation knowledge derives an answer from C alone; with several an-swers, C must yield all that the whole graph yields. The selector enumerates paths from the question’s entities, shortest first: walks of up to ten edges be-tween s and t, or paths of up to three edges from a topic entity. With rules, the selector tests the paths in turn: it maps each relation to its base relation, inverts it where the path runs against a stored fact, and asks whether the composition rules reduce the sequence to one relation, a parse that the CYK al-gorithm (Younger, 1967) performs with the rules as a grammar; it returns the facts of the first path that reduces, in path order. With a learned map-ping the selector returns every path realising the predicted relation sequence (Appendix A). We call the result a compact sufficient context ; if no path passes, the selector returns the best candidate, if any, marked insufficient. The derivability check ap-plies the same derivation to any context, and its re-sult is the derived answer . On WebQSP and CWQ the derived answer is executed on the full question graph, whereas the context shown to the LLM is cut at 100 facts; the cut can remove evidence needed to recover the complete answer set (Appendix K). 

What a passing check means. With rules, a pass-ing derivability check means that the rules entail the answer from the context. With a learned mapping three claims come apart: the predicted relations yield an answer from the context; the context holds their full answer set, as it does when the selector built it and nothing was cut; and they are the rela-tions the question asks for, which can fail. The con-sistency check, a separate test, can flag a passing context (Appendix K). On questions not used for testing, the derived answer where the check passes is right for over 95 percent with CLUTRR’s rules, MetaQA’s classifier and PathQuestion’s lexicon, but for 85.0 and 61.6 with WebQSP’s and CWQ’s chains (Appendices A and G). The PathQuestion, WebQSP and CWQ questions among them also set the lexicon’s lift and the chains’ settings. 

Consistency checks. A consistency check asks whether a fact of the context contradicts the others. With rules, it composes, for every fact (s, p, o ),the other facts into a relation between s and o

and detects a conflict when that relation disagrees with p. In Figure 1c, a false fact states that s is a parent of m, whereas the rules derive from the fact that m is the sister of s that the two are siblings. Without rules, a learned check estimates from the graph which relations hold between the two ends of a path. Random walks over the graph count, for each sequence of relations, which relation the graph stores between the walk’s two ends; a fact 

(s, p, o ) is suspicious when p is rarely stored at the ends of the paths that the other facts form between 

s and o. Appendix J works through an example. A second score asks the same of TransE relation vectors (Bordes et al., 2013), under which a path’s relations add up to the relation it implies. A context takes the score of its most suspicious fact, as one false fact suffices, and this score is com-pared with consistent calibration contexts of sim-ilar size: the context’s share is the fraction of them that score at least as suspiciously, so a small share marks an unusual context. The context is flagged when the smaller of its two shares falls below a threshold set so that 1 percent of all calibration con-texts are flagged. Separately, a relation that has one value for at least 99 percent of its subjects in the graph, such as a person’s place of birth, is treated as single-valued, and a context that gives a subject two values of it is flagged. No contradicted example is used to fit the scores or to calibrate the threshold; the decision rule was chosen on synthetic contra-dictions in training contexts and the default config-uration post hoc on the probe results of Appendix J. 

Deciding who answers. Routing returns the de-rived answer where the routing gate passes and the LLM’s answer elsewhere; it reads the derivability check and the derived set only. The consistency check enters as a variant (Table 5) and in the contradiction experiments, where a flagged context ends in an abstention reporting the conflicting facts (Table 7). On PathQuestion and MetaQA the 4routing gate is the derivability check. On WebQSP and CWQ we choose it among 43 candidate rules, each deciding from whether the check passes and how many answers are derived when to return the derived answer: “the check passes”, “at most 

t answers are derived”, “the check passes and at most t are” and “the check passes or at most t are”, with t from 1 to 10, 15, 20, 50 or unlimited. On validation questions we take the rule under which routing gives the seven open LLMs the highest mean exact match. It is “the check passes or there are at most 20 derived answers” on WebQSP and “exactly one derived answer” on CWQ, the one-answer gate (Table 10, Appendix L). The precision gate requires the check to pass and one derived answer; it is meant for answering without an LLM where precision matters more than coverage. The 43 rules were defined after test comparisons and the precision gate after its CWQ test precision had been seen; held-out questions then confirmed both gates’ pre-set precision criteria (Section 6). 

4 Experimental setup 

Benchmarks. CLUTRR generates family stories and asks how two people are related, with 22 kin-ship labels; we use 4,361 test questions with chains of 2 to 8 facts. PathQuestion asks two- and three-hop questions about people in Freebase (Bollacker et al., 2008), a large collaborative knowledge graph (782 test questions), and MetaQA one- to three-hop questions about films in WikiMovies (Miller et al., 2016), a film knowledge graph (900). WebQSP has 1,628 test questions written from search queries, and CWQ 3,531 that extend WebQSP’s queries by composition, conjunction, comparison and superlatives; the CWQ questions built from one WebQSP question form a seed cluster . Both use the Freebase question graphs released with RoG. 

Contexts and baselines. We compare our context with all facts of the story on CLUTRR or the one-hop neighbourhood of the question’s entities, cut at 300 facts on MetaQA and WebQSP; and the k facts most similar to the question under Sentence-BERT sentence embeddings (Reimers and Gurevych, 2019), with k our context’s size. We also use the annotated path, a reference context built from the question’s gold relation path, and four published methods (Appendix E): KAPING (Baek et al., 2023), up to 10 facts; SubgraphRAG (Li et al., 2025), up to 100, its retriever retrained on PathQuestion and MetaQA, also cut to our con-text’s size and to smaller budgets; Think-on-Graph (Sun et al., 2024), at width and depth 3; and RoG (Luo et al., 2024), which plans relation paths. Ev-ery context goes into the same prompt for the same LLMs; on WebQSP ours and SubgraphRAG’s facts are also read with its own prompt. 

Probes. Probes alter compact contexts to test what the checks catch and what LLMs do without them (Appendix A). On CLUTRR we delete a nec-essary fact, giving a depleted context ; replace one relation by friend, neighbour or coworker; add 2 to 16 distractors; shuffle the facts; or add a false fact: a reversed-edge contradiction adds, to the fact that 

m is the sister of s, that s is a parent of m, and a far contradiction relates two people at least two steps apart against the rules. PathQuestion and WebQSP get their own probes (Appendix A), and on all three false facts are also put in the graph before selection. 

Prompts and scores. The forced-choice prompt 

requires an answer; the abstention prompt adds the reply that the facts are insufficient, and the status prompt also the reply that they contradict each other (Appendix A). Answer sets are scored by ex-act match, Hit (some gold answer) and F1; answers of GPT-5 and Qwen3-32B cut at the output limit in Tables 6 and 12, re-asked with a larger limit, move no difference by over 1.3 points (Appendix M). 

Models and statistics. We use seven open instruction-tuned LLMs, Llama-3.2-3B (Meta, 2024), Llama-3.1-8B (Grattafiori et al., 2024), Qwen2.5-7B (Qwen Team et al., 2024), EXAONE-3.5-7.8B and EXAONE-3.5-32B (LG AI Research et al., 2024), Gemma-3-27B (Gemma Team et al., 2025) and Qwen3-32B (Yang et al., 2025), quantised to 4 bits and decoded greedily without reasoning. On a 564-question subset (264 CLUTRR, 150 PathQuestion and 150 MetaQA) we add Qwen3-32B with reasoning, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-70B (DeepSeek-AI et al., 2025), GPT-5 with and without reasoning, and GPT-5-mini (OpenAI, 2025); GPT-5 also reads every WebQSP and CWQ question, with and without rea-soning, and GPT-5 and Qwen3-32B with reasoning every PathQuestion, MetaQA and CWQ question (Appendices G and M). 5 Result 1: when compact contexts help 

This section asks whether the same LLM under the same prompt answers more accurately from the compact context than from the facts other methods select; we compare exact match per LLM and its mean over the seven open LLMs (Table 1). 

Questions written by people. On WebQSP, our contexts follow the chains that our learned mapping predicts and hold 14.7 facts on average. The seven open LLMs read them more accurately in exact match than SubgraphRAG’s 100 facts, by 10.2 points on average (Table 1) and by 4.6 to 13.0 points for each LLM, and than RoG’s planned paths; SubgraphRAG’s facts hold every gold an-swer more often and give more hits (Appendices E and F). On CWQ, 18.6-fact contexts are read by the seven open LLMs about as accurately in exact match as SubgraphRAG’s 100 facts at a quarter of the prompt tokens, with lower Hit and F1, and above RoG’s paths. Strong LLMs read Sub-graphRAG’s facts better, by 3.5 to 7.6 points of ex-act match (Appendix G); on all WebQSP questions GPT-5 with reasoning reads both alike in exact match (Appendix M). Most of the gain needs anno-tated chains: with chains from shortest question-to-answer paths instead (weak supervision) the open LLMs lead SubgraphRAG’s facts on WebQSP only in exact match (47.4 against 43.9) and trail on CWQ in all three metrics (Appendix H). With our context, SubgraphRAG’s own prompt lowers every open LLM’s exact match by 3.5 to 15.7 points against our forced-choice prompt (Appendix E). 

Where the routing gate passes, the compact context leads. SubgraphRAG’s Hit lead comes mostly from questions that fail the routing gate (Appendix H). Where it passes, our context leads SubgraphRAG’s 100 facts in exact match in all 53 settings, each one LLM reading one benchmark under one prompt, significantly in 52 of the 53, by a median of 15.1 points (1.2 to 34.4), and in F1 in 50 of the 53, trailing significantly in 2 (Table 6, whose caption also gives the pass rates; Appendix H). Settings share LLMs, so we also count units: all 9 LLMs lead, as do all 6 model series (sign tests, p ≤ 0.031 ). On WebQSP, MetaQA and CWQ the passing questions are easier for SubgraphRAG too, so the lead is conditional, not causal; its facts cut to our context’s size still lose there, by 19.8 to 25.0 points for every open LLM on WebQSP (Appendix H). 

What each part contributes. On WebQSP our context instead of SubgraphRAG’s facts gains the open LLMs 10.2 points of mean exact match, and routing to the derived answer adds 5.0; the gain thus comes from selecting the context and from returning the derived answer; picking the questions that get the derived answer by the vote share instead of the routing gate changes it by under one point (Table 5, Appendix H). 

Templated and rule-made questions. In exact match the compact context beats KAPING’s, Think-on-Graph’s and SubgraphRAG’s facts for every LLM on PathQuestion and MetaQA, by about 7 points or more ( p ≤ 7 × 10 −6), and GPT-5 and Qwen3-32B with reasoning read it better than 6SubgraphRAG’s facts on all their questions, by 5.0 to 12.9 points ( p ≤ 7.5 × 10 −6), with fewer hits on MetaQA (Appendix M). It needs one LLM call and about a tenth to a third of SubgraphRAG’s prompt tokens (Table 1). On CLUTRR the compact context, which for 96.6 percent of questions is the shortest path between the two people, beats the whole story by 2.1 to 5.2 points for six LLMs; there the rules matter for the checks, not for finding the facts (Appendices B and A). 

6 Result 2: what the checks catch 

This section asks how often a passing derivability check has a right derived answer per kind of rela-tion knowledge, whether a cheaper estimator does as well and what the consistency check catches. 

The derivability check is a precision signal. On CLUTRR, gating an LLM on the check returns its reply where the check passes and abstains oth-erwise. It raises the LLM’s balanced score , the mean of its accuracy on sufficient contexts and its abstention rate on insufficient ones, by up to 31 and 37 points with a necessary fact deleted or a rela-tion replaced. On the 330 strict two-fact questions, whose kinship label real families cannot change, the three largest LLMs already abstain on depleted contexts themselves, so gating raises their balanced score by at most 2 points (Appendices I and C). On WebQSP a pass shows that the predicted chain can be followed in the context to an answer, not that it is what the question means: with its constraints, it matches the question’s annotated chain for 79.3 percent of passing questions (Appendix K). Is the check the best signal for trusting the derived answer? We compare it with seven other confidence signals, such as the vote share alone and SubgraphRAG’s top-1 triple score, by risk ,the share of returned derived answers that are wrong, at equal coverage , the share of questions given one. Derived answers are most accurate on bench-marks with reliable relation mappings. Table 3 compares, per benchmark, the derived answer with the best open LLM and GPT-5 where the routing gate passes (routing on all questions: Table 11). With CLUTRR’s rules and MetaQA’s classifier al-most all contexts pass and the derived answer is above every LLM; with PathQuestion’s lexicon 77 percent pass, the derived answer beats every LLM, and routing beats every LLM alone, GPT-5 by 3.3 points (Appendix M). On CLUTRR the derived answer applies the rules that generated the labels, which the LLMs are not given; given them under the status prompt, GPT-5 with reasoning answers 89.0 rather than 44.7 percent of the subset correctly (Appendix M). With WebQSP’s and CWQ’s chains, less accurate behind the check, the derived answers are level with the three largest open LLMs and at most level with GPT-5 where the routing gates pass. Behind the precision gate they are right for 91.3 and 96.4 percent (Table 2), level with or above every LLM there (Appendices G and L). 

On WebQSP and CWQ, validation ques-tions choose who answers. Validation questions choose, per open LLM, one of six policies, such as the LLM alone or routing (all six: Appendix L), by accuracy or by the equal-cost score , which counts 

+1 for a right answer, −1 for a wrong one and 0 for an abstention; test and held-out questions then score it by the same criterion. No chosen policy was significantly worse than the LLM alone on the criterion that chose it. On held-out questions, rout-ing raised the accuracy of the four smaller open LLMs by 4.7 to 9.3 points and changed that of the three largest by −0.8 to +1 .4, so H2 held only for the smaller four. H3 held on CWQ (+2.6 [+1.4, +3.9]) but was not distinguished on WebQSP (+0.3 [−1.5, +2.1]; Appendix N). 8 Conclusion 

Relation knowledge selects compact contexts that open LLMs read better in exact match than relevance-ranked facts on PathQuestion, MetaQA and WebQSP, with far fewer prompt tokens than SubgraphRAG’s; on WebQSP most of this gain needs annotated chains, and on CWQ strong LLMs read SubgraphRAG’s facts better. A passing deriv-ability check does not guarantee a right answer, but a precision gate fixed in advance met its precision criterion on a small share of held-out questions, and no policy chosen per open LLM on validation questions was significantly worse than that LLM 8alone on its own criterion. A consistency check outside the LLM can judge a fact by path evidence only where a second path joins its entities, which few facts in our contexts have; graph contamination remains open. 

Limitations 

The reach of the derived answer on human-written questions is small and depends on seen chains. Of WebQSP’s test questions, 94.7 percent have a relation chain seen in training; the 63 with only unseen chains pass the check for 4 questions, all wrong, and two-relation chains pass with 60.7 percent right against 87.0 for one relation (Appendix F). On CWQ the check passes 11.8 percent and the one-answer gate 24.5 percent, and no comparative or superlative question passes with a right answer (Appendix G). Our derived answers reach 64.4 F1 on WebQSP, below semantic parsers such as RnG-KBQA (Ye et al., 2022) and DecAF (Yu et al., 2023), which report 75.6 and 77.1 to 78.8 (Yu et al., 2023); SubgraphRAG is the only strong retriever we run, and the others are cited. The chains come from annotated training questions or SPARQL queries; weakly supervised chains read 6.0 to 7.6 points worse on WebQSP and trail SubgraphRAG’s facts there in Hit and F1, and on CWQ in all three metrics. WebQSP and CWQ use RoG’s question graphs, which hold no gold answer for 19 percent of CWQ’s test questions and keep no dates or num-bers. Questions whose gold query has a date, nu-meric, ordering or literal constraint, 10.3 percent of WebQSP and 28.6 of CWQ, pass with 22.0 and 3.1 percent of derived answers exact, against 84.1 and 61.1 on reachable questions without one. Literals and unreachable answers explain a minority of the wrong passing answers; the rest, 108 and 137, are mostly wrong chains, constraints or answer sets, with other graph gaps such as missing or renamed answers (Appendix K). The checks read the compact context, so a false fact that becomes the selected path escapes them: a substituted relation or object in the graph yields an undetected wrong answer for 97.9, 41.1 and 43.1 percent of the CLUTRR, PathQuestion and WebQSP questions given one (Appendix J). Apart from the natural-error study, missing, false and irrelevant facts are studied through synthetic probes. The natural-error labels are LLM judge-ments against Wikidata, not the authors’: two LLM passes, the first by the same LLM that drafted the labelling rules, then a tie-break; an indepen-dent GPT-5 pass leaves the conclusion unchanged but changes the error count, so the labels should not be reused as verified facts (Appendix J). The learned check detects only contradictions the graph expresses through co-stored relations, paths or sin-gle values, is calibrated for small contexts, and its CLUTRR training families come from the bench-mark’s generator. CLUTRR’s labels come from the rules the checks use, so there the derived answer shows agreement with the rules rather than real kin-ship, and about 47 percent of its questions admit a step or in-law reading (Appendix C). The open models, up to 32 billion parameters and quantised to 4 bits, cover the full study. Rea-soning mode and the proprietary models cover a 564-question subset; GPT-5 also reads every We-bQSP and CWQ question, and GPT-5 and Qwen3-32B with reasoning every PathQuestion, MetaQA and CWQ question. Reasoning LLMs report con-tradictions themselves, at about 250 to 720 output tokens per question. The benchmarks are public and may be in the LLMs’ training data; on We-bQSP the open LLMs answer rightly from their own knowledge for at most 4.7 percent of depleted contexts (Appendix K). The study is in English, the prompts were not tuned per model, repeated runs vary by up to 1.2 points, and the selector presumes that the question’s entities are identified. Apart from the post hoc 90 percent line, the choice of path counts, the gates’ candidate family and the precision gate, defined after test comparisons, the constants were set by hand on training or valida-tion data; Appendix A gives their sensitivity and the steps for a new graph. 

Ethics statement 

All data are synthetic or come from public bench-marks; the knowledge-base facts about real, mostly public, people are used only as released, and no new personal data is collected. The checks let a pipeline abstain rather than guess, but they are only as sound as the relation knowledge behind them, and trusting them beyond that is a risk: incom-plete knowledge reports some sufficient contexts as insufficient, wrong knowledge returns a wrong answer as derived, and a learned consistency check misses contradictions of kinds that its graph does not express. A passing check should be shown to users as an answer derived under the predicted rela-9tions, with no conflict of the supported kinds found, not as a verified answer. 

Use of AI assistants. We used generative AI tools to help write code, draft and revise the manuscript, and find and correct errors. The au-thors reviewed all code, results and text and take full responsibility for the content of this paper.

A Setup details 

Complete tables. The appendices state the num-bers the paper cites; the supplementary material gives, under the same letters, every per-LLM and per-condition table and the details the appendices defer to it. Beyond Section 4, CLUTRR adds 2, 4, 8 or 16 distractors attached to the path or among people absent from the story, and 1, 2 or 4 reversed-edge or 1 or 2 far contradictions. PathQuestion reverses a family fact or makes a person their own grandparent’s parent; WebQSP reverses a family or containment fact, gives a single-valued relation a second value or deletes a fact of the predicted chain’s paths; Appendix J enters false facts before selection (reversed edges and facts, far facts, shortcuts, substitutions, second values). All conditions of a benchmark are built from the same graph and questions, so every comparison is paired. 

Prompts. On CLUTRR the prompt restricts the answer to exactly one of the 22 kinship labels, lists the facts as one sentence each and ends with the question (verbatim: the supplementary mate-rial). On PathQuestion, MetaQA and WebQSP the prompt states that each fact is written as (subject) --[relation]--> (object) , asks for the answer entities exactly as they appear in the facts, sepa-rated by “ | ” when several are correct, and ends with the question. The line permitting the reply INSUFFICIENT_CONTEXT appears only in the abstention prompt, to which the status prompt adds, before it, the line “If the FACTS contra-dict each other, output exactly: CONTRADIC-TORY_CONTEXT”; applied unchanged to clean, insufficient, contradicted and distracted contexts, the status prompt lets an LLM’s reports of a contra-diction be counted as detections and as false alarms. 

Relation knowledge. The CLUTRR rules are the benchmark’s own relation store: 11 base relations, each with a male and a female label, 4 inverse axioms, 2 symmetry axioms (we add a third for sibling-in-law, which the store omits) and 16 com-position rules. The rules omit some compositions, such as the uncle of a sibling, so the shortest path cannot be composed for 158 questions (3.6 percent); for 149 of them the selector finds another composable path, and for the other 9 it returns the shortest path marked insufficient, so 4,212 compact contexts (96.6 percent) are the shortest path. About 47 percent of the questions involve one of four com-positions, child of a grandparent, child of an uncle or aunt, spouse of a child and spouse of a grand-child, whose CLUTRR answer admits a step or in-law reading; Appendix C repeats the main com-parisons on questions free of them. PathQuestion’s lexicon is P (relation family | question word )

fitted on training questions and their annotated paths, a relation family grouping Freebase relations ending in the same name; it keeps only words that raise a family’s probability to at least 1.3 times its base rate (the lift) and is complemented by overlap of relation names with question words. MetaQA’s lexicon is of the same kind, and its supervised classifier is a naive Bayes model over question n-grams trained on about 5,000 questions per hop subset. The hops of a MetaQA question’s subset bound the length of the relation sequence; PathQuestion searches paths of up to three edges. Appendices F and J give WebQSP’s relation knowledge and the learned consistency check. 

Benchmark details. From PathQuestion we use PQ-2H and PQ-3H, two- and three-hop questions over graphs of 1,211 and 2,839 triples, and PQL-2H, two-hop questions of the larger PathQuestion-Large (PQL) release over 4,247 triples. Questions whose gold path contains a triple from an entity to itself, an artefact of entity names that collide in the released graphs, are dropped from the test sets and SubgraphRAG’s training but kept for fitting the lexicon, which uses only their words and relations; PQL-3H, where most questions are affected, is dropped. Training and test questions have disjoint topic entities (80/20); the test sets are 300 sampled questions each from PQ-2H and PQ-3H and all 182 remaining test questions of PQL-2H (of 348). MetaQA uses the WikiMovies graph of 133,582 triples over 9 relations. Its annotated paths execute each question’s released relation chain on the graph, keeping every branch, and reproduce the released answer set for all but a few one- and two-hop questions, which are dropped. WebQSP uses the 1,628 test questions and the per-question 13 Freebase subgraphs released with RoG (2,826 training and 246 validation questions), which SubgraphRAG also uses; the topic entities are given, and the subgraphs keep no literals such as dates. CWQ uses RoG’s graphs too (Appendix G). 

Models and decoding. The open models run on Ollama 0.9.6, 2 which serves them through llama.cpp, 3 in its Q4_K_M quantisation, a 4-bit weight format, decoding greedily at temperature 0 with a fixed seed. Outputs are limited to 24, 64, 256 and 128 tokens on CLUTRR, PathQuestion, MetaQA, and WebQSP and CWQ (512 for the status prompt on PathQuestion); answers that reached their limit were generated again with 512 tokens (1,024 on MetaQA), which, up to the de-coding noise reported below, equals running every condition with the larger limit, as an answer that ended earlier stays the same, with the exceptions listed in the supplementary material; other runs used the larger limits from the start. The context windows are 4,096 tokens on CLUTRR, 8,192 on MetaQA and 16,384 on PathQuestion, WebQSP and CWQ. Gemma-3-27B runs with 8,192 on PathQuestion and WebQSP, as its runner failed with longer windows, so it could not read the two-hop neighbourhood on PathQuestion (cut at 600 facts) or the one-hop one on WebQSP, whose prompts reach 15 and 11 thousand tokens. Byte-identical prompts in two conditions differ in net accuracy by at most 0.5 points on CLUTRR, 1.2 on PathQuestion and 1.0 on MetaQA. In reasoning mode, Qwen3-32B runs with thinking enabled and DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-70B reasons inside think tags removed before the answer is parsed; both may write up to 4,096 tokens in a window of 8,192 (6,144 and 12,288 on MetaQA); at most 14.4 percent of a condition’s answers reached that limit, and the limit bounds the reasoning, as GPT-5’s does; Appendix M re-asks Qwen3-32B’s 271 capped full-set answers with 8,192 tokens. Tests and intervals. Paired tests are exact McNemar tests on exact match and Hit, Wilcoxon signed-rank or bootstrap tests on F1, and bootstrap or sign tests on scores, all Holm-adjusted over the LLMs or fact budgets compared. Constants and a new graph. No constant was set on test questions, though path counts became the default consistency check after an ablation on the test probes (Appendix J). The lexicon’s lift of 1.3 lies on a plateau: fitted on 80 percent of PathQuestion’s training questions, lifts of 1.1 and 1.3 pass 78 and 79 percent of the other 20 percent (at most 300 per subset), with 95.2 percent of the derived answers exact, and lifts of 1.5 and 2.0 only 61 percent, with 91.2. WebQSP’s ten neighbours and vote threshold of 0.6, CWQ’s settings (Appendix G) and the routing gates of both (Appendix L) were chosen on validation questions; the gates were then confirmed on 500 WebQSP and 1,501 CWQ training questions held out under a pre-specified plan, read by the seven open LLMs with the decoding above and 512 output tokens (Appendix N). The learned consistency check’s remaining settings (Appendix J), the P (none ) skip, the 99 percent single-value rule, the cap of 64 paths per fact and the size bins, are insensitive or set by hand (supplementary material), and the false-alarm rate of 1 percent is an operating point to choose, not a fitted value (sensitivity tables and the threshold sweep: the supplementary material). For a new graph: fit the mapping on training questions and set its lift or vote threshold on held-out ones; count relations along random walks of the graph, train TransE on its facts and read off its single-valued relations; calibrate on consistent contexts sampled from held-out parts of the graph, at the false-alarm rate one accepts; and decide on validation ques-tions whether the derived answer or the LLM answers where the checks pass, and whether the LLM answers or the pipeline abstains where they fail. No step needs a contradicted example from the new graph; for the default check, flagging a context by the smaller of its two shares and splitting the threshold between them detect the same reversed 14 facts built from training questions (Appendix J). 

Compact contexts. With a learned mapping the selector keeps every path realising the predicted relation sequence, so it may reach an answer by two paths. A path fact whose deletion leaves the sequence’s answers unchanged occurs in 6.4, 11.3 and 24.9 percent of the PathQuestion, WebQSP and MetaQA (lexicon) contexts where the sequence yields an answer (23.6 with MetaQA’s classifier), and facts off every path (other facts of the paths’ unnamed records, facts testing a constraint) in 36.8 percent of the WebQSP ones. CLUTRR’s contexts are minimal: deleting any fact loses the derived la-bel; 97 percent are simple paths, the rest pass a per-son twice where the rules compose no shorter walk. 

Precise relation knowledge. Table 4 measures, on questions not used for testing, how often the derived answer is exact where the derivability check passes; its third column names the question set. MetaQA’s classifier is measured with its own and the lexicon’s contexts, on both of which the check executes the classifier’s sequence, and WebQSP’s and CWQ’s contexts are built as the test ones. F WebQSP 

Relation knowledge from predicted chains. 

WebQSP annotates each training question with its inferential chain, the relations from the topic entity to the answer, and with constraints: equalities, dates relative to the present and orderings; a date named in a neighbour’s own question cannot be carried over. A test question, its topic mention removed, is embedded with MPNet (all-mpnet-base-v2), and its K most similar training questions each vote for their chain with their cosine similarity. Among the chains that can be followed from the topic entity in the question’s graph we take the one with most votes; where none can (97 test questions), the context is empty and the check fails. The context holds every path that realises the chain, the other facts of the unnamed nodes on those paths (Freebase’s records grouping the facts of an event, such as a marriage or a term of office), and the facts that test each constraint shared by at least half of the voters; the derived answer is the set of path ends satisfying them. The derivability check passes when the chain holds at least a share τ of the votes, every shared constraint can be tested on the context, and some path end satisfies all of them. On the 246 validation questions, K = 10 

was chosen among 5, 10 and 20 by the derived answer’s exact match, and τ = 0 .6 is the smallest threshold whose derived answer is exact for at least 80 percent of the questions that pass (85.0 percent, with 40.7 percent passing, on the questions that chose it). The graphs keep no dates, so the check fails where the vote carries a date or ordering constraint; it passes 54 of the 143 test questions annotated with one, whose vote lacks it.

by the same rule). The lexicon selector applies the PathQuestion lexicon, fitted on the same paths, to paths of one or two steps from the topic entity. Data and relation knowledge. CWQ extends WebQSP queries by composition, conjunction, a comparison or a superlative (Talmor and Berant, 2018). J The learned consistency check 

The idea in brief. A graph without rules still shows which relations hold together. In CLUTRR’s generated families, for instance, a person’s sister’s parent is that person’s parent, and two people joined by a sister fact are siblings, never parent and child. The learned check collects such regularities by walking the graph at random and counting, for each sequence of relations a walk follows, which relation the graph stores between the walk’s two ends. A fact of a context is then suspicious when the other facts of the context join its two entities by a path at whose ends the graph almost never stores the fact’s relation: a context in which m is the sister of s and s is the father of m is such a case (Figure 3). The counts come from the graph’s own facts and the threshold from contexts known to be consistent, so the check needs no examples of contradictions, which a new graph would not provide. Its limit is that it can judge a fact by path evidence only where such a second path exists; the single-value rule below needs none. Most graphs lack rules, but their facts show how relations constrain each other. The check’s three signals mirror three kinds of ontology constraint: relations that cannot hold together, chains of relations that imply another, and relations with a single value; others, such as type mismatches, escape them. Co-stored relations. Random walks through the graph, each labelled with the relation the graph stores between its ends or none, give for each relation path π, its labels read with their directions, counts of the relations stored between its ends (Figure 3a); add-one smoothing turns these into P (r | π). As with rules, a fact (s, p, o ) is com-pared with what the other facts imply: each path π

they form between s and o, not the fact, scores it by 

P (p | π, stored ) = P (p | π)/(1 − P (none | π)) ,the probability of the claimed relation with the mass of none removed, as graphs often omit relations that hold. In Figure 3b a false fact makes

Composed relations. Some contradictions join two entities with no stored relation: PathQuestion’s knowledge bases store no grandparents, so the counts skip a path that makes g a grandparent of c while a fact makes c a parent of g. TransE (Bordes et al., 2013) gives relations vectors with h + r ≈ t for every fact (h, r, t ), so a path implies the sum of +r

per step along a fact and −r per step against one. A fact conflicts when its relation’s vector is far from this sum for another path between its entities: from g to c, −2rparent against the claimed +rparent .

Single values. A relation with one object for at least 99 percent of its subjects in the graph, such as place of birth, is contradicted by a second object. Training. The counts come from 40,000 random walks of each length (60,000 on WebQSP), of up to 8 facts on CLUTRR and 4 on the knowledge bases (at most 3 to test shorter walks, as many in total), each labelled with a relation stored between its ends, or none, and read with directions (“father>” 24 along a stored fact, “father<” against it). The walks cover 200 families from CLUTRR’s generator with the benchmark’s settings (3 generations, 4 children per couple, everyone married), which store every related pair’s relation in both directions and are never test stories, the three PathQuestion knowledge bases and the first 1,200 WebQSP training graphs. TransE is trained, and single-valued relations counted, on the same graphs; only its relation vectors are kept. The count, vector and GRU scores take 0.20, 0.15 and 0.62 ms per WebQSP compact context (the classifier, the count tables and the timings: supplementary material). 

Scoring and calibration. Each fact is scored against up to 64 simple paths that the other facts form between its entities, of up to the training length in contexts of up to 20 facts and of at most 2 facts on CLUTRR and 1 on the knowledge bases in larger ones; its vector score is the largest distance between its relation vector and a path’s vector. The shares above are taken within each size bin (up to 20, 21 to 60, more than 60 facts); in the GRU addition the GRU’s P (p | π, stored ) gives a third share. One threshold on the smallest share keeps false alarms at 1 percent; 1 percent for each of the default’s two scores would allow up to 2. Cal-ibration contexts are held-out pipeline contexts or consistent subgraphs of held-out graphs (per bench-mark and bin: supplementary material). On them the default raises 0.0 to 1.2 percent false alarms per bin, below 1 where scores tie at the threshold and above where the single-value rule fires. No contradicted example is used to count, train or calibrate, but smoothed counts became the default after an ablation on the test probes (supplementary material), so that choice is post hoc.

K What a passing check guarantees 

Four layers on WebQSP. A passing check on WebQSP can be read as four claims, which we call layers and test on each test question: (1) interpreta-tion: the predicted chain and constraints are exactly those of an annotated parse of the question; (2) exe-cution: on the context’s facts alone they leave some answer, every constraint testable; (3) completeness: that answer set equals the one on the question’s whole graph (the question graph); (4) consistency: the default consistency check finds no conflict. The derivability check passes 743 of the 1,628 questions (45.6 percent). All satisfy layer 2, by construction, but only 589 (79.3 percent) layer 1. Layer 3 fails for 14 of the 34 passing contexts cut at 100 facts; the returned answer, executed on the whole graph, is unaffected. The consistency check flags 4 passing contexts (11 with the GRU check); the derived answer is exact for 3 of the 4 and 10 of the 11, and for 585 of all passing questions (78.7 percent). A pass means that the answer is derived under the predicted query, not that it is verified. 

The first layer that fails. The supplementary ma-terial assigns each question to its first failing layer; the counts are chain not annotated 419, constraint missed 144, other constraints 82, no execution 65, incomplete 8, conflict found 8, 1 to 4 hold, wrong 63 and all five hold 839, where the group ‘1 to 4 hold, wrong’ passes all four layers with a wrong answer and the last group with a right one. 

Wrong answers that pass. Of the 158 wrong derived answers among passing questions (one withheld by the consistency check), 55 lie on an unannotated chain and 103 on an annotated one: 69 supersets of the gold set, 63 of them with a constraint of the annotated parse that the neighbours’ vote did not apply, 38 of these a date, 27 ‘now’ or ordering constraint the graph cannot test; 21 subsets; 7 overlapping and 6 disjoint. 

Checking the context against the graph. Agraph-aware check would also run the predicted query on the whole graph and pass only where the context yields the same non-empty answer set. On depleted contexts it passes 0, 56 and 0 of the 777, 801 and 1,438 PathQuestion, MetaQA and WebQSP contexts where the derivability check passes 80, 404 and 419, and gating on it raises every LLM’s balanced score further (the supplementary material); on the selector’s own contexts it costs nothing. 

Right answers where no answer follows. On the 636 depleted WebQSP contexts from which no answer follows, the seven open LLMs under the abstention prompt abstain on 56.8 to 74.5 percent, answer wrongly on 11.6 to 34.0, rightly with every gold answer named in the context (so readable from facts off the predicted chain) on 6.0 to 15.3, and rightly from own knowledge (a gold answer not named) on 0.0 to 4.7; 21.5 to 57.5 percent of their answers are right. 

Where the derived answer is executed. On CLUTRR, PathQuestion and MetaQA the check and the derived answer are executed on the final context’s facts only; the selector built that context from the graph, so it holds every path of the predicted sequence, and completeness rests on the selector (Section 3). On WebQSP and CWQ the selector enumerates the predicted chain’s paths in the whole question graph, tests the constraints there and returns the path ends, while the context it writes is cut at 100 facts: the derived answer of Tables 3 and 11, of the layer tables (supplementary material) and of Appendix G is the graph execution. The sentence of Section 3 that the check reads only the context describes the check applied to a given context. Executed on the context only, the derived answer where the check passes falls from 78.7 to 78.1 percent on WebQSP and from 52.0 to 51.8 on CWQ; routing moves by −0.3 points for every open LLM and for GPT-5 on WebQSP and not at all on CWQ; the pass set is unchanged on WebQSP and would lose 2 CWQ questions. The graph execution is kept because the selector has already run the chain on the graph (no further cost) and be-cause the context cut is a presentation limit for the LLM, not a limit of the evidence the selector saw. 

Literal constraints and unreachable answers. 

RoG’s question graphs keep no literals, so a date, ‘now’, numeric, ordering or literal-match constraint of the gold query cannot be tested (classified by the gold SPARQL; on WebQSP any of a question’s parses). Such questions are 10.3 percent of WebQSP and 28.6 of CWQ; they pass the check 35.1 and 6.4 percent of the time, with 22.0 and 3.1 percent of the derived answers exact, and hold 46 of the 158 wrong passing answers on WebQSP (among them the 38 untestable constraints counted above) and 63 of 200 on CWQ, where no com-parative (7 passing) or superlative (6) question passes with a right answer. The graph reaches no gold answer for 71 WebQSP and 683 CWQ questions (4.4 and 19.3 percent). On questions it can test, reachable and free of literal constraints (86.1 and 55.7 percent), the check passes 48.5 and 17.9 percent, with 84.1 [81.2, 86.7] and 61.1 [55.9, 66.0] percent exact, against 78.7 and 52.0 on all: literals and unreachable answers explain a minority of the wrong passing answers (50 of 158 and 63 of 200); the rest (108 and 137) is mostly the method’s (chain, constraint, answer set), with other graph gaps such as missing or renamed answers. 

WebQSP’s routing gate. Executed on the final context only, the derived answer where the routing gate passes falls from 65.1 to 64.8 percent (where it fails from 6.2 to 5.8; on all questions from 56.3 to 56.0), the answer sets differ for 16 of the 1,385 passing questions, and routing moves by −0.3 points for every open LLM.

N Held-out confirmation under a pre-specified plan 

Design. The gate rule of Appendix L, the resulting routing gates and the precision gate, four primary hypotheses with their thresholds and tests, and the secondary analyses were fixed in a pre-specified plan, a timestamped commit to the code repository on 2026-10-07 (hash and plan file released with the code), before any held-out ques-tion was selected; the analysis script was frozen, its hash recorded, before any held-out read. The held-out questions come from the training splits and were never used for evaluation: 500 WebQSP ques-tions drawn at random and 1,501 CWQ questions of 168 whole seed clusters (the WebQSP question a CWQ question was composed from), as the CWQ test set is built; they and their near-duplicates were removed from the chain-voting pool, and the seven open LLMs read our context for each under the forced-choice and the abstention prompt. WebQSP questions are the units of every test; on CWQ seeds are (seed bootstrap, seed sign-flip test). 

Primary hypotheses. H1, the chosen routing gate’s precision passes the pre-set tolerance: WebQSP 66.0 percent (282 of 427) [61.4, 70.4] against 68.3 on validation, CWQ 69.0 (234 of 339) [59.5, 76.6] against 69.2; both at or above the validation Wilson lower end, the tolerance (61.6, 66.0), and above 50 percent (one-sided 

p = 1 .6 × 10 −11 ; seed-bootstrap 5th percentile 60.9): confirmed. H2, routing beats each LLM alone (Holm over seven): confirmed for the four smaller LLMs on both benchmarks (+6.6 to +8.6 and +4.7 to +9.3 points), not for the three largest (−0.6 to +1.4 and −0.8 to +0.1); the mean over the seven gains +4.7 [+2.1, +7.4] and +3.4 [+2.0, +4.8]. H3, the chosen routing gate against the check in mean routed exact match: WebQSP +0.3 [ −1.5, +2.1], not distinguished (per LLM −2.6 to +2.4, none significant), CWQ +2.6 [+1.4, +3.9], better; the gates are not changed whatever H3 shows. H4, the precision gate’s precision (the check and one derived answer) is above the pre-set 80 percent: WebQSP 89.8 (79 of 88; one-sided lower bound 82.8), CWQ 95.9 (70 of 73; seed-bootstrap 5th percentile 89.5): confirmed; 89.8 and 95.9 are point estimates on 88 and 73 returned answers. 

Secondary analyses. With the answerer chosen per open LLM on validation (Appendix L): chosen by accuracy, accuracy against the LLM alone changes by +6.6 to +8.6 points for the four smaller LLMs on WebQSP and +4.7 to +9.3 on CWQ and by −0.6 to +0.8 and +0.0 to +0.1 for the three larger; chosen by the equal-cost score, the score changes by +4.6 to +26.6 and +31.2 to +74.8 (significantly for 6 and 7 of the seven); no change on a policy’s own criterion is significantly negative. The other secondary analyses are in the supplementary material.

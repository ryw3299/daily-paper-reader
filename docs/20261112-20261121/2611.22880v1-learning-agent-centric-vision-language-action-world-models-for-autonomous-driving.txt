## Abstract 

Recent progress in integrating vision-language-action (VLA) models with world modeling has significantly advanced end-to-end autonomous driving by allowing systems to reason about imagined futures instead of merely reacting to current observations. However, most world VLA models generate future frames at the pixel level with plenty of dense visual tokens. Although such generated futures can be visually plausible, they preserve many driving-irrelevant appearance details, i.e., only a small portion of the tokens directly encode critical planning-relevant factors, such as agent geometry, motion dynamics, and interactions. This introduces a significant performance bottleneck. An important question thus arises: what world state should a world VLA model reason over to support planning beyond pixel-level prediction? We argue that planning-oriented world modeling should focus on a compact and structured agent-centric latent state that captures the interactions between objects in the driving scene over time. As such, we introduce 

AgentWorld , an agent-centric VLA world model that uses discrete agent tokens jointly for reasoning and trajectory planning. Each token is dynamically grounded to a surrounding agent associated with its object’s state, such as semantic identity, 3D geometry, motion, and interaction context. AgentWorld learns these tokens through three key processes: explicit geometric grounding, reasoning-oriented supervised fine-tuning, and agent-oriented reinforcement learning. Extensive open-loop and closed-loop experiments under diverse driving scenarios show that AgentWorld enhances planning capabilities, fully exploits visual evidence and agent dynamics, and yields interpretable agent-centric world states. 

## 1 Introduction 

The advancements in Vision-Language-Action (VLA) [1–10] and world models [11–17] have sig-nificantly propelled end-to-end autonomous driving [18–22]. VLA models inherit the instruction-following and reasoning capabilities of Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) [23–29], effectively connecting perception, reasoning, and action generation within a unified framework. World models complement this ability by predicting how the driving scene will evolve before an action is taken. Recent efforts aim to integrate these two approaches into VLA world models [30–34], enabling VLA systems to consider imagined futures to produce more reasonable actions. Despite this promising paradigm shown in Fig. 1, most prior VLA world models imagine the future frames through pixel-level generation with autoregressive or multi-step diffusion. Although such gen-erated future frames can be visually plausible, they retain numerous task-irrelevant visual details, i.e., texture, lighting, background, and visual continuity. This introduces a significant bottleneck in VLA world models. As illustrated in Fig. 2, existing VLA world models demand far more tokens than the fine-tuned base model, i.e., Qwen2-VL [35], yet bring only slight planning improvement. Moreover, the visual grounding ablation reveals that removing image cues only slightly degrades existing world VLA models, even with their heavy use of dense visual tokens. This suggests that the visual inputs in these models are not yet fully transformed into planning-relevant reasoning, with many of the visual tokens instead being used for image generation. As such, the core challenge is not simply how to generate visually plausible future frames, but how to integrate world modeling with VLA through a compact, structured, and task-aligned state. An important question thus arises: what world state should a VLA world model reason over to support planning beyond pixel-level prediction? 

Motivated by JEPA-style paradigms [36–38], which prioritize predictive abstraction over pixel reconstruction, we argue that planning-oriented world modeling should emphasize a latent world state that captures the temporal interactions among objects in driving scenes. Rather than focusing on reconstructing dense visual futures, this state abstracts the scene into decision-relevant entities and their evolving relationships. We implement this idea by breaking down the scene into temporally evolving agents, each defined by semantic identity, 3D geometry, motion state, and interaction context. This structured abstraction enables the policy to recognize relevant agents, predict their motion evolution, and reason about how their future behaviors will influence the ego’s trajectory. In this way, we align the latent world state with the causal structure of driving, shifting world modeling from future frame generation to structured reasoning about interacting agents. In this paper, we introduce AgentWorld, an agent-based world VLA model designed for fully autonomous driving, as illustrated in Fig. 1. AgentWorld integrates causal reasoning, predictive world modeling, and agent interactions into a cohesive framework. In AgentWorld, the driving environment is represented by a set of discrete agent tokens, labeled as <agent_i> . These tokens are utilized for both reasoning and trajectory planning. This structure creates a well-defined interface among perception, reasoning, and planning processes. To make these latent agents physically meaningful, AgentWorld anchors them to 4D geometric features and 2D visual cues, and updates their hidden states through the MLLM into contextual representations of semantic identity, 3D geometry, motion, and interaction. AgentWorld learns these tokens via explicit geometric grounding, reasoning-oriented supervised fine-tuning (SFT), and agent-oriented reinforcement learning (RL) with group relative policy optimization [39]. The resulting agent tokens deliver a compact, physically grounded, and 2interaction-aware representation of the world state, facilitating interpretable reasoning and planning regarding surrounding agents in driving scenarios. Extensive open-loop and closed-loop experiments demonstrate the effectiveness of AgentWorld across diverse driving scenarios. By reasoning over interpretable agent-centric latent states, AgentWorld improves planning performance and better leverages visual evidence. These results validate agent-centric world states as an effective intermediate representation for VLA-based autonomous driving. Our contributions are summarized as follows: • We reveal that the bottleneck of existing world VLA models stems from the fact that a considerably large number of visual tokens for future generation are irrelevant to planning, which motivates our design of compact, structured, planning-oriented latent world modeling. • We introduce an agent-centric latent state for planning-oriented world modeling, which represents the driving scene as temporally evolving agents grounded in 3D geometry, motion, semantics, and interaction context. • We propose AgentWorld , an agent-centric world VLA model that instantiates this state with discrete agent tokens. Experiments on open-loop and closed-loop benchmarks show consistently improved planning performance and more interpretable world states. 

## 2 Related Work 

2.1 Vision–Language–Action World Models 

VLA models [1, 2, 40–43, 32, 44–52] and world models [12, 11, 53–68] have become two important directions for embodied intelligence and autonomous driving. Early driving VLMs [69, 4, 70, 40, 71, 72] demonstrate that language improves instruction following, scene understanding, and explicit reasoning in complex scenarios. However, language is often used as an auxiliary interface for explanation or high-level guidance, rather than being directly coupled with action generation. Recent VLA methods [2, 1, 3] address this limitation by unifying multimodal perception, language reasoning, and trajectory prediction within an end-to-end policy. In parallel, driving world models aim to improve planning through predictive scene evolution. Early works [17, 12, 15, 11] generate future driving scenes conditioned on videos, actions, or text prompts, while subsequent studies [73, 74, 56, 62, 75, 76] extend this paradigm with multi-view generation, autoregressive spatiotemporal modeling, and 4D occupancy prediction. These methods suggest that autonomous driving can benefit from reasoning over possible future states instead of reacting only to current observations. Recently, VLA world models have emerged by integrating VLA reasoning with world-model imagination. Representative approaches [30, 31, 33, 77–82] introduce future frames, latent world states, dynamic tokens, or spatiotemporal reasoning modules into action inference. Despite their progress, most existing methods still leave a fundamental question underexplored: what world state should a VLA world reason over to support planning beyond pixel-level prediction? We argue that this question is central to driving, where decisions are governed by physically grounded multi-agent interactions. 

2.2 World State Representations for Autonomous Driving 

The above question is closely related to how world states are represented for planning. Dense representations such as BEV [71, 83] and occupancy [84–86] preserve 3D spatial structure and geometric details, but they remain low-level and often entangle traffic participants with background elements, making them inefficient interfaces for high-level policy reasoning. Compact scene-level latents [34, 30, 77, 87] provide more efficient abstractions for future imagination, yet they typically compress multiple agents and interactions into global tokens, limiting structural interpretability and agent-level attribution. Language-aligned world states [1, 45, 42] naturally interface with MLLM reasoning, but are weakly grounded in physical quantities such as 3D geometry, motion, and inter-agent dynamics. As a result, policies can still rely on ego-status extrapolation, language priors, or dataset-specific correlations instead of modeling the physical evolution of surrounding agents. Consequently, existing representations either retain excessive low-level details, obscure agent-level causal factors, or lack explicit physical grounding, motivating a compact, interpretable, and agent-centric world state for driving-oriented reasoning. 3Multi-view Camera

## 3 Method 

As illustrated in Fig. 3, AgentWorld builds an agent-centric VLA world model for end-to-end autonomous driving. Instead of treating future images or global latent features as the reasoning state, AgentWorld represents the driving scene with discrete agent tokens that are grounded in 4D geometry, 2D visual features, and multimodal context. These grounded tokens act as structured world states, exposing surrounding participants and their interactions to the policy for reasoning and planning. The method is organized into three components. Sec. 3.1 motivates agent-centric world states for VLA world models. Sec. 3.2 details how agent tokens are physically grounded through multimodal routed anchoring. Sec. 3.3 describes the policy learning with grounded agent tokens. 

3.1 Rethinking World States for VLA World Models 

Let Ht = {o1: t, g } denote the driving context at time t, where o1: t contains historical multi-view observations and ego-status, and g is the navigation goal. The goal of end-to-end autonomous driving is to predict a future ego trajectory τt:t+H . Following the VLA world model paradigm [30, 34, 77], planning can be viewed as decision-making augmented by predictive imagination: 

p(τt:t+H , ˆS | H t) = pw( ˆS | H t) pπ (τt:t+H | H t, ˆS). (1) Here, pw denotes a world model that constructs an imagined world state ˆS from the driving context, while pπ denotes a VLA that predicts the ego trajectory conditioned on both the current context and the imagined state. This abstraction reveals that the world state is not merely an auxiliary prediction target, but the information interface through which predictive imagination influences decision-making. Therefore, the effectiveness of a VLA world model depends critically on the form of world state S

over which the policy reasons. Pixel-level future observations provide an intuitive form of imagination, but require the policy to process many dense visual tokens, most of which are devoted to image generation rather than planning-relevant factors. As a result, agent geometry, motion dynamics, and interactions are only sparsely and implicitly encoded in pixels, leading to high computational costs and limited task alignment. Compact global latents [77] improve efficiency, but often entangle heterogeneous scene factors and obscure the agent-level causal structure needed for planning. This motivates an agent-centric world state. For driving, the planning-relevant future is largely shaped by the evolution of surrounding participants: their 3D geometry, motion states, and interactions with the ego vehicle. Rather than representing the world state as dense pixels, language-only descriptions, or a compact scene latent, AgentWorld organizes the scene around potential agents. Specifically, we introduce a fixed set of special agent tokens into the MLLM sequence,

3.3 Policy Learning with Agent Tokens 

Grounded agent tokens serve as the world-state interface for policy learning. AgentWorld exposes the learned slots through the <perception> field, 

<perception> ⟨agent _1⟩ · · · ⟨ agent _N ⟩</perception> ,

where the state of each participant is carried by the token hidden state rather than an explicit text description. The policy is trained to output a structured response y = ( ξ, a, τ ), consisting of a concise agent-grounded rationale <think> , a high-level driving action <action> , and future ego waypoints 

<answer> . This format forces planning to be expressed through addressable participants instead of dense perception text or image-level future prediction.

For example, the model may state that <agent_3> is crossing the ego path and therefore the ego vehicle should decelerate and keep lane. To bridge training and inference, the final SFT phase adopts the same MLLM-only input-output format used in RL, where the auxiliary grounding heads and routed anchoring modules are no longer required in inference. 

Agent-oriented RL. We further optimize AgentWorld with GRPO [39]. Starting from the reasoning-oriented SFT checkpoint, the model samples multiple structured responses for each scene, each containing a concise rationale, a high-level action, and future ego waypoints. These candidates are evaluated by a composite reward that measures format, perception, action, trajectory, and interaction context. The trajectory reward encourages smoothness and imitation of expert behavior following [30], while the interaction reward explicitly evaluates the ego rollout against surrounding agents and penalizes collisions, insufficient clearance, and short time-to-collision. This reward design makes the RL stage agent-oriented: improving the final trajectory requires the policy to account for the dynamic agents represented by the learned <agent_i> tokens. GRPO ranks multiple responses sampled from the same scene by their relative rewards, using the SFT model as a reference to stabilize updates. During RL, only the MLLM policy is updated. ## 4 Experiments 

4.1 Experimental Setups Datasets and metrics. We evaluate AgentWorld on nuScenes [92] and Bench2Drive [98], covering both open-loop planning and closed-loop driving. For nuScenes, we report standard trajectory L2 error and collision rate. For Bench2Drive, we follow the official protocol and report Driving Score, Success Rate, Efficiency, Comfortness when available. More details of metrics can be found in the appendix. 

Implementation details. AgentWorld is initialized from Qwen2-VL and Qwen2.5-VL series models [35, 25], with multi-view images as visual inputs and frozen VGGT-style features as offline 4D geometry features. We train the model in three stages: physical grounding SFT, reasoning-oriented SFT followed by agent-oriented RL. The SFT stages jointly supervise grounded agent tokens, high-level actions, reasoning traces, and future waypoints, while the RL stage optimizes sampled responses using format, perception, action, trajectory, and agent-interaction rewards. All experiments are implemented in PyTorch on 4 × 80 GB NVIDIA A800 GPUs. Detailed hyperparameters, prompts, reward designs, and training schedules are provided in the appendix.

## 5 Conclusion 

We presented AgentWorld , an agent-centric VLA world model for end-to-end autonomous driving. Instead of relying on visually plausible future imagination alone, AgentWorld represents the driving scene through discrete agent tokens that are dynamically grounded to surrounding participants. These tokens encode semantic identity, 3D geometry, motion, and interaction, providing a compact and planning-oriented latent world state beyond images. Through explicit geometric grounding, reasoning-oriented supervised fine-tuning, and agent-oriented reinforcement learning, AgentWorld learns to reason over agents and output future trajectories. Extensive open-loop and closed-loop experiments demonstrate that this agent-centric formulation improves planning performance, reduces shortcut reliance on ego-status extrapolation and language priors, and enables more interpretable world states. Despite these promising results, AgentWorld still relies on explicit geometric supervision to ground 

⟨agent _i⟩ tokens to physical participants, rather than allowing agent tokens to emerge purely from end-to-end planning supervision. Future work will explore more scalable self-supervised grounding with stronger visual-semantic binding, aiming to maintain consistent agent identities, physical states, and interaction semantics over time. We hope AgentWorld provides a step toward physically grounded and interaction-aware world modeling for autonomous driving.

A.3 Training Details 

Our training follows three stages: agent grounding SFT, reasoning-oriented SFT, and agent-oriented reinforcement learning. The offline VGGT features are pre-extracted. 

Stage 1: Physical grounding SFT. We first train the agent-token grounding interface for 12 epochs. The trainable modules include the MLLM backbone, the special <agent_i> token embeddings, the multimodal routed anchoring module, and the auxiliary agent head. We use AdamW with a cosine decay schedule and a warmup ratio of 0.03. The learning rate is set to 1.0 × 10 −4. The per-device batch size is 2 with 8 gradient accumulation steps, giving an effective batch size of 16 per device. Input images are processed with a maximum resolution of 524288 pixels. For multimodal routed anchoring, we use L = 3 routing layers by default. The grounding loss supervises category/existence, 3D bounding box, and future trajectory prediction after Hungarian matching. The number of agents is set to 20. 

Stage 2: Reasoning-oriented SFT. Starting from the Stage-1 checkpoint, we train the MLLM to use grounded agent tokens for reasoning and planning for 12 epochs. The multimodal routed anchoring module and auxiliary agent head are frozen. The trainable parameters are the MLLM policy parameters and the special agent-token embeddings. We use AdamW with a learning rate of 

5.0 × 10 −5 and a cosine decay schedule. The batch configuration follows Stage 1, with a per-device batch size of 2 and 8 gradient accumulation steps. Each sample is trained with the structured output format consisting of <perception> , <think> , <action> , and <answer> . To reduce mismatch with the RL stage, we progressively drop the training-time grounding scaffold during SFT: early batches use full grounding inputs, middle batches use partial dropout, and the final SFT phase uses the same MLLM-only format as RL. 

Stage 3: Agent-oriented RL. We further optimize the Stage-2 checkpoint with GRPO for 1 epoch. During RL, only the MLLM policy parameters are updated. The auxiliary agent head is kept 21 frozen and used only as a reward-side generator to decode top-K agent trajectories from the learned 

<agent_i> tokens; it receives no gradients. The routed anchoring modules are also frozen and are not optimized during RL. We use a learning rate of 1.0 × 10 −6 and a global batch size of 16. For each prompt, we sample 8 responses to estimate group-relative advantages. The KL coefficient to the SFT reference policy is set to 1.0 × 10 −2. The reward is a weighted sum of format reward, perception-slot reward, action reward, trajectory reward, and agent interaction reward, with all reward weights set to 1 unless otherwise specified. After grounding and SFT, the agent tokens have already been aligned with physical participants and learned as the planning interface of the MLLM. Therefore, in the RL stage, we no longer optimize the auxiliary grounding modules. Starting from the SFT checkpoint, GRPO updates only the MLLM policy, which samples multiple structured responses for each scene, including a rationale, a high-level action, and future waypoints. The auxiliary agent head is kept frozen only as a reward-side decoder. It decodes the hidden states of agent tokens into physical quantities, such as agent existence, 3D boxes, and short-horizon motions, which are then used to compute candidate-dependent interaction rewards for the sampled ego trajectories. This frozen decoder receives no gradient and is not part of the policy forward path. At inference time, it is completely removed, and AgentWorld performs planning with the MLLM and the learned agent-token interface alone.

First, we perform agent grounding by supervising the hidden state hit at each agent-token position with a lightweight head that predicts category/existence, 3D state, and future trajectory. A.4 Comparison with Different Latent VLA World Models 

Recent latent VLA world models reduce the cost of future imagination by moving from pixel-space generation to latent-space reasoning. For example, Latent-CoT-style driving models [81] replace verbose textual reasoning with latent world-model tokens, DriveWorld-VLA [33] unifies world modeling and planning in a shared latent space, and LaST-VLA [82] performs reasoning in a latent spatio-temporal space with geometric and dynamic alignment. These methods improve efficiency over explicit future-frame generation, but their world states remain largely image-, BEV-, occupancy-, or spatio-temporal-latent representations. Such latents can still preserve fine-grained scene factors that are weakly related to planning, including appearance, background layout, static context, dense occupancy patterns, and generic scene evolution, while leaving the agent-level causal variables behind driving decisions implicit. In contrast, AgentWorld changes the unit of world modeling from dense scene evolution to agent evolution. Rather than compressing the future into a holistic latent state, AgentWorld represents the driving world as a set of grounded and addressable <agent_i> tokens, where each token is supervised to correspond to a surrounding traffic participant or a no-object state and encodes semantic identity, 3D geometry, motion tendency, and interaction context. This 22 agent-centric world state filters out much of the planning-irrelevant nuisance information retained by dense latent world models and exposes the physical entities and interactions that directly govern ego planning.

A.6 The Theoretical Motivation of AgentWorld 

We provide a theoretical perspective on why agent-centric latent states are more suitable than pixel-level future frames for planning-oriented VLA world models. Let Ht denote the driving context and let τ be a future ego trajectory. A pixel-level world model predicts future visual observations 

ˆIt+1: t+H , whereas AgentWorld predicts an agent-centric latent state ˆAt+1: t+H . Each agent state encodes semantic identity, 3D geometry, motion state, and interaction context. We view future images as a composition of two parts: 

It+1: t+H = g(At+1: t+H , Ut+1: t+H ), (30) where A denotes the physical and interactive evolution of traffic participants, and U denotes nuisance visual factors such as texture, illumination, background, and visual continuity. For planning, the cost of an ego trajectory is determined by the evolution of traffic agents rather than by all pixel-level details. We therefore assume that

Distribution of object counts. Fig. 10 motivates the choice of the maximum agent-token number N=20. The object-count distribution is highly imbalanced: most scenes contain a moderate number of objects, while only a small fraction of samples fall into the long-tail high-density regime. This suggests that using an excessively large number of agent tokens would introduce many unused or low-relevance slots in typical scenes, increasing the reasoning cost and no-object supervision burden without proportional benefit. Choosing N=20 provides a practical trade-off between coverage and efficiency. It covers the high-frequency region of the distribution, while the remaining crowded scenes can still be handled by the Top-K influential-agent selection strategy. Importantly, AgentWorld does not aim to exhaustively represent every annotated object; instead, it prioritizes traffic participants that are most relevant to planning, such as nearby vehicles, cut-in agents, pedestrians, and agents whose future motion may conflict with the ego trajectory. Therefore, N=20 is sufficient to preserve the key planning-relevant agents in most scenarios while keeping the agent-token world state compact. This design also supports the motivation of AgentWorld: world modeling for driving should allocate capacity to decision-relevant agents rather than dense scene reconstruction or all visible objects. The long-tail distribution further justifies using fixed agent slots with no-object handling and influential-agent selection, rather than scaling the token count with the total number of objects. 

Qualitative trajectory visualization. Fig. 11 compares the predicted trajectories of VLA-World and AgentWorld against GT across representative nuScenes validation scenes. The qualitative results show that AgentWorld produces trajectories that more closely follow the ground-truth driving intent, especially in scenes requiring subtle curvature, lane following, or cautious adjustment around surrounding traffic participants. In contrast, VLA-World shows larger deviations in several cases. This suggests that visual future imagination alone may not provide a sufficiently structured interface for planning: although future frames can be visually plausible, the policy may still fail to extract the physical and interaction cues needed to align with the expert trajectory. AgentWorld alleviates this issue by grounding the scene into agent-level tokens, allowing the policy to reason over surrounding participants directly rather than relying on dense visual features or ego-motion shortcuts. These visualizations support the quantitative findings: AgentWorld improves planning not merely by adding more information, but by organizing scene information into a more actionable world state. The closer 26 alignment between predicted and ground-truth trajectories indicates that agent-centric representations provide a stronger interface for translating perception and prediction into driving actions. 

A.8 Broader Impacts 

AgentWorld aims to improve autonomous driving systems by representing dynamic scenes through compact, agent-level world states. By explicitly grounding traffic participants in 3D geometry, motion, and interaction context, the proposed framework has the potential to improve the transparency and reliability of planning-oriented reasoning, especially in complex multi-agent driving scenarios. Such representations may also facilitate diagnostic analysis, since the learned agent tokens provide an interpretable interface for examining which surrounding agents influence the ego vehicle’s decisions. At the same time, autonomous driving is a safety-critical domain, and improvements in open-loop or closed-loop benchmarks should not be interpreted as sufficient evidence for real-world deployment. Failure cases may arise from imperfect perception, long-tail traffic behaviors, distribution shifts, sensor degradation, or ambiguous human intentions. In addition, agent-centric abstraction may underrepresent rare objects, vulnerable road users, or context factors that are not well captured by the predefined agent tokens. Therefore, AgentWorld should be used as a research step toward more structured and interpretable world modeling, rather than as a standalone deployable driving system. Practical deployment would require rigorous validation under diverse geographic, environmental, and social conditions, as well as careful integration with redundancy, uncertainty estimation, rule compliance, and human oversight mechanisms. 27 NeurIPS Paper Checklist

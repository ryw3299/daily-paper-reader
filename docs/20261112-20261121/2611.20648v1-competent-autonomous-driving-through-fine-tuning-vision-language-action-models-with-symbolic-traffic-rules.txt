# Competent Autonomous Driving Through Fine-Tuning Vision–Language–Action Models with Symbolic Traffic Rules 

Barkin Dagda 1, Vinal Asodia 1, Iman Sharifi 2, Peng Wei 2, Saber Fallah 1

Abstract —Vision-language-action (VLA) models offer a unified approach to perception, reasoning, and control for autonomous driving, but accurate action prediction alone does not establish compliance with traffic regulations. This paper presents a rule-guided VLA framework that incorporates symbolic traffic-rule feedback into policy optimisation. The framework combines supervised fine-tuning on expert demonstrations with reinforce-ment learning (RL) refinement using a reward that encourages encoded-rule compliance while penalising deviations from expert actions. Selected provisions of the United Kingdom Highway Code are formalised as executable first-order logic rules using PyReason, covering speed adaptation, following distance, and road-user priority. A frozen vision-language model extracts scene predicates, which are combined with vehicle-state information to assess the projected consequences of candidate control actions. Offline evaluation on held-out data collected in the CARLA simulator compares three group-based RL methods. The results show improvements in both action prediction accuracy and encoded-rule compliance over the supervised baseline. The best-performing variant increases the overall rule pass rate from 92.9% to 97.6%, with zero observed violations of the encoded road-user-priority check in the evaluated samples. These findings support symbolic rule feedback as a means of improving VLA policy alignment with explicit traffic-rule criteria. 

Index Terms —Vision-language-action models, reinforcement learning, autonomous driving, first-order logic. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

# Autonomous driving requires intelligent systems capable of perceiving complex environments, reasoning about dy-namic traffic situations, and executing safe driving manoeuvres that comply with traffic regulations [1]. End-to-end learning addresses these interconnected tasks by mapping sensor obser-vations to driving actions within a unified differentiable model. By jointly optimising perception and control, such approaches simplify system design and learn representations of complex driving behaviour [2]. However, ensuring that learned policies comply with explicit traffic rules remains a challenge. Large language models (LLMs) and vision-language models (VLMs) have expanded the role of semantic scene understand-ing and language-based reasoning in autonomous driving [3], [4]. Frameworks such as DriveLM, LMDrive, and DriveGPT4 explore connections between scene understanding, reasoning, planning, and vehicle control [5]–[7]. Vision-language-action (VLA) models further integrate multimodal observations, lan-guage, and action generation within a shared framework.

Related models and benchmarks investigate the capabilities and limitations of these approaches in scene understanding, visual grounding, and driving decision-making [8]–[10], [12]– [16]. Their development extends beyond imitation learning: AutoVLA employs reinforcement learning (RL) to refine plan-ning and reasoning efficiency [11], while AutoDrive-R 2 uses RL to improve reasoning and self-reflection [17]. Nevertheless, imitation objectives measure agreement with demonstrations, and RL objectives depend on the chosen rewards; neither inherently provides an explicit assessment of compliance with codified traffic rules. Symbolic representations offer a means of making these requirements explicit. Traffic rules describe context-dependent constraints on driving behaviour, which can be encoded as logical conditions and evaluated against scene and vehicle-state information. Previous research has integrated symbolic knowledge into learning-based autonomous driving to support safety [18], interpretability [19], and policy alignment [20]. SafeAuto [21], for example, represents traffic regulations using Markov logic networks to assess whether generated behaviours satisfy specified constraints. These developments motivate connecting symbolic rule evaluation with VLA policy refine-ment. The challenge addressed here is to translate contextual traffic-rule checks into action-dependent feedback that directly guides continuous-control policy optimisation. This paper proposes a rule-guided VLA framework that integrates symbolic traffic-rule evaluation into a two-stage learning procedure. Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) first adapts a pretrained VLM using expert driving actions and reference reasoning annotations. Reinforcement learning fine-tuning (RLFT) then refines the policy using a reward that combines rule-compliance feedback with a penalty for deviation from ex-pert actions. Selected provisions of the United Kingdom (UK) Highway Code [23] are formalised as executable first-order logic (FOL) rules using the PyReason symbolic reasoning engine [22]. A separate frozen VLM extracts scene predicates, which are combined with vehicle-state information to evaluate the projected consequences of candidate actions. The resulting checks cover speed adaptation, following distance, and road-user priority, providing explicit learning signals for compliance with the encoded criteria. We investigate this framework using three group-based opti-misation methods: group relative policy optimisation (GRPO) [24], [25], group reward-decoupled normalisation policy opti-misation (GDPO) [26], and GRPO Done Right (Dr. GRPO) [27]. Offline experiments on held-out simulated driving data compare action prediction accuracy and encoded-rule compli-ance, while ablations examine the contribution of individual rules to policy refinement. B. VLA Policy Architecture 

The driving policy is built on the Qwen3-VL-2B-Instruct backbone. The visual observation St is provided as an image, while the ego-vehicle state and telemetry xt are converted into textual descriptions and combined with the navigation instruction L to form the accompanying prompt. These in-puts are encoded as visual and text tokens within a shared multimodal sequence. A learnable action token, denoted as 

[ACT_TOKEN] , is added to the vocabulary and appended to the input sequence to provide a dedicated representation for continuous action prediction. The multimodal sequence is processed by the transformer backbone, which integrates the visual, vehicle-state, and nav-igation information through self-attention. The resulting con-textualised hidden states support both action prediction and language generation. In particular, the hidden state associated with [ACT_TOKEN] represents the driving context used by the action head. Two output heads generate the vehicle-control action and the natural-language reasoning output. The action head is a multilayer perceptron with Gaussian error linear unit (GELU) activations that maps the action-token hidden state to at =(at, throttle , a t, steering ) ∈ [0 , 1] × [−1, 1] . A sigmoid activation bounds the throttle output to [0 , 1] , while a tanh activation bounds the steering output to [−1, 1] . The language-modeling head autoregressively generates the reasoning output yt from the corresponding language-token hidden states. These two outputs are supervised using expert control actions and ref-erence reasoning annotations, respectively, as described next.

The symbolic evaluator provides the rule-compliance feed-back used by the learning framework described in Section II. It operationalises selected UK Highway Code provisions covering speed adaptation, following distance, and road-user priority. A. Symbolic Scene Representation 

The information required for symbolic evaluation is iden-tified through manual analysis of the selected Highway Code provisions. This analysis defines a predicate vocabulary de-scribing road geometry, environmental conditions, nearby road users, and vehicle interactions. Qualitative scene properties are represented by these predicates, while numerical quantities required for action evaluation, such as ego speed and following distance, are supplied separately through the vehicle-state information xt.A frozen Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct model extracts the pred-icates from the visual observation: zt = fϕ(St), where fϕ is the fixed visual-to-symbolic mapping and zt contains the rule-relevant predicates for the observed scene. Structured prompts specify the meanings of the predicates and consistency re-quirements, such as mutually exclusive road-surface labels. The model’s output is parsed into a dictionary of Boolean predicates that serves as input to the symbolic reasoning engine. PyReason applies logical inference rules to these predicates to derive the contextual factors used by the compliance checks. C. Candidate-Action Evaluation 

To assess the consequences of a candidate action without executing it in the simulator during fine-tuning, the evalu-ator projects the ego-vehicle state over a short horizon ∆t

using a kinematic bicycle model [33]. For candidate at,i , this projection is represented as ˆxt+1 ,i = F∆t (xt, at,i ) , where 

F∆t denotes the state-projection model and ˆxt+1 ,i is the predicted ego-vehicle state at the end of the horizon. The model provides a computationally inexpensive approximation of vehicle motion, while omitting higher-order dynamics such as tyre slip. The projected state is evaluated together with the symbolic predicates zt and the relevant vehicle-state information. Can-didates sampled for the same observation share the extracted scene predicates, while their projected states reflect their different control actions. PyReason uses the scene predicates and inferred contextual factors to identify the applicable rules and their parameters. The candidate’s projected motion is then assessed against the corresponding criteria defined in the preceding subsection. Rules whose activation conditions are absent remain inactive and are excluded from the rule-compliance reward. For each candidate, this procedure produces a compliance vector qt,i through the evaluator Q introduced in Section II. Its entries are mapped to the satisfied, violated, or inac-tive scores defined in Equation 4, which supply the rule-compliance reward in Equation 5. The same checks support the offline assessment of predicted actions. Their outputs indicate satisfaction of the encoded criteria under the supplied scene in-formation and the state-projection assumptions. The following section describes the data collection, training configuration, and evaluation protocol used to study the framework. IV. EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

A. Data Collection 

To train and evaluate a VLA model with traffic-rule compli-ance, scene attributes serve as key inputs for the symbolic rule engine. The CARLA simulation environment [30] provides ground-truth scene attributes, including road conditions, road geometry, the presence of nearby vehicles, and the proximity of vulnerable road users. To collect the expert driving data, an automated rule-based agent navigates across multiple CARLA towns, covering residential, urban, highway, and multi-lane environments under clear and rainy weather conditions, along with dedicated pedestrian-crossing scenarios. The agent op-erates with a two-level planning architecture: at the global level, it calculates optimal routes across the road network to generate target waypoints and high-level navigation commands (e.g., LANEFOLLOW , TURNLEFT , TURNRIGHT ). At the local level, it tracks these waypoints and manages speed using proportional-integral-derivative controllers [31] while reacting to red lights and leading obstacles. At each time step, the system records synchronized data, including a front-facing RGB image, vehicle kinematic states (speed, heading, and acceleration), distance to leading vehicles, the high-level nav-igation commands, the executed steering, throttle, and braking actions. To extract scene attributes directly from raw perceptual data for the symbolic rule engine, each collected front-facing RGB image is processed using a frozen pre-trained Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct model. The model is guided by structured prompt instructions with explicit semantic definitions and logical consistency constraints, such as ensuring road surface conditions remain mutually exclusive and asserting pedestrian presence whenever crossing behavior is identified. Each visual frame is parsed into a verified dictionary of boolean predicates representing environmental visibility, road geometry, obstacle categories, and vulnerable road users. > •

Analysis: Explains the driving situation by considering the planned route and safety conditions. Reaction: Explains what the vehicle should do next. It describes whether the vehicle should accelerate, maintain its motion, coast, or brake. To avoid having too many similar frames when the vehicle is stopped for a long time, a temporal filter removes repeated stationary frames. Important frames, such as the start of braking and the beginning of movement, are kept. Finally, the generated reasoning text is combined with the vehicle-state prompt, the input image, and the continuous driving actions. Together, these form the final multi-modal training samples. 

B. Training & Testing 

The Qwen3-VL-2B-Instruct backbone is fine-tuned using LoRA adapters applied to the attention projection layers (q_proj , k_proj , v_proj , and o_proj ), with a rank of 16 , a scaling factor of 32 , and a dropout rate of 0.05 .SFT is conducted on a dataset of approximately 20,000 CARLA images partitioned into an 80/20 train-validation split. Training is performed over four epochs with a batch size of 16 and a maximum sequence length of 2048 tokens. Optimization is governed by the AdamW optimizer with a learning rate of 2 × 10 −5, utilizing a cosine learning-rate scheduler, gradient clipping, mixed-precision training, and gradient check-pointing to maximize memory efficiency. To jointly optimize the VLA model for both continuous vehicle control and interpretable driving reasoning, a multi-task ob-jective is formulated, consisting of an action prediction loss and a language reasoning loss. The action prediction loss is defined via MSE, which minimizes the discrepancy between the predicted control commands and the expert trajectories. Concurrently, the language reasoning loss trains the model to generate scene-grounded explanations through auto-regressive next-token prediction over the ground-truth captions. RLFT was initialized from the pretrained SFT checkpoint and op-timized using AdamW with a learning rate of 1 × 10 −6.For each training sample, four candidate action trajectories were generated to estimate relative advantages, and KL reg-ularization was applied to constrain policy drift from the supervised initialization in GRPO and GDPO. RL training was conducted for 15 epochs, a duration determined empirically by monitoring reward curves. All experiments are conducted on a machine equipped with NVIDIA RTX 5070 Ti GPU, with 16 GB VRAM, using the same effective batch size and mixed-precision configuration as SFT. The SFT-pretrained and RLFT VLA policies are evaluated with approximately 3,600 image test dataset. The test dataset includes selected driving scenarios to ensure that each of the three active Highway Code rules is triggered across a sufficient number of frames for statistically meaningful per-rule compliance estimation. The test set covers a diverse range of driving scenarios, including pedestrian crossings, rainy weather conditions, and highway driving. For each scenario, the VLA model is tasked with predicting vehicle actions over the next five time steps, thereby generating a future vehicle trajectory. V. RESULTS & DISCUSSION This section evaluates the proposed rule-compliant VLA method from both optimization and deployment perspectives. During RL, training dynamics, including reward convergence and rule-learning behaviour are analyzed. The resulting policy is then evaluated on unseen driving scenarios with respect to driving performance, learned driving behaviour, UK High-way Code compliance at both the aggregate and condition-specific levels, and the contribution of individual symbolic rules through ablation. 

A. Training Performance & Policy Convergence 

Figure 4 summarizes the optimization behaviour of the proposed approach throughout both SFT and RLFT. During the SFT stage, the training loss shown in Figure 4a decreases monotonically before converging to a stable plateau, indicat-ing that the model successfully learns the joint distribution of driving actions and their corresponding chain-of-thought reasoning from expert demonstrations. Dr. GRPO consistently achieves higher reward values compared to GRPO and GDPO. This improvement can be attributed to the removal of sequence-length and standard-deviation normalization terms, which reduces variance intro-duced by dynamic scaling during optimization. In addition to that, omitting the KL-divergence regularization term appears to yield a smoother reward trajectory, as it reduces competing constraints on policy updates, allowing for more consistent reward maximization over training. C. Rule Compliance Analysis 

The previous evaluations provide aggregate measures of rule compliance. They do not reveal how the learned policy behaves under individual traffic scenarios. To address this, a scenario-specific analysis is performed by evaluating each UK Highway Code rule under different driving conditions that activate its predicates. Figure 7 presents the corresponding pass rates for the SFT policy together with the improvements achieved by RLFT optimization methods. Several driving scenarios are already handled reliably by the SFT policy. For Rule 125, compliance reaches 100% under clear weather, wet roads, bending roads, and pedestrian-related conditions, while poor-visibility scenarios achieve 98.4% compliance. These results suggest that conventional speed regulation under common driving conditions is effectively learned through supervised imitation. However, rule compliance drops significantly under icy-road conditions. Although this scenario is governed by the same safe speed rule, the SFT policy achieves only 43.6% compliance. This reveals a fundamental limitation of VLA policies trained without explicit rule guidance. SFT policy ef-fectively learns to match expert demonstrations however those demonstrations themselves do not always comply with traffic rules. In contrast, the rule-guided RLFT methods eliminate these violations and improve the compliance by 56.4% points to achieve perfect performance. Considering the safe following distance rule, across all vehicle-following scenarios, the SFT policy achieves moderate compliance ranging from 75% to 87%, indicating that SFT policy captures nominal headway maintenance but remains inconsistent across different environ-mental conditions. Unlike the Safe Speed rule, no individual scenario reaches complete compliance after RLFT, and only modest improvements of approximately 2–4 percentage points are observed. The most significant behavioural deficiency of the SFT policy is observed for Rule H. Compliance is zero across all pedestrian- and child-related scenarios, demonstrat-ing a systematic inability to initiate the required precautionary deceleration when a must-give-way condition is triggered. RL substantially improves performance in all three scenarios, indi-cating that explicit symbolic supervision successfully teaches the policy to adapt its behaviour in the presence of vulner-able road users. While GRPO and GDPO recover compli-ance to approximately 86–90%, Dr. GRPO achieves complete compliance across every evaluated condition, suggesting that the optimized policy generalizes consistently across different must-give-way situations rather than correcting only individual cases.

VI. C ONCLUSION 

This paper presented a rule-guided VLA framework for autonomous driving that combines supervised learning from expert demonstrations with symbolic traffic-rule feedback dur-ing RL fine-tuning. Selected UK Highway Code provisions are formalised as executable FOL rules using PyReason to eval-uate the projected consequences of candidate control actions. The resulting reward encourages compliance with the encoded rules while penalising deviations from expert actions. Offline experiments on held-out CARLA data show that the three evaluated RL methods—GRPO, GDPO, and Dr. GRPO— improve both action prediction accuracy and encoded-rule compliance over the SFT baseline. Dr. GRPO achieves the highest overall rule pass rate, increasing it from 92.9% for the SFT baseline to 97.6%, with zero observed violations of the encoded road-user-priority check in the evaluated samples. These findings support explicit symbolic feedback as a com-plement to demonstration-based learning, allowing traffic-rule criteria to directly influence VLA policy refinement. Several limitations remain. The evaluation is conducted entirely with pre-collected multimodal CARLA data, and the degree to which these findings transfer to real-world driving conditions remains an open question. The symbolic rule set is also limited to three checks covering selected UK Highway Code provisions, and broader coverage of traffic regulations, as well as adaptation to other regulatory frameworks, warrants future investigation. Future work will focus on extending the symbolic rule set to cover a wider range of traffic scenarios, exploring closed-loop evaluation in CARLA to assess the impact of rule-guided actions on longer driving trajectories, and investigating the transferability of the framework to real-world driving datasets and regulatory frameworks beyond the UK Highway Code.

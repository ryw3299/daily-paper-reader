Abstract 

Graph retrieval-augmented generation (GraphRAG) uses document-derived knowl-edge graphs to assemble evidence for large language model (LLM) answers. Its security depends on both the seed chunks retrieved for a query and the graph evidence assembled from them, yet attacks on this evidence path remain underexplored. We introduce GraphRAG-LTM ,a Layered Threat Modeling framework that tests three nested levels of attacker access. BB Query uses ranking feedback to make query edits under a semantic-similarity constraint and demote relevant seed chunks. WB Query uses retriever gradients for the same objective. Graph-aware WB keeps the white-box adversarial query and deletes bridge triplets from the query-specific evidence graph, disrupting multi-hop evidence propagation through localized graph edits. Across datasets, retrievers, and LLM backbones, rank feedback alone reduces answer quality, gradient access strengthens query attacks, and bridge-triplet deletion further impairs supporting-fact retrieval. Further analyses show how seed retrieval and graph evidence jointly contribute to these failures.

In these pipelines, re-trieved seed chunks determine which pre-extracted triplets enter a query-specific evidence graph and, ultimately, which evidence reaches the generator. We introduce GraphRAG-LTM to instantiate these access levels with corresponding attacks. At the first two levels, a ranking-guided query attack uses semantically constrained substitutions to demote originally retrieved seed chunks, relying on rank feedback or retriever gradients according to the available access. At the third level, the attack reuses the white-box adversarial query and deletes bridge triplets in the query-specific evidence graph. This matched design measures the added effect of graph edits beyond query perturbation. Our eval-uation across multiple datasets, retrievers, LLM backbones, and graph-retrieval mechanisms reveals a layered failure pattern: rank feedback alone can degrade answer generation, retriever gradients strengthen query attacks, and bridge-triplet dele-tion further impairs supporting-fact retrieval. Our contributions are summarized as follows: 

❶ Layered Threat Modeling for GraphRAG. 

The contrasting effects of several existing at-tacks in Flat RAG and GraphRAG motivate a threat model centered on GraphRAG’s evidence pipeline. We formulate three nested access lev-els and evaluate a corresponding attack at each level to distinguish what rank feedback, retriever gradients, and graph-triplet access each enable. 

❷ Ranking-guided Query Attack on Graph-construction Anchors. Seed chunks anchor the query-specific evidence graph and downstream context selection. We develop a ranking-guided query attack that demotes them through semanti-cally constrained edits. It uses ranking feedback in the black-box setting and retriever gradients in the white-box setting. 

❸ Graph-aware Extension via Bridge Triplets. 

To examine what graph access adds to the query attack, we delete bridge triplets while keeping the white-box adversarial query fixed across the paired conditions. This comparison isolates the additional effect of graph edits on evidence re-trieval, which we further examine under two alternative graph-retrieval mechanisms that dif-fer in how they select and organize evidence. 

2 Related Work 

LLM safety. Hallucination and factuality re-main central LLM reliability concerns (Huang et al., 2025; Yehuda et al., 2024; Kryscinski et al., 2020), motivating the use of retrieved evidence to ground generation. Other work studies misuse and failures across the model lifecycle (Wang et al., 2025; McGuffie and Newhouse, 2020; Ma et al., 2023). Guardrails, rule-guided feedback, and self-correction address model behavior (Rebedea et al., 2023; Mu et al., 2024; Gou et al., 2023); retrieval-based systems also depend on the reliability of external evidence supplied at inference time. 

Retrieval-augmented generation. RAG augments LLMs with retrieved documents to ground answers and access external knowledge across a broad range of tasks (Lewis et al., 2020; Guu et al., 2020; Izac-ard et al., 2023). Noisy or misranked passages can displace relevant evidence and harm genera-tion (Karpukhin et al., 2020; Izacard et al., 2021; Cuconasu et al., 2024). Query refinement, ranking-feedback rewriting, and active retrieval aim to im-prove evidence selection (Chan et al., 2024; Mao et al., 2024; Jiang et al., 2023). GraphRAG systems organize text and graph evidence for multi-hop reasoning or query-focused summarization (Edge et al., 2024; Jin et al., 2024; Mavromatis and Karypis, 2024; Zhu et al., 2025), using different mechanisms to select and compose evidence.

3 Problem Settings and Threat Models 

3.1 GraphRAG Pipeline 

We study GraphRAG pipelines that organize re-trieved evidence with document-derived knowl-edge triplets. Given text chunks D = {ci}ni=1 , the system extracts triplets (h, r, t ) from each chunk and forms a knowledge graph T , where triplets re-main linked to their source chunks. For a query Q,a semantic retriever returns the top-K seed chunks from the document collection, SQ = πK (Q; D),where πK (·) denotes the retriever ranking. The system then constructs a seed subgraph by collecting triplets associated with SQ and expands it through graph traversal or reranking to obtain a query-specific evidence graph GQ. A context organizer selects the final context CQ from the re-trieved chunks and graph-expanded evidence, and the generator M produces A = M(Q, C Q). The attack objective is to degrade the final answer qual-ity, measured by the discrepancy between A and the gold answer. Unlike Flat RAG, the seed chunks in GraphRAG not only provide initial textual evi-dence but also determine the downstream evidence graph and final context. Therefore, studying attacks along the query-to-graph pathway is critical for un-derstanding the robustness of GraphRAG systems. 

3.2 Layered Threat Models 

We consider three progressively stronger attacker access levels at different stages of the GraphRAG pipeline, as illustrated in Figure 2. 

Black-box Ranking-feedback Query Attack. 

The attacker observes retrieved rankings or top-

K results and perturbs the seed retrieval an-chor without accessing retriever internals or graph triplets. This setting models an external user of a GraphRAG service that exposes ranked source passages or retrieval results. 3White-box Gradient-guided Query Attack. 

The attacker uses retriever gradients to guide query edits but cannot inspect or modify graph triplets. This setting applies when retriever parameters are available to the attacker, as in an open-source sys-tem or a compromised retrieval service. 

Graph-aware White-box Propagation Attack. 

Building on WB Query, the attacker can inspect and cut bridge triplets in the query-specific evi-dence graph. This setting models an insider or compromised graph-processing component with access to per-query graph artifacts, without control of the full corpus or global graph. 

4 The Proposed GraphRAG-LTM 

4.1 Overview 

GraphRAG-LTM demotes originally relevant seed chunks through constrained query edits, so ranking shifts can propagate into graph expansion and final context selection. Its BB Query and WB Query 

variants share a retrieval-anchor objective but use rank feedback and retriever gradients, respectively; 

Graph-aware WB adds bridge-triplet perturbation to the same white-box query. Figure 3 shows where these interventions enter the GraphRAG pipeline.

5.1 Experiment Setup Datasets and Metrics. Our main evaluation uses 7,405 questions from HotpotQA-Distractor (Yang et al., 2018), 2,417 from MuSiQue (Trivedi et al., 2022), and a fixed 1,000-question development subset from 2WikiMultihopQA (Ho et al., 2020), with graphs built from document text for all three datasets. We report Answer F1 and Supporting-fact F1 as primary metrics; dataset preprocessing and evaluation details appear in Appendix B. 

GraphRAG Setup. For the main experiments, we instantiate GraphRAG with KG2RAG (Zhu et al., 2025). This pipeline extracts document-derived triplets, retrieves the top-K=10 seed chunks, expands evidence through the graph, and selects the top 10 context chunks for generation. We evaluate three retrievers: Contriever (Izacard et al., 2021), BGE-large-en-v1.5 (Xiao et al., 2024), and mxbai-embed-large. The main tables use two separate triplet-extraction and answer-generation settings, LLaMA3-8B (Grattafiori et al., 2024) and Qwen3-8B-Base (Yang et al., 2025); other ablation studies and analyses use LLaMA3-8B unless oth-erwise stated. Both model settings use the same extraction and generation prompt templates, re-produced in Appendix C. Further implementation details and retrieval settings are provided in Ap-pendix B. 

Attacks and Baselines. All attacks are applied at inference time. For all main-result tables, we set the query-attack iteration budget to Tmax =5 under semantic similarity threshold θsim =0 .8. For graph-aware evaluation, Bridge Edge Cut removes α=5% 

of triplets from the query-specific evidence graph. We compare with Clean, PoisonedRAG (Zou et al., 2024), HotFlip corpus poisoning (Ebrahimi et al., 2018; Zhong et al., 2023), Indirect Prompt Injec-tion (Greshake et al., 2023), PromptInject (Perez and Ribeiro, 2022), Universal Adversarial Trig-gers (Wallace et al., 2019), and Random KG Dele-tion. Full attack and baseline details are in Ap-pendix B.

We use these results to examine how attacker access affects the generated answer and ev-idence retrieved by GraphRAG under increasingly permissive threat models. Our attacks produce larger and more consistent degradation than the existing baselines. Even BB Query , which uses only ranking feedback, yields lower Answer F1 than most of the six attack base-lines in every evaluated dataset–retriever–generator setting. WB Query and Graph-aware WB further reduce the overall average Answer F1, with Graph-aware WB achieving the lowest average under both generators. Several baselines substantially affect answers while changing supporting-fact retrieval only slightly; in contrast, our attacks reduce both Answer F1 and Retrieval F1. The concurrent de-clines provide initial evidence that seed-ranking perturbations may affect downstream evidence se-lection for subsequent answer generation. The three tiers expose successive points of vulnerability: ranking feed-back guides query edits, gradients sharpen them, and graph access disrupts evidence propagation through the query-specific evidence graph. These patterns extend across the three datasets, all three retrievers, and both generator models. The magnitude of the decline varies by setting, but ranking feedback alone is sufficient to weaken GraphRAG, while access to retriever gradients and graph structure increases the overall impact. The observed vulnerability is therefore not tied to any single dataset, retriever, or generator model.

6 Conclusion 

We examined GraphRAG’s evidence path through three nested attacker access levels. Across three multi-hop QA datasets, three retrievers, and two extraction-and-generation model settings, ranking feedback alone was sufficient to degrade answers, while retriever gradients strengthened the query at-tack. When the adversarial query was held fixed, bridge-triplet cuts consistently reduced supporting-fact retrieval; the component ablation suggests that these cuts weaken evidence recovery after seed rankings shift. The attacks also remained effective under PPR and community-based evidence selec-tion on the same extracted graph. These findings identify seed retrieval and graph evidence propaga-tion as connected vulnerabilities in GraphRAG. 87 Limitations 

Our findings are bounded by the three attacker ac-cess levels and GraphRAG configurations evalu-ated here. They suggest two directions for im-proving robustness. At seed retrieval, independent retrieval signals and seed-evidence checks could reduce reliance on a single ranking before graph expansion. At graph processing, verifying triplet provenance and retaining alternative evidence paths could help detect missing bridge relations and re-cover supporting evidence. Evaluating these di-rections against adaptive attacks and across inde-pendently constructed graphs, languages, and tasks would clarify their generality. 

8 Ethical Considerations 

This work investigates adversarial vulnerabilities in GraphRAG under three levels of attacker access. We acknowledge that security research is dual-use. Our experiments use public benchmarks to study failures in seed retrieval and graph-based evidence selection; they do not target deployed products or collect user data. The main experiments use HotpotQA, MuSiQue, and 2WikiMultihopQA and involve no human participants. Language models are used for knowledge-triplet extraction and answer genera-tion. We reuse precomputed graphs and batch in-ference where possible to reduce repeated compu-tation. The paper and appendix describe the eval-uated models and datasets, attacker permissions, intervention locations, attack settings, and evalua-tion procedures so that the reported results can be examined in context. During manuscript preparation, generative AI tools assisted with language editing, sentence-level rewriting, and organization. All scientific claims, experimental results, numerical values, method-ological descriptions, and citations were checked and approved by the authors. The authors remain responsible for the accuracy, interpretation, and presentation of the paper.

question development subset converted to the same paragraph-and-sentence schema. Across datasets, triplets (h, r, t ) are extracted from document text and linked to source chunks; gold evidence and answers are excluded from graph construction. 

Retrieval and models. KG2RAG retrieves 

K = 10 seed chunks, expands their linked triplets, and reranks candidate chunks with 

bge-reranker-large ; the final context has at most 10 chunks. At this fixed K, the exponen-tial importance weights in Eq. 2 do not overflow; a larger-K implementation can evaluate the same normalized weights with rescaling or in log space. We evaluate Contriever, BGE-large-en-v1.5, and mxbai-embed-large. The two main-table model set-tings use LLaMA3-8B or Qwen3-8B-Base for both triplet extraction and answer generation. Other analyses use LLaMA3-8B unless stated otherwise. Appendix C reproduces the prompts. 

Attack settings and baselines. The main query-attack budget is Tmax = 5 iterations. Accepted sub-stitutions must reduce the ranking loss and satisfy cosine similarity ≥ 0.8 under all-MiniLM-L6-v2. BB Query uses observed rankings and WordNet candidate substitutions; WB Query uses retriever gradients over a top-(K + M ) candidate pool with 

M = 50 . Graph-aware WB deletes 5% of triplets in the query-specific evidence graph, prioritizing bridges; Random KG Deletion uses the same dele-tion budget. HotFlip is a corpus-poisoning baseline: it optimizes one injected passage per question with at most 20 token-replacement steps and 32 candi-dates per step, capped at 96 tokens. It does not edit the query. The remaining baseline implementations follow their cited attacks. 

Evaluation. Answer F1 uses normalized token overlap with the gold answer; yes , no , and noan-swer require exact matching. Supporting-fact F1 uses exact matching of (title, sentence-index) pairs. We report means of question-level F1 values, so F1 need not equal the harmonic mean of separately av-eraged Precision and Recall. For paired uncertainty analyses, bootstrap resampling uses questions as the unit.

# ABSTRACT 

Autonomous driving decision systems must balance safety, efficiency, and social norms in complex traffic interactions. Philosophical and ethical considerations have received limited attention in existing autonomous driving decision-making approaches based on numerical optimization, sequence prediction, and large lan-guage models (LLMs). We propose Chinese Philosophical Wisdom-Guided Driv-ing (CPW-Drive), a closed-loop retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) frame-work that incorporates value guidance derived from Chinese philosophy into autonomous driving decision-making. Using Chinese Confucian thought as its knowledge source, CPW-Drive consolidates LLM-extracted keywords from rele-vant classical texts into driving-relevant value principles through manual screen-ing and validation. It then contextualizes these principles through scenario-specific cases to form retrievable and reusable value guidance. We further pro-pose Physics-aware Spatial Similarity Retrieval (PSSR), which compares vehicle layouts and velocity-extrapolated states to retrieve physically relevant historical cases. On Highway-env’s multilane highway-driving task, CPW-Drive achieves success rates of 93.0%, 86.0%, and 72.0% across three traffic configurations. These results outperform the strongest baseline by 8.0, 22.5, and 25.0 percentage points, respectively. Across all configurations, CPW-Drive achieves the highest collision-free step count and maintains a low lane-change frequency. The results suggest that structured value guidance can improve simulated closed-loop safety and stability while introducing efficiency and latency trade-offs. 

Keywords: Autonomous driving, Large language models, Chinese philosophical wisdom, Confu-cian thought, Physics-aware spatial similarity retrieval 

# 1 INTRODUCTION 

Recent advances in autonomous driving have improved perception, planning, and control. However, data-driven approaches, including reinforcement learning (RL) and imitation learning (IL), still face interpretability and generalization challenges in open-ended, long-tail scenarios (Kiran et al., 2022; Zhu & Zhao, 2022; Tian et al., 2025a). Moreover, numerical optimization and prediction objectives 1arXiv:2610.03948v3 [cs.AI] 14 Oct 2026 Preprint do not explicitly encode every social norm that can influence human driving. Interactive traffic therefore requires trade-offs among safety, efficiency, and traffic etiquette. Knowledge-driven autonomous driving has consequently attracted increasing attention. Recent stud-ies have explored large language models (LLMs) for driving decisions because they can combine language-based reasoning, external knowledge, and scene descriptions (Mao et al., 2024; Wen et al., 2024). Most LLM-based driving methods rely on task instructions, commonsense knowledge, or historical experience (Mao et al., 2023; Sha et al., 2023; Wen et al., 2024). More recent systems re-trieve traffic regulations or learn value preferences (Cai et al., 2026; Xia et al., 2026). However, the literature has not established how culturally grounded philosophical principles can be represented as reusable, scenario-level driving examples. This gap limits the systematic application of abstract value principles to concrete traffic interactions. 

Figure 1: Conceptual illustration of CPW-Drive. Driving-relevant value principles derived from Chinese philo-sophical wisdom provide retrievable guidance for closed-loop LLM-based driving decisions. To address this gap, we propose Chinese Philosophical Wisdom-Guided Driving (CPW-Drive), an LLM-based framework that integrates Chinese philosophical wisdom into autonomous driving decision-making. The frame-work transforms abstract values into structured driving knowledge for concrete traffic scenarios. We select Con-fucian thought as the knowledge source, extract high-frequency keywords from relevant classical texts, and consolidate them into ten driving-relevant value princi-ples through manual screening and conceptual integra-tion. These value principles are then combined with spe-cific driving scenarios to form retrievable and reusable value-guided driving cases, while Physics-aware Spatial Similarity Retrieval (PSSR) retrieves relevant historical cases for the current decision. Figure 1 illustrates the overall concept. The main contributions of this paper are summarized as follows: • To the best of our knowledge, CPW-Drive is the first LLM-based autonomous driving framework to incorporate driving-relevant value principles derived from Chinese philosophical wisdom. • We derive driving-relevant value principles from Confucian classics and organize them into retrievable, scenario-specific cases, and introduce PSSR for historical scene retrieval. • Closed-loop experiments across three traffic configurations show higher success rates, more collision-free steps, and restrained driving behavior under Confucian value guidance, with an efficiency trade-off. 

# 2 RELATED WORK 

2.1 LLM S FOR AUTONOMOUS DRIVING DECISION -M AKING 

The reasoning and commonsense capabilities of LLMs have extended their use to sequential decision systems, as demonstrated by reasoning-and-acting, self-reflection, and skill-acquisition agents such as ReAct, Reflexion, and Voyager (Yao et al., 2023; Shinn et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024). LLMs have also been applied to autonomous driving decisions. DriveLikeAHuman organizes decisions using a human-inspired cognitive structure (Fu et al., 2024b). DiLu integrates reasoning, memory, and reflection into a knowledge-driven closed-loop driving framework (Wen et al., 2024). GPT-Driver formulates planning as language-sequence generation (Mao et al., 2023), while DriveGPT4 studies interpretable end-to-end autonomous driving with LLMs (Xu et al., 2024). LanguageMPC connects LLM reasoning to vehicle control through adaptive planning (Sha et al., 2023). LMDrive integrates multimodal sensor information with natural-language instructions for closed-loop driv-ing (Shao et al., 2024). LimSim++ provides a closed-loop evaluation platform (Fu et al., 2024a). 2Preprint More recently, DriveGPT4-V2 further explores enhanced closed-loop autonomous driving with mul-timodal LLMs (Xu et al., 2025). These methods primarily address language-based planning, commonsense reasoning, and the use of historical experience. DriveReg and KnowVal additionally incorporate traffic regulations or learned value preferences (Cai et al., 2026; Xia et al., 2026). However, how philosophical principles can be grounded in concrete driving scenarios to support decision-making remains underexplored. 2.2 ETHICAL AND PHILOSOPHICAL VALUES IN AUTONOMOUS DRIVING 

Early autonomous-driving ethics mainly focused on collision dilemmas and harm allocation in un-avoidable accidents. Bonnefon et al. (2016) identified a public-acceptance paradox for utilitarian autonomous vehicles, while Awad et al. (2018) used the Moral Machine to reveal cross-cultural differences in ethical preferences. Sui & Sunday Gr` eve (2024) proposed a Confucian moral al-gorithm for trolley-type harm allocation, rather than LLM-based continuous closed-loop driving. However, dilemma studies provide an insufficient basis for policy and limited guidance for contin-uous real-world driving, while policy discussions address broader normative considerations (Dewitt et al., 2019; Horizon 2020 Commission Expert Group to advise on specific ethical issues raised by driverless mobility (E03659), 2020). Technical approaches largely formalize traffic rules, safety principles, and ethical requirements as constraints, cost functions, or decision rules. The Responsibility-Sensitive Safety (RSS) model en-codes safety responsibilities as proper-response rules (Shalev-Shwartz et al., 2017), while legal-rule models represent traffic regulations and exceptions as structured obligations (Bhuiyan et al., 2023). Although operationally clear, these methods provide limited support for broader value considera-tions. LLMs offer an alternative means of incorporating regulations, social norms, and ethical knowledge expressed in natural language, with recent driving studies exploring regulatory compliance and pref-erence alignment (Cai et al., 2026; Xia et al., 2026). Beyond driving, ConfuciusLM applies Con-fucian pedagogical principles through fine-tuning for primary education, whereas CyberConfucius retrieves classical Confucian texts for philosophical counseling (Xu et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2025). FLAMES and CMoralEval benchmark Chinese values and moral norms for LLM evaluation (Huang et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2024). Existing studies have incorporated philosophical and culturally situated values into LLM training, retrieval, or evaluation, but their use in continuous driving decision-making remains underexplored. Rather than formalizing Confucian values as scalar rewards or optimization objectives, CPW-Drive represents them through natural-language principles and scenario-grounded historical cases to guide LLM-based closed-loop driving decisions at inference time. 2.3 RETRIEVAL -A UGMENTED DECISION -M AKING AND SCENE SIMILARITY MATCHING 

In autonomous driving, retrieval-augmented methods use external knowledge or historical experi-ence to support decision-making. Existing approaches commonly retrieve relevant knowledge or historical cases based on natural-language descriptions or embedding representations (Cai et al., 2026; Xia et al., 2026; Ye et al., 2025; Wen et al., 2024; Mao et al., 2024). However, semanti-cally similar representations may correspond to driving scenes with substantially different spatial configurations and motion states. To better characterize driving scenes themselves, recent studies have incorporated structured driving information, including BEV representations, scene graphs, trajectory graphs, and spatiotemporal structures, into scene retrieval (Tang et al., 2025; Tian et al., 2025b; Cheng et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2026). These methods typically measure scene similarity through learned scene representations or structural matching, allowing spatial relations, interactions, and trajectory information to be reflected in the retrieval process. In such approaches, scene similarity is generally determined through the resulting representations or structures rather than by an explicit similarity function defined directly over vehicle physical states. Unlike the representation- or structure-based matching approaches above, PSSR defines scene sim-ilarity directly from vehicle physical states. It jointly considers the current spatial configuration and 3Preprint a short-term future spatial state estimated from current vehicle velocities, allowing the similarity calculation to reflect both spatial layout and motion trends. 

# 3 METHODOLOGY 

Figure 2 illustrates the overall architecture of CPW-Drive, which consists of four main components: driving-relevant value principle construction, philosophical driving knowledge-base construction, historical scene retrieval, and closed-loop LLM decision-making. 

Figure 2: System overview of CPW-Drive. Driving-relevant value principles are extracted and com-bined with scenario-specific driving cases to construct the Philosophical Driving Knowledge Base, from which PSSR retrieves relevant historical cases to support closed-loop LLM decision-making. 3.1 LLM-B ASED CLOSED -L OOP DRIVING FRAMEWORK 

CPW-Drive operates as a continuous observation, decision-making, and execution loop. At each step, the Scene Encoder converts the environment state into a structured natural-language Scenario Description for LLM reasoning. The system retains vehicle physical states for PSSR to retrieve relevant historical cases from the Philosophical Driving Knowledge Base. Each case contains four fields: scenario, value principles, reasoning process, and driving action. The LLM reasons over the current Scenario Description, retrieved cases, and available driving ac-tions to generate the current reasoning process and driving action. The Decision Encoder processes the generated decision for execution in the driving environment, and the updated environment state enters the next step. The prompts used by CPW-Drive and an illustrative closed-loop decision example are provided in Appendix B. 3.2 DERIVATION OF DRIVING -R ELEVANT VALUE PRINCIPLES 

We use 131 Confucian classics as the knowledge source and divide the texts into chunks, from which an LLM extracts keywords that capture their core ideas. Keywords extracted across different 4Preprint classics and text chunks are then aggregated, deduplicated, and ranked by identification frequency to form a candidate keyword set. The resulting candidate set contained 40 keywords. The titles of the 131 Confucian classics are listed in Appendix A. The keyword-extraction procedure and results are available in the public code repository. Based on the candidate keyword set, we manually review, screen, and conceptually integrate the keywords according to their consistency with the source texts, conceptual overlap, and relevance to driving decision-making, consolidating similar or redundant terms and removing weakly relevant candidates. The resulting set is then subject to a final review by one of the authors, who holds a doctoral degree in Chinese philosophy. This process yields ten driving-relevant value principles: Wisdom, Righteousness, Respect, Adaptability, Benevolence, Courage, Moderation, Propriety, Em-pathy, and Trustworthiness. These value principles provide high-level value considerations rather than directly prescribing driv-ing actions. We further combine them with driving scenarios, reasoning processes, and actions to construct historical driving cases for the philosophical driving knowledge base. 3.3 PHILOSOPHICAL DRIVING KNOWLEDGE BASE CONSTRUCTION 

The Philosophical Driving Knowledge Base is organized as a set of historical driving cases. Each case contains four fields: scenario, value principles, reasoning process, and driving action, and can be represented as C = ( S, V, R, A ). Here, S describes the traffic state at the decision moment, including the positions, velocities, and accelerations of the ego vehicle and surrounding vehicles; V

denotes the value principles relevant to the scenario; R records the corresponding reasoning process; and A specifies the resulting driving action. This structure preserves both the driving decision and its value-guided reasoning process. To construct historical driving cases, we collect more than 3,000 candidate scenarios from Highway-env under different lane configurations, vehicle densities, and random seeds, and remove scenarios with highly similar local traffic configurations. For each retained scenario, the scenario information and the full set of value principles derived in the previous section are provided to the LLM, which identifies the relevant principles and generates the corresponding reasoning process and driving action, forming an initial historical case. The generated cases are then manually reviewed for the appropriateness of the selected value prin-ciples, the consistency of the reasoning process with the scenario and selected principles, and the plausibility and feasibility of the driving action. Cases with correctable issues are manually revised, while unsuitable cases are removed. The final knowledge base contains 135 historical driving cases for subsequent PSSR-based retrieval. 3.4 PHYSICS -AWARE SPATIAL SIMILARITY RETRIEVAL

# 4 EXPERIMENTS 

4.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

We evaluate CPW-Drive in the highway-v0 environment of the Highway-env simulator (Leurent, 2018). The discrete meta-action space contains five driving actions: change lane left, maintain the current state, change lane right, accelerate, and decelerate. Following the experimental settings of DiLu (Wen et al., 2024), we use three traffic configurations with increasing traffic complexity: four lanes with density 2.0, five lanes with density 2.5, and five lanes with density 3.0. For each configuration, we conduct 10 evaluation rounds, each comprising 20 runs with different random seeds. Each run contains 30 decision steps at a simulated policy frequency of 1 Hz, and a trial is considered successful if the ego vehicle completes all 30 steps without a collision. Main and ablation results are reported as the mean and standard deviation across the 10 rounds. Knowledge-base and test scenarios are generated with non-overlapping random seeds. All main experiments use 135 human-screened historical cases, with the top three PSSR-ranked cases retrieved at each decision step as reasoning demonstrations; wall-clock inference latency is evaluated separately in Appendix E. We compare CPW-Drive with DriveLikeAHuman (Fu et al., 2024b), DiLu (Wen et al., 2024), and LimSim++ (Fu et al., 2024a). DriveLikeAHuman represents an LLM-based human-like driving decision method, while DiLu and LimSim++ represent LLM-based autonomous driving methods incorporating knowledge retrieval or simulation-based reasoning. All methods use DeepSeek-V4-Pro through the same API without additional fine-tuning. The baselines are reproduced from their publicly available implementations while preserving their original prompt templates and method-specific settings. We use four evaluation metrics: success rate (SR), collision-free steps (CFS), efficiency score (ES) (Jiang et al., 2025), and lane-change frequency (LCF). SR is the proportion of trials that com-plete all 30 decision steps without collision, and CFS is the number of steps completed before collision or termination. Following KoMA, ES measures driving efficiency based on the ratio of the ego vehicle’s speed to the average speed of surrounding vehicles; it is set to 10 when the ego vehicle’s speed reaches or exceeds that average and is otherwise scaled proportionally. LCF records the frequency of lateral maneuvers and is treated as a descriptive behavioral metric rather than a metric with a monotonic preference. 4.2 MAIN PERFORMANCE ANALYSIS

The following ablation study further examines the roles of retrieval, philosophical knowledge, and PSSR, and tests whether this performance pattern can be explained by generic conservative driving prompts. 4.3 ABLATION STUDY 

We evaluate three ablations and two prompt-based controls to isolate retrieval, PSSR, philosophical content, and generic conservatism. No RAG removes historical-case retrieval; Text RAG replaces PSSR with text-vector retrieval using the same database and retrieval count; and RAG w/o Philoso-phy retains PSSR and the same historical scenarios but excludes value principles, generating reason-ing and actions solely from scene information. Philosophy in Prompt directly places the ten value principles in the system prompt without retrieval, whereas Conservative Prompt combines non-philosophical historical cases with generic conservative-driving instructions. Both control prompts are provided in Appendix B. Retrieval ablations support complementary roles for historical cases and PSSR. Relative to CPW-Drive, No RAG reduces SR and CFS and increases LCF, showing that retrieved cases support current-scene decisions. Text RAG also reduces SR and CFS, indicating that PSSR retrieves better-matched cases by modeling spatial relations and motion states. The philosophy-related comparisons reveal a safety-efficiency trade-off. Relative to CPW-Drive, 

RAG w/o Philosophy yields lower SR and CFS but higher ES and LCF across all configurations, indicating that philosophical content contributes to safer, more restrained behavior at an efficiency cost. Philosophy in Prompt approaches the full model in SR and CFS but further lowers ES and LCF, indicating greater conservatism from direct value injection. Grounding values in concrete scenarios, reasoning processes, and actions clarifies their scene-specific application, mitigating this conservatism and efficiency loss. Conservative Prompt produces the lowest LCF but incurs greater efficiency loss and degrades more sharply as traffic complexity increases. Thus, CPW-Drive’s per-formance cannot be explained by generic conservatism alone, and excessive restrictions on proactive maneuvers, such as lane changes and acceleration, may reduce adaptability to complex traffic. # 5 CONCLUSION 

This paper presents CPW-Drive, a closed-loop RAG framework that integrates Chinese philosophi-cal wisdom into LLM-based autonomous driving decision-making. The framework derives driving-relevant value principles from Confucian classics and organizes them together with specific driving scenarios, reasoning processes, and actions in the Philosophical Driving Knowledge Base, while PSSR retrieves relevant historical cases according to vehicle spatial structure and motion trends. Across three simulated traffic configurations, CPW-Drive achieves the highest SR and CFS and maintains a relatively low LCF, while exhibiting lower ES. The current study is limited by a knowledge base containing 135 historical cases and relatively short 30-step trials. In addition, the approximately 20 s mean end-to-end decision latency does 9Preprint not yet meet real-time control requirements. Future work will further evaluate the framework in more diverse driving environments and with larger knowledge bases, while improving its real-time decision-making capability. AI USE STATEMENT

# C EXTERNAL EVALUATION OF CONFUCIAN VALUE CONFORMITY 

This appendix further evaluates the extent to which the reasoning processes and driving decisions generated by CPW-Drive conform to Confucian values. To enhance the independence and objectiv-ity of the evaluation, we adopt the publicly available evaluation framework from PICACO as a third-party assessment tool rather than devising evaluation criteria specifically for our method. PICACO, published at ICML 2026, investigates pluralistic value alignment and explicitly treats Confucianism as a value composition rooted in a specific cultural and philosophical tradition (Jiang et al., 2026). 17 Preprint It represents Confucian values along four dimensions, namely Benevolence, Conformity, Tradition, and Security, thereby providing an evaluation module that measures the extent to which a model response reflects these values. We do not use PICACO’s meta-instruction optimization method and employ only its Confucian-value evaluation module. The entire evaluation directly runs the offi-cial publicly available code, without any modification to the evaluation prompts, value definitions, scoring criteria, or aggregation procedure. Specifically, we randomly sampled one decision frame from each of 40 completed runs, each ini-tialized with a random map seed, yielding 40 evaluation samples. Because adjacent frames within the same driving episode typically contain highly similar traffic scenes and decision contexts, only one frame was sampled from each run to reduce temporal redundancy and prevent nearly identical scenes from being overrepresented in the evaluation set. For each sample, the original driving de-cision query sent to CPW-Drive contained the current traffic scene information, and the complete reasoning process and final driving decision generated by CPW-Drive constituted the model re-sponse to be evaluated. The Confucian value evaluation module in PICACO was provided with both the original query and the model response and assessed the extent to which the response reflected Confucian values in the context of the scene described in the query. The PICACO evaluation module scores each model response along four dimensions, namely Benev-olence, Conformity, Tradition, and Security, and also provides a relevance score that measures the correspondence between the model response and the original query. All scores use a scale from 1 to 5, where a higher score indicates that the response more strongly reflects the corresponding Confu-cian value or is more relevant to the original query. The module first averages the scores across the four Confucian value dimensions, then multiplies this average by the relevance score and divides the result by the maximum score of 5 to obtain the overall Confucian conformity score. The final score therefore jointly reflects the extent to which the response embodies Confucian values and its relevance to the original query. The distributions of these scores across the 40 evaluation samples are shown in Figure 9.

Figure 10(b)–(d) shows that text-vector retrieval returns historical cases with the same number of surrounding vehicles as the query, but does not consistently preserve their longitudinal positions or lane relations. Two factors may explain this behavior. First, all scenarios are rendered using highly similar fixed text templates, with each surrounding vehicle contributing a similarly structured clause. Scenes containing the same number of surrounding vehicles therefore have similar text lengths and sentence structures, and may receive high overall text similarity even when their physical configura-tions differ. Second, text-vector retrieval does not explicitly model ego-centered vehicle geometry, and its similarity measure is not directly constrained by lane relations, relative distances, or motion states. Consequently, spatial differences that occupy only a small portion of the description but are critical to driving decisions may be weakened by the overall semantic similarity. In contrast, the PSSR results in Figure 10(e)–(g) are determined directly from ego-centered vehicle physical states and do not depend on the length or template structure of the scene descriptions. Although these historical cases differ from the query in vehicle count and exact positions, their principal vehicle relations and lane constraints are better preserved. PSSR neither requires two scenes to contain identical vehicle sets nor establishes explicit one-to-one vehicle correspondences; instead, it measures scene similarity according to the consistency of the overall spatial configurations around the ego vehicle. By combining the current and velocity-extrapolated spaces, PSSR accounts for both the current vehicle layout and short-term motion trends, thereby providing the LLM with historical decision references under more closely matched physical constraints. This appendix analyzes the computational complexity and working-memory cost of PSSR and em-pirically compares its retrieval efficiency with vector-based retrieval methods, complementing the method description in Section 3.4. Let the historical driving knowledge base contain M cases, with each scene containing at most N

surrounding vehicles. For a current scene and a single historical scene, the scene-level spatial overlap in PSSR requires pairwise spatial similarity computation between the two vehicle sets, resulting in a computational complexity of O(N 2). Computing both the current and velocity-extrapolated spatial states introduces only a fixed number of additional operations and therefore does not change the asymptotic complexity. Comparing the current scene with all M historical scenes thus requires 

O(M N 2) time. After all similarity scores are computed, the M scores are fully sorted and the Top-

k historical cases are selected. The sorting step requires O(M log M ) time, while selecting the first 

k results incurs an additional O(k) cost. Since this term is small relative to the preceding similarity computation and sorting costs, the overall online retrieval complexity of PSSR can be written as 

O(M N 2 + M log M ). In terms of working memory, the retrieval process stores the vehicle states of the current scene together with the similarity scores for all M historical cases, resulting in an additional working-memory complexity of O(M + N ).We next evaluate the online decision latency of all methods to quantify the computational overhead introduced by different inference and retrieval pipelines.

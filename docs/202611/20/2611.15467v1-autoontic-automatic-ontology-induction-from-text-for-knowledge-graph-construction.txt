## AutoOntic: Automatic Ontology Induction from Text for Knowledge Graph Construction

Abstract 

Graph-based retrieval-augmented generation depends on the quality of the knowledge graph (KG) it retrieves over, yet schema-free LLM-based extraction pipelines typically produce flat graphs: deduplicated entities and relations with no type hierarchy and no domain/range constraints. We present AutoOntic, a pipeline that automatically constructs an ontology from text by canonicalizing open-vocabulary entity types and relations, inducing a hierarchy over the canonicalized types, and mining relation-level domain and range constraints. Its hierar-chy induction procedure prioritizes types using corpus-derived relation-argument profiles and places them through local, LLM-adjudicated parent/child checks, allowing newly inserted types to trigger reassignment of existing parent-child links. The resulting hierarchy constrains entity deduplication and supports the induction of relation-level domain/range constraints. For intrinsic evaluation, we introduce a reference-based protocol that classifies induced ontology types as exact, broader, narrower, or unrelated to reference domain and range classes. On the Wikidata-TekGen and DBpedia-WebNLG subsets of Text2KGBench, over 90% of Au-toOntic’s induced endpoint types are semanti-cally aligned with the reference classes across two LLM backbones: about half match exactly, while most remaining differences concern gran-ularity. On MINE, knowledge graphs orga-nized using an ontology built by AutoOntic achieve 88% retrieval accuracy. AutoOntic is available at https://github.com/screemix/ AutoOntic .

1 Introduction 

Retrieval-augmented generation grounds large lan-guage model (LLM) outputs in retrieved evidence (Lewis et al., 2020). However, retrieval over chun-ked text can struggle when answering multi-hop questions that require connecting evidence across documents. Graph-based approaches organize this evidence into knowledge graphs (KGs), where in-formation is represented as entities and relations, enabling retrieval across connected facts (Edge et al., 2024). The effectiveness of this approach de-pends not only on which facts a knowledge graph (KG) contains, but also on how those facts are orga-nized. In particular, KG-RAG benefits from ontol-ogy (Chepurova et al., 2026), a hierarchy of entity types that represent general categories and their specializations, and relation domain and range con-straints that express which types of entities a rela-tion connects. These structures provide a basis for category-level retrieval and structural validation, but must be constructed reliably to be useful. Building a KG with this structure from text re-mains challenging. Schema-free extraction meth-ods such as KGGen (Mo et al., 2025) extract and canonicalize entities and relations to provide more compact and well-connected KG without requir-ing a predefined domain schema, but do not ex-plicitly induce a hierarchy of entity types or re-lation domain and range constraints. Conversely, ontology-aware KG construction methods can use pre-built ontologies to organize and constrain ex-tracted knowledge (Chepurova et al., 2026), but applying an existing ontology to a new corpus may require aligning its vocabulary with corpus-specific expressions and introducing concepts absent from the original schema. These observations motivate a method that goes beyond flat KG construction to induce a corpus-grounded ontology, while retain-ing the option to incorporate an existing ontology when available. We present AutoOntic , an ontology construc-tion pipeline that extends schema-free approaches to close this gap: on top of a deduplicated entity and relation vocabulary, it produces a hyponymy-aware, hierarchically organized type vocabulary, an entity vocabulary deduplicated against that hier-archy, and a set of per-relation domain/range con-arXiv:2611.15467v1 [cs.CL] 11 Nov 2026 Input — Text Corpus

2 Methods: AutoOntic 

AutoOntic turns a textual corpus into a canonical-ized, typed knowledge graph: a deduplicated rela-tion vocabulary, a deduplicated and hierarchically organized entity-type vocabulary, a deduplicated entity vocabulary with class assignments, and a set of relation-level domain/range constraints (Fig-ure 1). At first, a schema-free LLM extractor (§2.1) produces a collection G of raw triplets, each repre-sented as: 

g = ( s, t s, r, o, t o, Q ), (1) where s – subject entity, ts – subject type, r – re-lation predicate, o – object entity, to – object type, and Q – an optional list of qualifiers extracted from texts (Chepurova et al., 2026). Then G is passed further for canonicalization and typing. Ev-ery downstream stage resolves fields into canonical identifiers, keeping each stage’s data dependencies explicit and independently inspectable. The AutoOntic pipeline comprises six ordered stages: (1) typed triplet extraction, (2) relation canonicalization, (3) entity-type canonicalization, (4) hierarchy induction, (5) entity canonicalization and class assignment, and (6) domain/range con-straint induction . This ordering follows data de-pendencies: canonical relations inform type-label disambiguation; the induced type hierarchy con-strains candidate entity merges; and the canonical vocabularies and direction-corrected triplets pro-vide evidence for domain/range constraints. 

Shared canonicalization procedure. The fol-lowing design choices reoccur across stages. (1) Deduplication of synonymous surface forms is performed by clustering Contriever sentence embeddings (Izacard et al., 2021) with HDB-SCAN (Campello et al., 2013) at a stage-specific cosine-similarity threshold θ, and every merge/s-plit decision is adjudicated by a single LLM call per candidate cluster. (2) Merging is done through several rounds: each round re-embeds the current pool, re-clusters it, and sends multi-member clus-ters to the LLM for verification, folding accepted merges back into the pool for the next round. The procedure repeats until the vocabulary size stops reducing or a configurable round limit R is reached. (3) The result of each stage is checkpointed once it completes, allowing independent inspection of every stage’s output and reuse in follow-up exper-iments. Every θ and R instance below is stage-specific (relation, type, and entity deduplication each set their own); concrete values for these and every other hyperparameter introduced in §2.2– §2.6 are collected in Table 3 (Appendix A). 

2.1 Typed Triplet Extraction 

An LLM extracts subject–relation–object triplets enriched with subject/object types and contextual qualifiers with meta-information that cannot be ex-pressed as a standalone triplet such as time, lo-cation, or condition. Types are initially uncon-strained free text. Each extracted record has the form g = ( s, t s, r, o, t o, Q ), where s, o are entity mentions, ts, t o their raw types, r the relation, and 

Q an optional qualifier list. The extracted records form G. The full extraction prompt is given in Appendix F, Listing 1. 

2.2 Relation Canonicalization 

Relation canonicalization maps the raw, free-text 

relation strings observed across all triplets onto a canonical relation vocabulary R∗. Candidate clus-ters are generated by embedding every raw relation surface form and clustering with HDBSCAN at similarity threshold θr, as described in §2. Every multi-member candidate cluster is then passed to a single LLM call that decides whether to merge all members into one relation or split them into several (see the prompt in Listing 2); this repeats for up to 

Rr rounds until no round shrinks R∗ any further. 

2.3 Entity-Type Canonicalization 

Raw type labels are canonicalized into T ∗. Canon-ical types are then organized into a single-parent hierarchy H∗, which may form a forest. Here, en-tity types correspond to ontology classes. 

Type Deduplication Candidate clusters over raw type labels are generated the same way as for rela-tions (§2.2, threshold θt), but with one additional source of evidence: each candidate label shown to the LLM verifier is annotated with the top kctx 

canonical relations it most often co-occurs with (a PPMI-weighted relation-argument profile). These profiles provide corpus-derived structural evidence beyond lexical or embedding similarity: two types may have similar labels but different relational roles, or different labels but nearly identical dis-tributions of relational contexts. The verification step uses this evidence alongside the labels to de-cide whether a candidate pair should be merged. The verification prompt additionally enforces a hyponymy (“kind of”) test: two labels are merged only if neither is a hyponym of the other, even when they are lexically similar or their relation-argument profiles overlap almost entirely. For example, doc-umentary film and film are similar and participate in the same relations, but LLM is instructed to keep them distinct, whereas movie and film , true synonyms, should be merged. 2.4 Hierarchy Induction 

Flat type canonicalization removes surface redun-dancy but does not encode subsumption relation-ships between them. AutoOntic therefore induces an explicit is-a hierarchy over T ∗ to support two op-erations that depend on these relationships: restrict-ing entity-merge candidates to structurally com-patible types (§2.5) and generalizing observed do-main/range types to shared ancestors when estimat-ing relation constraints (§2.6). In AutoOntic, canonical types are organized into a single-parent hierarchy H∗, which may form a forest. Rather than asking an LLM to organize the entire type vocabulary in a single call, AutoOntic decomposes placement into local decisions over the current hierarchy H∗, inserting types one by one from general to specific. To find such ordering of entity types, we compare the PPMI-weighted relation-argument profiles associated with each deduplicated type. Following the distributional in-clusion principle, a more general type is expected to occur in a broader range of relational contexts than its more specific subtypes. We use Weeds pre-cision (Weeds et al., 2004) to estimate the extent to which the contexts associated with one type are contained in those associated with another. The re-sulting containment scores define an approximate generality score based on how many other types could fit beneath each type. Types are processed in descending score order; these scores are not treated as proof of subsumption. Actual parent-child rela-tionships are determined by the subsequent LLM-adjudicated placement procedure, which can revise earlier attachments as additional types are inserted. The hierarchy can be initialized from a seed or ex-isting ontology and extended with types discovered in new text. Selected seed categories can be pinned as roots. For each type, AutoOntic traverses the hierarchy downward from the current roots. At each level, the LLM performs separate parent and, if no par-ent is found, child checks. The parent check asks whether an existing candidate subsumes the new type. A parent match continues traversal through that node’s children; a synonymous candidate is instead merged with the new type. The child check asks whether the new type subsumes any existing candidates. A child match attaches the matched nodes beneath the new type and stops traversal. If neither succeeds, the type is inserted at the current position. When a level contains more than B can-didates, a consolidation call attaches clear siblings under synthesized parents proposed by the LLM. This design keeps each decision focused on a bounded part of the hierarchy, reducing the context required for an individual LLM call and avoiding repeated presentation of the entire ontology. More importantly, the decisions have explicit structural consequences: a later, more general type can cor-rect an earlier attachment by becoming the parent of an existing node. Thus, the procedure does not merely append labels to a taxonomy; it incremen-tally constructs and repairs the hierarchy as new evidence becomes available. Placement depends on the existing hierarchy and the order in which types are processed. The local checks, consolidation procedure, and reassignment rules are specified in Appendix F. 2.5 Entity Canonicalization and Class Assignment 

Entity mentions are represented by (name , type ),allowing identical names with different types to remain distinct (e.g., Paris the city and Paris the person). Initial comparisons are restricted to mentions with the same type or sibling types under the same immediate parent in H∗ (§2.4). Clustering enti-ties surface forms is performed with HDBSCAN among candidates within one sibling group. Within each such partition, merging then follows the round-based procedure common to every deduplication stage (§2, Listing 7), applied independently per par-tition. Candidates are clustered at similarity thresh-old θe, and each partition’s embed-cluster-verify cycle repeats for up to Re rounds. A final reconciliation pass broadens candidate comparisons across sibling groups whose types share a common ancestor in H∗. A merged entity retains the union of its mention types, with their lowest common ancestor assigned as its primary type. 

2.6 Domain/Range Constraint Induction 

Constraint induction first re-resolves every raw triplet against the canonical relation and type vocab-ularies, R∗ and T ∗, produced by §2.2 and §2.3. Be-cause relation canonicalization may merge a pred-icate with its grammatical inverse (e.g. directed 

and was directed by ), each canonical relation with multiple surface forms is processed by an LLM that classifies each form as forward or inverted ;inverted forms then have their subject and object types swapped before constraint estimation. For each canonical relation, domain and range types are considered independently. We compute the exact lowest common ancestor (LCA) of ob-served types in H∗. If observations span discon-nected roots, they are partitioned by root and an LCA is computed within each partition, yielding multiple domain/range signatures when a relation connects unrelated type families. Finally, triplets are grouped by their direction-corrected (Td, T r) signature, and each signature is scored by a confidence equal to the share of relation 

r’s own triplets it accounts for: 

conf( r, T d, T r) = support( r, T d, T r)total( r) , (2) where Td and Tr are the domain and range entity types, respectively. 

3 Evaluation 

3.1 Ontology evaluation Benchmark . We evaluate AutoOntic on 

Text2KGBench (Mihindukulasooriya et al., 2023), a benchmark of sentence–triple pairs annotated against small, hand-curated domain ontologies, comprising two subsets: Wikidata-TekGen (10 domain ontologies) and DBpedia-WebNLG (19 domain ontologies). Each domain ontology de-fines a set of relations coupled with domain and range types (allowed subject and object types re-spectively). Every test sentence is paired with a gold triple set {(s, r, o )}, where r is drawn from the ontology’s relation vocabulary. We use the 

test split with 4,062 sentences across the 10 Wikidata-TekGen ontologies and 2,014 across the 19 DBpedia-WebNLG ontologies. Text2KGBench’s intended task is ontology-guided generation: a system is given the ontology and must extract compliant triplets. We consider the inverse setting: AutoOntic is schema-free and receives no ontology as input. Instead, it must induce a schema and extract triplets from the text. We then compare the induced schema and extracted triplets against the benchmark’s gold ontology and gold triplets. We used two LLM backbones driving every stage of the pipeline: Qwen3-Next-80B-A3B-Instruct (Yang et al., 2025) and gpt-oss-120b (Agar-wal et al., 2025), both run with the identical pipeline hyperparameters listed in Table 3 (Ap-pendix A). Judging is performed in every condi-tion by a fixed model, GPT-5.1, independent of whichever backbone built the KG. 

Triple matching. For each gold triple, we col-lect the triples extracted by AutoOntic from the same sentence and ask an LLM judge to identify an extracted triple that expresses the same under-lying fact, allowing for paraphrased relation labels and inverse formulations that swap the subject and object (e.g., X composed Y vs. Y was composed by X). The judge returns the index of a matching triple and labels its direction as same or inverse , or indi-cates that no match exists. Matching is one-to-one: once an extracted triple is matched to a gold triple, it is removed from the candidate pool and cannot be matched to another gold triple. We match triples from the gold side outward to evaluate recovery of the reference annotations without automatically treating every unmatched ex-traction as an error. Text2KGBench uses curated re-lation vocabularies containing roughly 15–20 rela-tions per domain, whereas a schema-free extraction system may recover valid facts that are not repre-sented in the gold annotations. We therefore report three complementary metrics. Recall is the pro-portion of gold triples matched by extracted triples. The extra rate is the proportion of extracted triples left unmatched after all gold triples have been pro-cessed; these triples may represent facts omitted from the reference annotations rather than extrac-tion errors. We report the extra rate separately from recall because unmatched extractions cannot be re-liably classified as incorrect solely on the basis of their absence from the gold annotations. 

Domain/Range Type Matching. For each matched triple, we compare AutoOntic’s subject and object types with the gold relation’s domain and range classes. For matches classified as inverse ,we swap the gold domain and range. An LLM judge classifies each comparison as E XACT (equiv-alent types), M ORE GENERAL (AutoOntic’s type subsumes the gold type), M ORE NARROW (Au-toOntic’s type is a subtype of the gold type), or N OT 

RELATED . We report the distribution of these judg-ments separately for domain and range, thereby distinguishing exact type alignment in triplets from differences in the generality of the induced types. 

Constraint Plausibility. Type matching evalu-ates individual triples, whereas AutoOntic also in-duces relation-level domain and range constraints by generalizing observed argument types using their lowest common ancestor (LCA). To evaluate these constraints, for each matched triple we iden-tify the induced constraint(s) whose domain and range cover the triple’s subject and object types, ac-counting for inverse matches and allowing multiple constraints when a relation spans disconnected hi-erarchy partitions. We then compare each selected constraint’s domain and range with the correspond-ing gold classes using the same four judgment cat-egories as in Domain/Range Type Matching. We report the resulting distributions alongside each constraint’s support and confidence, including con-straints below the τhint threshold and regardless of their H ARD , S OFT , or H INT tier (§2.6). Comparing instance-level type judgments with constraint-level judgments shows how well the induced constraints align with the reference ontology and whether they tend to be more general or more specific than the gold classes. Text2KGBench’s Ontology Conformance metric measures whether extracted triples use relations from the input ontology’s canonical relation vo-cabulary; leaving domain/range validation as a di-rection for future extension (Mihindukulasooriya et al., 2023). Our evaluation complements this met-ric by assessing domain and range alignment at both the instance and induced-constraint levels, us-ing graded judgments that distinguish equivalent, broader, narrower, and unrelated types. 

3.2 Information Retention Dataset . We complement reference-ontology align-ment §3.1 with MINE-1 (Mo et al., 2025), which evaluates how much factual information from source text remains recoverable from a constructed KG. This directly tests a practical requirement of ontology-aware KG construction: organizing and canonicalizing extracted knowledge should preserve the information needed for subsequent retrieval. We use all 100 essays (topic and con-tent) and their associated generated factual queries (1,500 in total). Every essay’s triplets are ex-tracted (§2.1) into one combined corpus and passed through the pipeline once, yielding a single shared ontology and KG spanning all 100 essays. We eval-uate both construction without a supplied ontology and a Wikidata-seeded variant, whose type hierar-chy is initialized with Wikidata classes. This com-parison assesses whether existing ontology struc-ture can further improve information retention. 

Task and method. We mirror KGGen’s own MINE evaluation methodology exactly, for direct comparability to its reported numbers on the same benchmark: each generated query is both the re-trieval query and the fact to check for. Context is retrieved from AutoOntic’s constructed KG via KGGen’s own method (sentence-embedding simi-larity over entities followed by a fixed-depth graph walk, top-k = 8 ), and an LLM judge is asked a single binary question: whether the retrieved con-text contains the information stated in the query. We report overall accuracy : the fraction of the 1,500 queries the judge scores as supported by their retrieved context, both averaged over the whole benchmark and broken down per essay.

A single overly broad type can therefore make a relation’s constraint less specific, even when most instances have informative types. This issue appears more pronounced on Wikidata-TekGen: the raw type vocabulary is substantially more fragmented relative to the gold classes than on DBpedia-WebNLG ( 1). Seeding the hierar-chy with Wikidata classes can anchor its upper levels, but does not resolve fragmentation through-out the hierarchy. Limiting hierarchy depth has a larger effect in several conditions, often increas-ing the share of constraints classified as related to gold classes. However, this increase can reflect coarser constraints rather than better alignment: a broad ancestor may turn a N OT RELATED judg-ment into M ORE GENERAL without increasing ex-act matches. These results show that Constraint Plausibility is sensitive to hierarchy structure and should not be interpreted as a standalone measure of ontology quality.

Table 2 reports reports information retention on MINE-1. AutoOntic’s KG built with gpt-4.1 reaches 85.87% accuracy vs KGGen 82.37% and Wikontic 82.93. Initializing the hierarchy with Wikidata seed classes further raises retention to 88.00. Unlike the Text2KGBench evaluation above, MINE does not score agreement with a reference schema directly. It queries the constructed KG through embedding-similarity retrieval and a fixed-depth graph walk over entities. So this result demonstrates that the typed, canonicalized KG built for a corpus with no supplied schema improves downstream retrieval.

5 Conclusion 

We presented AutoOntic, a schema-free pipeline that extends KG extraction and canonicalization with a hierarchical type ontology, hierarchy-aware entity deduplication, and induced domain/range constraints. On Text2KGBench, over 90% of induced types in are semantically aligned with reference classes, with most mismatches reflect-ing differences in granularity rather than unre-lated types. On MINE-1, AutoOntic achieves 85.87% information-retention accuracy, increas-ing to 88.00% with Wikidata-seeded hierarchy ini-tialization, outperforming KGGen and Wikontic. These results demonstrate that schema-free ontol-ogy induction can recover useful semantic structure and support effective downstream retrieval. 

Limitations 

Our evaluation measures agreement between the induced ontology and a benchmark’s reference schema, and the usability of the resulting KG for downstream retrieval; it does not directly measure extraction precision, since a schema-free pipeline can correctly extract facts a narrow, curated refer-ence ontology simply does not cover, and such ex-tractions are excluded from recall by design rather than scored as errors. Finally, our evaluation covers English-language corpora and existing benchmark ontologies only; generalization to other languages remains untested.

Used by hierarchy induction (§2.4) to resolve how one focal type relates to the candidates at its current level. Do not consider whether focal is a parent of any candidate -- that is asked separately, in a different call. These are NOT "is-a", no matter how strongly associated the two are: - focal happens DURING / emerged in / belongs to the era of the candidate - focal is PART OF, made of, or located in the candidate - focal is CAUSED BY, produced by, used by, or studied by the candidate - focal is merely a related topic, or co-occurs with the candidate If a candidate is not just a broader category but the SAME concept as focal (a true synonym, e.g. "vehicle" / "means of transport"), say so explicitly --that is a merge, not a parent relationship. F.5 Entity-Name Cluster Verification 

Used during entity deduplication (§2.5) to adjudi-cate a merge/split decision for one candidate cluster of compound name [type] entity mentions, within one parent-gated partition. 

You are a knowledge graph engineer. Below is a group of entity mentions, each in the format "name [type]". These were clustered together by embedding similarity ONLY --being in this list is NOT evidence that they are the same entity. Most clusters contain several DIFFERENT entities that merely sound alike or share a type. Your task: 1. Decide which mentions are SYNONYMOUS: different ways of writing the SAME single real-world entity. 2. Group synonymous entities together. A mention with no synonym forms its own group of one. 3. For each group, choose the BEST canonical name -- the most complete, unambiguous form of that entity's name, taken from the mentions themselves. The canonical name must be JUST the entity name, WITHOUT the [type] bracket. 4. Each group's "members" array must contain the EXACT compound strings as provided (including the [type] part). ### The test for a duplicate Two mentions are SYNONYMOUS ONLY if they are denoting the same person, organization, place, work, event, or concept --such that **every statement true of one is true of the other**, and merging them would LOSE NO INFORMATION. Apply it by substitution: if you swapped one mention for the other in a sentence, would the meaning stay identical? - "Chris Nolan" / "Christopher Nolan" -- identical meaning. SYNONYMOUS. - "Nobel Prize" / "Nobel Prize in Physics" -- one is the award family, the other a specific award. Merging discards " in Physics".

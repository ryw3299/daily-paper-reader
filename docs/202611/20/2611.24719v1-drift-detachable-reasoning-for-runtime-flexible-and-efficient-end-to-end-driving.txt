Abstract 

The integration of vision-language models (VLMs) into end-to-end (E2E) autonomous driving has demonstrated strong potential by providing scene description and reasoning ca-pabilities. However, VLMs are computationally expen-sive, and switching language reasoning on and off at run-time without hurting closed-loop performance has not been demonstrated, which hinders practical adoption. To ad-dress these issues, we propose DRIFT, an E2E autonomous driving framework that integrates a lightweight language model (LM) for visual question answering and action com-mentary. By efficiently projecting intermediate driving rep-resentations into the language embedding space, we demon-strate that a compact LM ( <150M parameters) can achieve strong scene description and action commentary. Further-more, we introduce a runtime-flexible planning module that is trained on surrogate reasoning features distilled from the LM and can be conditioned on either the LM or the surro-gate reasoning features at inference. This design enables bypassing the LM entirely when required by deployment constraints, without sacrificing driving performance. With only 255M parameters, DRIFT reaches up to a Driving Score (DS) of 97.03 and a Success Rate of 89.39% (mean over three runs) on Bench2Drive by interleaving its LM and surrogate reasoning at a chosen reasoning rate. This is on par with the strongest non-language models while being far more compact than existing VLM-based mod-els. DRIFT further achieves competitive results on the real-world NAVSIM and AlpaSim benchmarks at signifi-cantly lower computational cost than existing VLM-based approaches. 1. Introduction 

End-to-end (E2E) driving models [13, 16] achieve promis-ing driving performance, but they lack interpretability and high-level semantic reasoning. To address these limita-tions, recent works have integrated vision-language models (VLMs) into the driving pipeline, enabling visual question answering (VQA) [32, 34] and language-guided reasoning [8, 32]. Despite their success, existing VLM-based E2E methods typically improve language performance by scal-ing up the language models (LMs), incurring substantial computational overhead. Approaches such as DriveVLM [34], ORION [8], and AdaThinkDrive [25] employ mod-els exceeding 7B parameters, making real-time deployment on edge hardware increasingly challenging. This motivates us to study how strong language reasoning can be achieved with a significantly smaller LM. A second limitation is that these frameworks couple lan-guage reasoning tightly to the driving pipeline, requiring LM inference throughout deployment. To reduce this cost, some models run separate fast (standard E2E) and slow (VLM-based) branches and switch between them at run-time [10, 30, 41], while others switch a single VLA between thinking and non-thinking modes [25, 45], but most of them still rely on a large model in complex scenes. Others distill the LM into the driving model, such as DiMA [11], VLM-AD [38], and VERDI [7], but language only shapes the planner during training, and the planner cannot take reason-ing as input at inference. DiMA’s Dual variant is the only one of them that is able to run the LM at inference, but it is evaluated only in open-loop settings, leaving the switching of the LM on and off in difficult closed-loop driving unex-plored. We argue that efficiently integrating a language model into E2E driving requires two properties: compactness , so that the LM adds little computational cost, and detachabil-ity , so that the LM can be removed at runtime without de-1arXiv:2611.24719v1 [cs.CV] 18 Nov 2026 grading driving score. We propose DRIFT, an E2E driving framework that realizes this paradigm. Our main contributions are summarized as follows: • Efficient driving VQA and action commentary with a compact LM: by aligning dense driving representations with the language embedding space, a 135M-parameter LM achieves competitive VQA and action commentary at an edge-compatible latency on a Jetson AGX Orin. Addi-tionally, as an interesting finding, we show that language training also enriches the shared backbone features. • Detachable LM reasoning: we distill the LM’s reasoning into a lightweight surrogate module, so that a single plan-ner can run on either reasoning source and the LM can be enabled or detached without degrading driving score. • Extensive closed-loop experiments on Bench2Drive show, first, that language training improves driving over the language-free base model (+0.5 DS), and second, that combining the LM and surrogate reasoning at a chosen reasoning rate reaches 97.03 DS (+1.8 over TFv6/LEAD [27]). On the real-world NAVSIM bench-mark, DRIFT is competitive with much larger VLM-based planners, and on AlpaSim it is comparable to the LTFv6 and TransFuser-K1 baselines. Moreover, similar to Bench2Drive, enabling the LM on these benchmarks does not degrade planning performance, which shows that the detachable design carries over from simulation to real-world data. 

2. Related work 

2.1. VLMs in end-to-end autonomous driving 

Pioneering E2E autonomous driving models [14, 16, 46] show promising results in directly mapping raw sensors to actions. Recently, LEAD [27] showed that reducing the asymmetry between student and expert boosts perfor-mance, yielding the strong TFv6 and the diffusion-based BridgeDrive [23] models. However, despite their success, these models offer limited interpretability and lack explicit language reasoning. Hint-AD [5] improves interpretabil-ity by decoding language from the intermediate features of a frozen E2E model, but its language only explains the model’s decisions post hoc and does not influence driving. This motivates the integration of VLMs into E2E driving. VLM-based autonomous driving frameworks [18, 24, 26, 36, 37, 40, 43, 44] show the potential of integrating LMs into E2E driving through VQA and language-guided driving. However, they were primarily evaluated in open-loop settings, which may not reflect real-world driving [15, 21]. This has motivated closed-loop VLM-based driv-ing [8, 9, 17, 39, 42], where SimLingo [31] shows that a 1B model suffices when reasoning and driving actions are generated jointly, while LinkVLA [35] and FIVE-VLA [29] further improve efficiency. Yet these frameworks are computationally demanding because of their large LMs, the tight coupling between language reasoning and planning, or both, which limits deployment. 

2.2. Efficient reasoning for autonomous driving 

Fast-slow methods. To reduce the inference cost of VLMs, fast-slow methods invoke them only when needed. Dual-branch designs [10, 20, 30, 34, 41] pair a fast VLM-free path with a slow VLM path, while adaptive-thinking VLAs [22, 25, 45] switch a single model between thinking and direct action. However, most of them still rely on a large VLM or VLA, whose use remains mandatory in complex scenes, which limits deployment under resource constraints. DRIFT also invokes its LM at a chosen reasoning rate, but with a compact LM, a single planner, and the option to by-pass the LM without a drop in driving performance. 

VLM knowledge distillation. A complementary direc-tion transfers VLM knowledge into the driving model dur-ing training so that the VLM can be dropped at deployment. VLM-AD [38] and VERDI [7] use an offline VLM to do reasoning on training samples and train the planner’s fea-tures to match text embeddings of it, whereas DiMA [11] jointly trains a large multi-modal LM (Vicuna 7B) with the planner and aligns their hidden features. In all three, lan-guage only shapes the planner during training and the plan-ner cannot take reasoning as input at inference. DiMA’s Dual variant can also run the LM at inference by fusing LM and planner features, but these methods are evaluated mostly open-loop or in simplified closed-loop settings, so, to our knowledge, switching the LM on and off while driv-ing was not evaluated. In DRIFT, a single planner works interchangeably and flexibly with both surrogate and LM reasoning sources in challenging closed-loop driving. 

3. Proposed method: DRIFT 

In this section, we present DRIFT, an E2E autonomous driv-ing framework equipped with a lightweight, plug-and-play language reasoning module. An overview of the proposed architecture is shown in Fig. 1. Given raw sensor observa-tions, a TransFuser-based backbone extracts a dense scene representation, which serves as the input to the LM for VQA (Sec. 3.1). In addition, the planner is conditioned on surro-gate reasoning tokens that are aligned to the reasoning to-kens of the LM, which can optionally replace them at infer-ence (Sec. 3.2), to facilitate aligned reasoning and driving decisions. Finally, Sec. 3.3 describes the training procedure that enables seamless integration of the detachable language module without degrading driving performance.

3.2. Detachable reasoning 

Effective reasoning for autonomous driving requires align-ment between language reasoning and driving decisions, as also highlighted in [31]. At the same time, we want the lan-guage module to be optional at runtime. We address both with a set of surrogate reasoning tokens that condition the planner together with dense scene features. They are pre-dicted from BEV features alone, which makes the planner independent of the LM. The LM serves as the teacher dur-ing training, and as an optional replacement for the surro-gate module at inference, allowing the planner to condition on language reasoning. Concretely, we use the generated commentary response as the reasoning signal, since it describes the current action together with its justification. During the final training stage and inference, the reasoning tokens are computed from the LM’s own greedy commentary, not from ground-truth text. We extract the last-layer hidden states at the generated an-swer positions, denoted as hLcq , and pool them into K fixed-size reasoning tokens Zreason through an attention pool-ing Pl→r, in which K=16 queries attend over the variable-length hidden states with padded positions masked. 3.3. Training 

3.3.1. Training objective 

Our overall training objective is defined in Eq. (6). To learn rich scene representations, we first optimize a set of perception tasks, including depth estimation, seman-tic segmentation in both RGB and BEV views, and ob-ject detection. Their corresponding losses are jointly de-noted as Lperc . The language model is trained to autore-gressively predict VQA answer tokens using the standard cross-entropy loss, denoted by Llm . For planning, we super-vise the predicted route using an L1 loss over route points (Lcp ), an L1 loss over future ego waypoints ( Lwp ), and a cross-entropy loss over target speed bins ( Lts ), which to-gether define the planning loss Lplan = Lcp + Lwp + Lts .It is applied to the surrogate planning branch only, yield-ing Lsurr plan . 3.3.2. Training schedule 

Motivated by the number of modules and their dependen-cies, we split training into three stages. Stage 1 trains the backbone and perception heads to produce the visual fea-tures all downstream modules rely on. Stage 2 trains the language modules (the LM with LoRA, the projector, and the surrogate projector) to answer VQAs and reason in lan-guage, as well as the auxiliary perception tasks of Stage 1, with the alignment losses active on the ground-truth com-mentary (teacher-forced), while the backbone receives the LM gradient scaled down by 0.1. Stage 3 is the final end-to-end training. Stage 3a trains the backbone, planner, and surrogate path on all frames without the LM. Stage 3b ad-ditionally trains the LM jointly: the alignment losses are re-introduced with reduced weights and a warm-up, and are computed on the LM’s own greedy commentary, which matches inference and avoids targets that depend on the ex-pert’s future, at the cost of one extra LM pass per step (the language loss uses the teacher-forced pass). Ground-truth text is used earlier because it is cheap and informative while the LM is still weak. 

4. Experiments 

4.1. Experimental setup 

We evaluate DRIFT in closed loop on the challenging Bench2Drive benchmark [15] in the CARLA simulator [6], 4reporting Driving Score (DS) and Success Rate (SR). We focus on closed-loop evaluation, since the planner’s ac-tions affect subsequent states, which reflects driving be-havior better than open-loop evaluation [31, 46]. Train-ing uses the LEAD dataset [27], for which we generate VQA annotations following DriveLM [32] and commen-tary annotations following SimLingo [31]; language qual-ity is measured with SPICE, CIDEr, BLEU-4, ROUGE-L and METEOR. To evaluate DRIFT on real-world data, we train it on the navtrain split of NAVSIM [2, 3] and report the PDM Score (PDMS) on navtest and the extended PDM Score (EPDMS) on navhard. We additionally evaluate it in closed loop on the nuPlan track of AlpaSim [28], fol-lowing the 123D protocol [4]. Language annotations for NAVSIM are generated with Qwen3-VL [1]. Training con-sists of three stages, namely perception pre-training follow-ing TFv6 [27], LoRA [12] finetuning of the LM with the alignment losses, and end-to-end training, and runs on 8 NVIDIA H100 GPUs. Further details on the data, metrics and training are in the supplementary.

4.3. Insights 

How often should the LM reason? Because the LM in DRIFT is detachable, we can study how often reasoning needs to be invoked. We vary the reasoning rate (RR), i.e., the LM is invoked every RR frames and the surrogate rea-soning is used in between (Fig. 3), and make two observa-tions. First, invoking the LM periodically slightly improves driving over the model without LM (95.97 DS), with the best setting, RR=2, reaching 97.03 DS (+1.06 over no LM, 95% route-level bootstrap CI [0.2, 2.0]). Since we selected RR on this benchmark, we regard the gain as indicative. The gains of the other rates are within run-to-run variation. Second, invoking the LM at every frame (RR=1) is on par with no reasoning (96.06 vs. 95.97 DS) and below the in-termediate rates. We use a fixed reasoning rate, which gives predictable latency at runtime. Because surrogate and LM tokens are interchangeable, the switch between them could also be controlled by an adaptive trigger; since a trigger brings its own design choices and failure modes that could confound the impact of DRIFT, we leave studying adaptive strategies to future work. To understand why the LM and surrogate paths differ, we visualize the Grad-CAM heatmaps of the reasoning tokens produced by the LM and surrogate paths for an unprotected junction left turn scenario (Fig. 2). The surrogate branch mainly focuses on the ego lane and a few localized cues, such as the traffic light. In contrast, the language reasoning branch shows broader contextual awareness, attending not only to the ego lane but also to semantically relevant cues such as the oncoming vehicle and the pedestrian crossings. These observations suggest that the two reasoning paths provide complementary information. The LM contributes richer scene-level reasoning for complex decisions, while the surrogate emphasizes localized cues that suffice for most planning updates. Consistently, the gain of RR=2 concen-trates in negotiation scenarios, e.g., unprotected left turns (DS 84.5 without LM vs. 96.9 at RR=2 on 15 routes). Re-freshing language reasoning at a moderate rate thus lets DRIFT benefit from semantic reasoning without overriding the stable surrogate reasoning at every step. More analysis is in the supplementary.

Laterally, both variants agree equally (85.2 vs. 85.0 and 89.4 vs. 88.9), as lateral decisions are largely determined by the road geometry [46]. While both benchmarks show similar trends, the numbers should not be compared directly, as their evaluation protocols differ. Lat-eral agreement is a coarse measure, since several descrip-tions can be correct for the same situation, so 85% reflects a limit of the evaluation rather than low alignment.

5. Conclusion 

In this paper, we argue that VLM-based E2E driving mod-els become more practical by following two design choices: compactness and detachability. To this end, we introduce DRIFT, which shows that rich intermediate driving features enable effective visual question answering and action com-mentary with a compact LM of under 150M parameters, connected through a simple per-token projector. A surro-gate reasoning module trained via knowledge distillation allows the LM to be detached at inference without sacri-ficing driving performance. On Bench2Drive, DRIFT with LM reasoning reaches up to 97.03 DS and 89.39% SR with 8about 4× fewer parameters than the strongest language-based model. Our analysis shows that VQA training can help encode additional features in the driving backbone. It further shows that linking the planner to the LM’s reasoning at inference improves the alignment between the generated language and the planned speed, compared to a decoupled variant. Moreover, benchmarking DRIFT on NAVSIM and AlpaSim shows competitive driving quality with a drasti-cally smaller footprint.

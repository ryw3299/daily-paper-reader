# Security of Deep Research Agents: Attack Surface, Defenses, Benchmarks, and Agenda Seeding against Research Planning 

Yang Luo, Guangpu Chen, Yichen Liu, Zhaoyu Chen, Yifeng Gao , Zhiyang Ding , Qingni Shen , Zhonghai Wu *

Peking University 

> {luoyang,qingnishen,wuzh}@pku.edu.cn {chenguangpu,liuyichen,2001210212,yfgao25,dingzhiyang26}@stu.pku.edu.cn

Abstract 

Deep research and agentic search agents au-tonomously retrieve content from the open web and generate reports with citations, and their outputs are often taken directly as research con-clusions. This paper systematizes security re-search in this area along four stages, namely retrieval, planning, evidence integration, and report generation. We present a unified threat model, a taxonomy of attacks and defenses, and a review of evaluation benchmarks, and we align the area dimension by dimension with retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) poison-ing, prompt injection, and generative engine op-timization (GEO). Coding 34 primary studies shows that only 26.5% of them involve the plan-ning stage, and none performs an end-to-end poisoning evaluation on commercial products. To address this gap, we propose the agenda seeding attack, in which the attacker writes no instructions and only introduces a fictitious evaluation metric. Across 3,520 sandboxed re-search runs under the same budget, GEO and prompt injection are nearly ineffective, whereas agenda seeding makes the attacker’s target the top pick in 16% to 28% of reports. Injection detection and query anchoring fail against it, verification prompting hurts utility, and our pro-posed provenance tagging preserves utility but can be bypassed by multi-site bait. Finally, we propose eight research directions. 

1 Introduction 

Starting from WebGPT (Nakano et al., 2021) and ReAct (Yao et al., 2023), large language mod-els (LLMs) have been allowed to call search en-gines and read web pages during reasoning. Sub-sequently, Search-o1 (Li et al., 2025c) embedded retrieval into the thinking process of reasoning mod-els, and works such as Search-R1 (Jin et al., 2025), DeepResearcher (Zheng et al., 2025), WebThinker (Li et al., 2025d), WebSailor (Li et al., 2025b),

and WebDancer (Wu et al., 2025b) further used reinforcement learning to train models to decide autonomously when to search, what to search for, and how deeply to read. On the product side, Ope-nAI Deep Research (OpenAI, 2025), Gemini Deep Research (Google, 2024), and Perplexity Deep Re-search (Perplexity, 2025) can visit hundreds of web pages within minutes to tens of minutes and output structured research reports with citations. The academic community has also open-sourced frameworks such as STORM (Shao et al., 2024), Co-STORM (Jiang et al., 2024), OmniThink (Xi et al., 2025b), and MindSearch (Chen et al., 2025c). We refer to such systems collectively as deep re-search and agentic search agents, abbreviated as DR agents in what follows. The value of a DR agent lies in reading, on the user’s behalf, a large number of web pages that the user would never read, which also means that anyone who can place content into its retrieval results can influence a report that the user treats as a research conclusion. Several primary studies since 2025 have made this risk concrete. Agent-Fence (Puppala et al., 2026) organizes 14 attack classes by trust boundary and finds, on 8 deep agent archetypes, that wallet draining, authorization con-fusion, retrieval poisoning, and planning manipu-lation pose the highest risks. WARP (Zhang et al., 2026b) rewrites only about 13 words on a single frequently retrieved user-generated content (UGC) page, which causes the page to be retrieved in 57% to 76% of research runs and cited in 38% to 51% of reports. HAE-GEO (Bi et al., 2026) constructs hierarchical web evidence poisoning with three pro-gressive levels and finds that fabricated mutual cor-roboration weakens the agent’s ability to recognize suspicious evidence. A jailbreak study targeting deep research (Chen et al., 2026a) shows that harm-ful requests that would be refused on their own can, after plan injection or reframing as academic research, yield harmful reports that are more struc-1arXiv:2611.20045v1 [cs.CR] 15 Nov 2026 tured and more actionable than those produced in ordinary chat. However, these works are scattered across secu-rity, natural language processing, information re-trieval, and machine learning venues and preprints, and they use different terminology, threat models, and metrics. Existing surveys on search agents and deep research (Xi et al., 2025a; Zhang et al., 2025c; Huang et al., 2025; Xu and Peng, 2025; Li et al., 2025e) systematically review paradigms, op-timization, and evaluation, but they focus only on capability. Existing agent security surveys (Deng et al., 2025; He et al., 2025, 2026; Gan et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2025; Kim et al., 2026; Chu, 2026; Ling et al., 2026) organize threats by model, memory, tools, and environment and treat retrieval as one of many untrusted input channels. They charac-terize neither the open-web evidence ecosystem (publishers, UGC platforms, ranking, and search services) nor the risks that arise when reports are taken directly as conclusions. In adjacent areas, retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) poisoning (Zou et al., 2025; Zhong et al., 2023), prompt injec-tion (Greshake et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2024), and generative engine optimization (GEO) (Aggarwal et al., 2024; Nestaas et al., 2025) have each accumu-lated many results, but whether their threat models transfer to multi-round, open research pipelines in which queries are autonomously rewritten has not been systematically answered. This paper systematizes security research on DR agents and conducts a controlled experiment tar-geting a gap identified during the systematization. The paper is organized around four stages, namely retrieval, planning, evidence integration, and report generation, and answers five research questions (RQs). RQ1: What are the attack surface and threat model of DR agents? RQ2: How can existing at-tacks and defenses be categorized by stage, and what assumptions does each rely on? RQ3: What do existing benchmarks measure and what do they miss, and how do they relate to RAG poisoning, prompt injection, and GEO? RQ4: What research gaps does the overall distribution of the literature reveal? RQ5: At the planning stage, which litera-ture coding shows to be the most neglected, can an attacker who uses no instructions and only changes the comparison criteria change the conclusions of a DR agent, and can existing defenses at each layer stop it? Our contributions are as follows: • We present a four-stage system model and a unified threat model for DR agents, includ-ing five trust boundaries, six attacker capabil-ity levels (C0 to C5, with the supply-chain attacker C6 listed separately as a gap), and six classes of security goals, and we explain how its threat assumptions differ from those of RAG, Web agents, and ordinary search agents. • We build a stage-organized attack taxonomy (4 stages, 19 attack classes) and a defense tax-onomy (5 layers), give for each defense class the assumptions it relies on and its known fail-ure conditions, and review the coverage and metrics of 9 security benchmarks. • We align DR agent security dimension by di-mension with RAG poisoning, prompt injec-tion, GEO, and classical search engine poi-soning, and identify which conclusions trans-fer and which assumptions break down in re-search pipelines, in particular that instruction-free data-level manipulation bypasses de-fenses centered on instruction detection. • We code 34 primary studies along six dimen-sions and quantify the skew of the literature in stages, attacker capabilities, attack goals, evaluation environments, and defense evalua-tion. On this basis, we propose eight research gaps and future directions and explain how this paper differs from existing agent security surveys. • We propose the agenda seeding attack and conduct 3,520 paired research runs in a re-producible offline sandbox. Under the same budget, it is significantly stronger than GEO and prompt injection, injection detection and root-query anchoring fail against it, and veri-fication prompting can lower its success rate only at a clear cost in utility. We also propose provenance tagging as a lightweight defense that does not degrade research quality, and we use an adaptive attack to expose its limita-tions. The rest of the paper is organized as follows: Section 2 introduces the pipeline and system land-scape of DR agents, Section 3 describes literature collection and coding, Section 4 presents the threat model, Sections 5 and 6 present the attack and defense taxonomies, Section 7 reviews evaluation benchmarks, Section 8 establishes connections to adjacent areas, Section 9 reports literature coding 2statistics, Section 10 reports the agenda seeding experiment, Section 11 proposes research gaps and directions, Section 12 explains the differences from existing surveys, and Section 13 concludes. 

2 Background 

2.1 The Research Pipeline 

A DR agent receives an open-ended research ques-tion and, after multiple rounds of planning, re-trieval, and integration, generates a report. Imple-mentations vary widely across systems, but all of them can be abstracted into the four stages in Fig-ure 1. An existing capability survey (Zhang et al., 2025c) adopts a division into planning, question generation, web exploration, and report generation. We merge question generation into planning and split web exploration into retrieval and evidence in-tegration, because from a security perspective, what content is fetched and what content is believed are two different kinds of failure. 

Retrieval. The agent issues queries to a search engine or search API, obtains the result list, and then selectively fetches web pages, PDFs, or UGC. Retrieval can be one-shot (as in ordinary search-augmented question answering (Vu et al., 2024)) or repeated tens to hundreds of times within a sin-gle research session. The retrieval stage spans the widest trust boundary between the agent and the outside world: search ranking is determined by third parties, web content is determined by any publisher, and fetched content enters the model context directly. 

Planning. The idea of interleaving retrieval and reasoning can be traced back to Self-Ask (Press et al., 2023), IRCoT (Trivedi et al., 2023), and Tool-former (Schick et al., 2023). In a DR agent, the planner decomposes the research question into sub-questions, generates queries for each sub-question, and decides the next step based on what has been read: following up, switching perspectives, dig-ging into a particular source, or stopping. STORM (Shao et al., 2024) organizes research through multi-perspective questioning, MindSearch (Chen et al., 2025c) organizes sub-questions as a directed graph, and “Towards AI Search Paradigm” (Li et al., 2025e) further splits planning, execution, and writing across multiple collaborating agents. Search agents trained with reinforcement learning (Jin et al., 2025; Zheng et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025b) make planning implicit in the reasoning trajectory. What makes planning special is that it depends on data: subsequent queries are determined by previ-ously read content, so retrieved content can in turn control the direction of research. 

Evidence integration. The agent filters and deduplicates the retrieved evidence, assesses its credibility, resolves conflicts, and extracts key points. This stage determines which claims en-ter the conclusions. Most existing systems leave credibility judgments to the model, relying on its implicit judgments of source authority, consis-tency, and timeliness (Wang et al., 2025). On the open web, evidence often conflicts, and agreement among multiple sources does not necessarily imply truth. 

Report generation. The agent writes the inte-grated evidence into a long, sectioned report with citations. Citations make reports appear verifiable, but prior work has found that only about half of the sentences in generative search engine responses are fully supported by their citations (Liu et al., 2023), and generation with citations remains an open prob-lem (Gao et al., 2023a). The report is used directly by users, downstream agents, or decision processes and is the only outlet through which a DR agent affects the outside world.

Scope. The security research included in this paper targets LLM-driven systems that can autonomously initiate web retrieval and generate answers or re-ports based on the retrieval results, and covers attacks, defenses, benchmarks, and measurement studies. Pure capability studies, studies targeting only RAG over closed knowledge bases, and stud-ies targeting only Web agents that perform web operations are not counted in the core corpus, but they are included as adjacent work in the alignment analysis of Section 8. Studies on manipulating gen-erative engines (LLM ranking and recommendation systems with retrieval) are counted in the core cor-pus, because they attack the evidence integration and generation stages of DR agents. 

Sources and search. We searched the four secu-rity conferences USENIX Security, IEEE S&P, ACM CCS, and NDSS, other venues including ACL, EMNLP, NAACL, NeurIPS, ICLR, ICML, COLM, and KDD, and the arXiv categories cs.CR, cs.CL, cs.IR, and cs.AI, covering January 2023 to September 2026. Keyword combinations paired deep research, search agent, generative engine, and web agent with poisoning, injection, jailbreak, manipulation, leakage, and safety, and we then per-formed forward and backward snowballing on the matched papers. Since many results in this area are still published as preprints, we included arXiv papers. 

Inclusion and exclusion. The inclusion crite-ria are: (i) the subject of study falls within the above scope, (ii) the work contains an explicit threat model or security goal, and (iii) the work reports empirical results (attack effectiveness, de-fense effectiveness, or measurement data). We ex-clude opinion pieces without empirical results, ven-dor blogs, and news reports. This yields 34 primary studies, plus 88 adjacent works on attacks and de-fenses, capability systems, and surveys, which are used for the alignment analysis in Section 8 and for background. 

Coding scheme. We code six dimensions for each primary study. (1) Type: attack (A), defense (D), benchmark or evaluation framework (B), or measurement study (M), according to the main contribution. (2) Stages involved: retrieval (R), planning (P), evidence integration (I), and report generation (G), multi-label, determined by where the attacker exerts influence or where the defense acts. (3) Attacker capability: C0 to C5, defined in Section 4. (4) Attack goal: integrity of evidence and conclusions (Int), targeted promotion and rank-ing manipulation (Pro), harmful content generation (Harm), task or tool hijacking (Hij), confidential-ity (Conf), and availability and cost (Avail), multi-label. (5) Target system: deep research framework or product (DR), search agent (SA), generative en-gine (GE), or retrieval-augmented pipeline (RAG). (6) Evaluation environment: the real web (Live), a sandbox that inserts controlled content into real search results (Sandbox), or an offline or simulated corpus (Sim), together with whether defenses were evaluated. 

4 System and Threat Model 

4.1 Trust Boundaries 

Figure 1 marks the five trust boundaries of a DR agent. B1 is the boundary between the user and the agent, and the user’s research request may itself be malicious. B2 is the boundary between the agent and search engines and web pages. Neither web content nor ranking is under the agent’s control, and this is the widest and most studied boundary. B3 is the boundary where search result slots and ex-ternal observers sit: ad slots, compromised search proxies, or third-party search APIs can insert or replace results, and search service providers and network observers can see all queries the agent issues. B4 is the feedback boundary between evi-dence integration and planning, namely the loop in which content already read determines subsequent queries and subtasks, and in multi-agent systems it also includes delegation and shared memory among sub-agents (Gadgil et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2024). B5 is the boundary between the report and its con-sumers, where the report is used as a conclusion by humans or downstream agents. 

4.2 Security Goals 

We group the security goals of DR agents into six classes. Integrity of evidence and conclusions : fac-tual conclusions in the report should be supported by credible evidence and should not be swayed by claims planted by attackers. Fairness of ranking and recommendation : in comparison and recom-mendation research, the appearance and ranking of entities should not be determined by adversarial optimization by content owners. This is a special case of integrity in commercial settings, listed sep-arately because of its distinctive incentive structure. 

Safety : the agent should not turn harmful requests 5into actionable harmful reports. Control-flow in-tegrity : the agent’s planning and tool calls should follow user intent and should not be hijacked by retrieved content. Confidentiality : the user’s re-search intent, local private documents, and session data should not leak through queries, requests, or reports. Availability and cost : research should not be induced into unbounded loops or into consum-ing computation and API costs far beyond what the task requires. 

4.3 Attacker Capabilities 

Table 2 lists attacker levels from weakest to strongest, and Figure 1 marks where each of them acts. C1 is the most common assumption: the at-tacker can publish or control their own web pages but does not know the user’s specific queries and cannot control search ranking. C2 can edit exist-ing, frequently retrieved third-party pages, such as forum comments, Q&A answers, or encyclope-dia entries. The key observation of WARP (Zhang et al., 2026b) is that such pages already enjoy a ranking advantage, so the attacker does not need to compete with the search engine. C3 can insert en-tries directly into search results, corresponding to ad slots, compromised search proxies, or malicious search tools. C4 is a malicious user who submits re-quests directly to the agent. C5 is a passive observer who can see the agent’s outbound queries or net-work traffic. C0 denotes natural failures without an active attacker, such as reference fabrication (Rao et al., 2026), which causes the same harm as attacks and shares defenses with them, so we include it as well. There is also a stronger class of attacker, C6, namely supply-chain attackers who control search APIs, crawling services, tool servers, or sub-agents. No study in the core corpus addresses it, and we list it as a gap in Section 11. 

Knowledge assumptions. Unlike the white-box setting common in RAG poisoning (Zou et al., 2025; Zhong et al., 2023), realistic attackers in DR scenarios usually know neither the user’s spe-cific queries nor the model and prompts used by the agent, and they cannot access retriever parameters. WARP (Zhang et al., 2026b) explicitly adopts this weakest knowledge assumption, and KidnapRAG (Choi et al., 2026a) and Breadcrumbing (Li et al., 2026b) adopt black-box settings. We argue that evaluations of DR attacks should report the knowl-edge assumption together with the capability level, otherwise attack success rates across works are not comparable.

UGC and third-party page edits. WARP (Zhang et al., 2026b) does not publish new doc-uments but instead makes minimal modifications to UGC pages, such as forums and Q&A sites, that DR agents already retrieve frequently, and the attacker neither controls retrieval nor knows the model, prompts, or user queries. The authors perform end-to-end evaluation on the open-source STORM, Co-STORM, and OmniThink using a simulation framework that intercepts the retrieval pipeline, and a single modification of about 13 words is retrieved in 57% to 76% of runs and cited in 38% to 51% of reports. For OpenAI and Gemini Deep Research, the authors only analyze retrieval and citation patterns without performing end-to-end attacks. SIREN (Caville et al., 2026) iteratively rewrites retrieved web pages in the style of PAIR (Chao et al., 2025) to push a designated entity to first place in recommendation results. An earlier study of commercial agents (Li et al., 2025a) like-wise used forum posts to lead agents to malicious websites. Such attacks borrow the retrieval ranking and credibility of existing pages, and defenses at the C1 level (for example, filtering new domains) are ineffective against them. 

Result-slot injection. SafeSearch (Dong et al., 2026) inserts one LLM-generated unreliable web-site into real search results so that all tested agents see the same evidence, and evaluates three classes of scaffolds, namely search workflows, tool calling, and deep research, on 300 test cases across 5 risk categories. The highest attack success rate reaches 90.5%, and misinformation that is hard to verify is the most dangerous. Breadcrumbing (Li et al., 2026b) points out that modern search agents follow up and cross-check, so a single injected page is often diluted, but if the attacker can append one controlled entry to the results of every query and co-ordinate this evidence across the whole trajectory, the attack success rate rises significantly. There-fore, for multi-round agents, attacker capability should be measured by the number of entries that can be injected per round rather than by the total amount injected. 

Prompt injection in retrieved content. Indirect prompt injection (Greshake et al., 2023) has a direct counterpart in search settings. Nestaas et al. (2025) manipulate the recommendations of LLM search systems simply by embedding instructions in third-party web pages, and point out that all content owners have an incentive to do so, which creates a prisoner’s dilemma. Khodayari et al. (2026) con-duct a large-scale measurement of indirect prompt injection in real web pages and find that most of it is invisible to human readers. Chang et al. (2026) push injected content past the retrieval barrier us-ing only the API of an embedding model. Rall et al. (2025) demonstrate that a malicious website can, through hidden instructions, make an agent with search tools retrieve sensitive enterprise data and send it to the attacker’s server via network re-quests. Compared with environmental injection on Web agents (Liao et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025d; Wu et al., 2025a), the injection payload against DR agents does not need to induce a click, it only needs to be read. 

Agent-targeted cloaking. Cloaking techniques in classical search engine poisoning return differ-ent content to crawlers and to humans (Wang et al., 2011; John et al., 2011; Leontiadis et al., 2011). The fetchers of DR agents likewise have identi-fiable request features, so an attacker can show poisoned content only to the agent, and humans reviewing the report’s citations will then see clean pages. No work in the core corpus specifically studies this attack. 

Query and traffic leakage. The retrieval stage is also an outlet for confidentiality breaches. Mo-saicLeaks (Gurung et al., 2026) constructs 1,001 multi-hop research tasks that link private enter-prise documents with the public web. An adversary model that sees only query logs can infer research intent, answers to private questions, and claims in the documents, and agents with better task perfor-mance tend to pack more context into their queries and thus leak more. Jeong et al. (2026) further show that even without seeing query content, the sequence of visited domains and packet metadata suffice to infer the hidden prompts and user charac-teristics of local research agents. 

5.2 Planning-Stage Attacks Plan injection and intent hijacking. A jailbreak study targeting deep research (Chen et al., 2026a) proposes two attacks: plan injection directly ma-nipulates the agent’s execution plan, and intent hijacking rewrites harmful requests as academic research questions. Unlike jailbreaks that require optimization, such as GCG (Zou et al., 2023) and PAIR (Chao et al., 2025), both require only natural-8language rewriting. The authors find that the re-searcher or planner role setting and multi-step ex-ecution weaken the alignment of the underlying model, requests that would be refused on their own receive near-complete compliance, and the reports are more structured and contain more technical de-tail than ordinary answers. This is consistent with jailbreak results on browser agents (Kumar et al., 2024) and with the findings of AgentHarm (An-driushchenko et al., 2025): refusal behavior trained for chat is diluted once models act as agents. 

Research-trajectory hijacking. Subsequent queries depend on previously read content, so ad-versarial documents retrieved early can change the entire research trajectory. FORGE (Pan et al., 2026) studies this planning-level attack, in which adver-sarial documents steer subsequent subtasks and contamination spreads recursively to the final re-port. KidnapRAG (Choi et al., 2026a) achieves hijacking on agentic RAG with documents in three roles: Bait documents attract the first retrieval, Chain-Link documents induce query rewriting, and Mal-Ins documents provide attacker-controlled ev-idence. Unlike retrieval-stage poisoning, such at-tacks do not require the attacker’s page to be rele-vant to the user’s original question, only that it can be fetched by some intermediate query. 

Cost amplification and wallet draining. In the evaluation of Agent-Fence (Puppala et al., 2026), the highest-risk class is wallet draining. DR agents are billed by the number of calls and tokens, and a single research session is already expensive. Over-Think (Kumar et al., 2025) injects decoy problems into retrieved content to inflate the thinking tokens of reasoning models, and Breaking Agents (Zhang et al., 2025a) induces agents into repetitive or mal-functioning action loops. For a DR agent, being induced to research a direction more deeply is se-mantically perfectly reasonable, so this is harder to detect than for ordinary agents. 

Alignment erosion. Yang et al. (2026) find that training search agents with reinforcement learning weakens the safety behaviors acquired during in-struction tuning. SafeSearch (Zhan et al., 2026) reports that search agents produce harmful outputs more often than their base models, and proposes using reinforcement learning to jointly optimize safety and utility. Such risks do not require an external attacker to plant content but instead stem from the training process for research capability itself. 

5.3 Evidence Integration Attacks Exploiting credibility and authority. DR agents tend to cite authoritative-looking sources, but au-thoritative sources may also be easy to inject into. Mochizuki et al. (2025) estimate the difficulty of content injection from publisher attributes of cited sources, and find on political topics in Japan and the United States that the proportion of primary sources in answers differs markedly between the two countries and that sources with low injection barriers are frequently cited. AuthRank (Jia et al., 2025) counters poisoning in an LLM pipeline for threat intelligence with PageRank-like authority scores, but it assumes that sources with high au-thority scores are hard to pollute, an assumption that the C2 attacks of WARP (Zhang et al., 2026b) and SIREN (Caville et al., 2026) break. 

Corroboration trap. Cross-checking is the most natural defensive intuition for DR agents and has therefore become an attack target. The evalu-ation of 10 agents in HAE-GEO (Bi et al., 2026) finds that when multiple poisoned pages corrob-orate each other, the agents’ ability to recognize suspicious evidence declines. Agentic search im-proves the final resistance rate but improves neither evidence recognition nor utility. Defensive prompts increase verification behavior but rarely translate into correction of claims already accepted. Bread-crumbing (Li et al., 2026b) constructs the same coordinated corroboration at the result-slot level. 

Factual distraction and misleading knowl-edge. Manipulation does not necessarily require false content. Lazy Grounding (Zhang et al., 2026c) shows that entirely true but distracting evidence lowers the accuracy of search agents by 5.9 per-centage points on average. Zhu et al. (2026) study how misleading knowledge induces deep research to reach wrong conclusions, and Topic-FlipRAG (Gong et al., 2025) manipulates the opinion stance of RAG outputs at the topic level. Defenses based on detecting false content or detecting instructions cannot discover such attacks. 

Ranking manipulation and GEO. GEO (Ag-garwal et al., 2024) was the first to formalize con-tent optimization for generative engines, reporting that the visibility of content in generated answers can be increased by up to 40%. Subsequent re-search pushed it in an adversarial direction. Ku-mar and Lakkaraju (2024) add strategic text se-quences to product pages to raise LLM recommen-dation rankings, Pfrommer et al. (2024) study rank-9ing manipulation of conversational search engines, StealthRank (Tang et al., 2025) seeks a balance between effectiveness and stealthiness, CORE (Jin et al., 2026) controls the output ranking of genera-tive engines, and GEO-Bench (Nimase et al., 2026) evaluates black-box and gradient-based ranking ma-nipulation uniformly on 5 datasets. Most of these works are conducted in single-round ranking set-tings and have not yet considered the multi-round retrieval and cross-checking of DR agents. 

5.4 Report Generation Attacks Harmful long-form synthesis. DR agents in-tegrate scattered retrieved information into doc-uments with clear steps and complete citations, which makes their harm under harmful requests greater than that of ordinary chat (Chen et al., 2026a). Safety evaluation for ordinary chat only checks whether the model refuses, whereas in DR settings the actionability of the report and the de-gree of information integration matter more, and existing metrics do not yet capture them well. 

Citation laundering. Citations in a report are supposed to let readers verify claims, but when the cited source is poisoned, the citation instead endorses it. WARP (Zhang et al., 2026b) uses the proportion of final reports that cite the poisoned source as its core metric, which measures exactly this effect. A report contains dozens of citations and the probability that a reader checks any par-ticular one is low, so a polluted sub-conclusion can gain credibility from the many correct citations around it. The measurements of Liu et al. (2023) show that even without attacks, only about half of the sentences produced by generative search en-gines are fully supported by their citations, which provides cover for citation laundering. 

Reference fabrication. Rao et al. (2026) detect and correct reference hallucinations in commercial LLMs and deep research agents. Reference fabrica-tion requires no attacker, but like citation launder-ing it undermines the verifiability of reports, and the two are hard to distinguish at the report level: a reader cannot tell whether a citation that cannot be opened was fabricated, was deleted, or points to a page that served cloaked content to the agent. 

Output-channel exfiltration. Links, images, and requests in reports and answers can carry data out. Imprompter (Fu et al., 2024) constructs obfus-cated prompts that induce agents to generate image links carrying private data, and Rall et al. (2025) directly use search tools to issue outbound requests. Reports of DR agents are usually rendered in web pages or documents, and any automatically loaded external resource is a potential exfiltration channel. 

5.5 Cross-Stage Propagation 

The four stages influence each other, and we iden-tify three cross-stage propagation patterns. The first is feedback from retrieval to planning: once poisoned content is read, it can expand its own in-fluence by affecting subsequent queries, which is what FORGE (Pan et al., 2026) and KidnapRAG (Choi et al., 2026a) exploit. The second is ampli-fication from integration to generation: accepted claims are restated multiple times in the report and receive citations. The gap between the retrieval rate and the citation rate in WARP (Zhang et al., 2026b) reflects the filtering effect of the integration stage, and also reflects that content is amplified once it passes the filter. The third is diffusion from the report to its consumers: after a report is read by other agents or published on the web, it becomes corpus for the next round of retrieval, forming a self-reinforcing pollution loop, which is consistent with concerns about LLM-generated content pol-luting the web (Pan et al., 2023). The 14 attack classes of Agent-Fence (Puppala et al., 2026) cover prompt and state injection, planning manipulation, tool-use hijacking, retrieval and search poisoning, delegation abuse, authorization confusion, wallet draining, and more. It is organized by trust bound-ary, whereas this paper is organized by research stage, and the two are complementary: the same class of trust-boundary attack differs greatly in de-tectability and harm when it takes effect at different stages.

The fundamental limita-tion of source-level defenses is that they use pub-lisher identity as a proxy for content credibility, whereas C2 attackers borrow precisely someone else’s publishing identity. 

6.2 Content-Level Defenses 

Content-level defenses inspect the text entering the context. The mainstream approach to prompt injec-tion defense is to separate instructions from data: Spotlighting (Hines et al., 2024) marks the bound-aries of external data, StruQ (Chen et al., 2025a) and SecAlign (Chen et al., 2025b) train models to execute only instructions in the instruction chan-nel, the instruction hierarchy (Wallace et al., 2024) assigns priorities to instructions from different sources, and DataSentinel (Liu et al., 2025) and MELON (Zhu et al., 2025) detect injections. These defenses are ineffective against a large class of at-tacks in DR settings: the 13 words of WARP, the true evidence of Lazy Grounding, and the persua-sive rewrites of GEO contain no instructions at all. Surface-level anomaly detection is equally unreli-able. SCI-Defense (Yu et al., 2026) reports that ex-isting defenses such as perplexity filtering, content safety classifiers, and paraphrasing have zero recall against semantic-level manipulation, and WARP also finds that lightweight anomaly detection on in-puts and outputs cannot reliably identify poisoned content (Zhang et al., 2026b). Moreover, adaptive attacks have repeatedly broken prompt injection de-fenses (Zhan et al., 2025; Nasr et al., 2026), and Ab-delnabi and Bagdasarian (2026) argue that agents may always remain susceptible to prompt injection. 

6.3 Integration-Level Defenses 

Integration-level defenses change how the agent processes multiple pieces of evidence. RobustRAG (Xiang et al., 2024) first generates an answer from each retrieval result separately and then performs secure aggregation, providing certifiable robustness when the number of polluted passages is bounded. TrustRAG (Zhou et al., 2025), InstructRAG (Wei et al., 2025b), and Astute RAG (Wang et al., 2025) improve robustness through clustering-based fil-tering, self-generated rationales, and resolution of conflicts between internal and external knowledge, respectively. These methods implicitly assume that most retrieval results are benign, and the corrobo-ration trap (Bi et al., 2026) and per-round injection (Li et al., 2026b) are designed precisely against this assumption. Their guarantees also cover only single-round retrieval, not multi-hop research in which the evidence of one round determines the queries of the next. The integration-level defense actually evaluated on DR agents is mainly veri-fication prompting (Dong et al., 2026; Bi et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2026b), and HAE-GEO con-cludes that such prompts increase verification ac-tions but rarely translate into corrections of conclu-sions. 

6.4 Planning-Level and System-Level Defenses 

FORGE (Pan et al., 2026) proposes root-query an-choring, which constrains recursively generated follow-up queries to stay close to the original ques-tion, and reduces the infection rate from 38.5% to 18.3% on a defense subset of 10 queries. Works such as DeepResearchGuard (Huang et al., 2026) place guardrails at multiple stages, including input, planning, retrieval, and output. Privilege control and information flow methods from general agent security, such as CaMeL (Debenedetti et al., 2026), FIDES (Costa et al., 2026), Progent (Shi et al., 2025), IsolateGPT (Wu et al., 2025c), and design patterns (Beurer-Kellner et al., 2025), prevent hi-jacking by fixing the control flow before untrusted data is read. However, the value of a DR agent lies in adjusting its plan according to what it reads, so these methods can only protect side-effecting actions outside research (such as sending reports or calling tools with write operations) and cannot protect the direction of research. For query pri-vacy, MosaicLeaks (Gurung et al., 2026) proposes using reinforcement learning to penalize queries that increase leakage, which is currently the only training-time defense for DR query privacy. 

6.5 Generation-Level Defenses 

Generation-level defenses act on the final output. SafeSearch (Zhan et al., 2026) aligns search agents for both safety and utility with reinforcement learn-ing, Luo et al. (2025) propose agent-based filtering after measuring the safety risks of AI search en-gines, and GEO Defender (Li et al., 2026a) covers the whole pipeline from retrieval to generation to counter malicious GEO. Citation verification is the most natural generation-level defense for DR re-ports. ALCE (Gao et al., 2023a) and verifiability measurement (Liu et al., 2023) provide evaluation 12 methods, and Rao et al. (2026) provide detection and correction tools. However, citation verification can only confirm that the report is consistent with its sources, not that the source content is true, so it is powerless against citation laundering. 

6.6 Lessons 

Existing defenses have three structural problems. First, the assumptions of defenses and attacks are misaligned: content-level defenses assume that at-tacks carry instructions, integration-level defenses assume that most evidence is benign, and source-level defenses assume that authoritative sources are hard to pollute, while representative attacks in DR settings break each of these three assumptions. Sec-ond, defense composition across stages is missing: most existing works deploy defenses at only one stage, yet attacks propagate across stages. Third, adaptive evaluation is missing: in the prompt in-jection area, defenses have repeatedly been broken by adaptive attacks (Zhan et al., 2025; Nasr et al., 2026), and DR defenses have not yet undergone such systematic testing. 

7 Benchmarks and Evaluation 

Table 5 lists evaluation benchmarks directly rele-vant to DR agent security and compares them in five aspects: target system, stages covered, threat type, environment, and metrics. 

Coverage. Only Agent-Fence covers all four stages, but it is organized by trust boundary and has no metrics targeting research quality. HAE-GEO and the red-teaming version of SafeSearch cover retrieval and integration, the alignment version of SafeSearch and the jailbreak study cover planning and generation, and MosaicLeaks is the only con-fidentiality benchmark. General prompt injection benchmarks such as AgentDojo (Debenedetti et al., 2024), InjecAgent (Zhan et al., 2024), ASB (Zhang et al., 2025b), and WASP (Evtimov et al., 2025) can evaluate injection in retrieved content, but their tasks center on tool calls and include neither long-form research nor reports. 

Metrics. Existing metrics fall into three classes. Outcome metrics measure the final output, such as attack success rate, the citation rate of poisoned sources (Zhang et al., 2026b), harmfulness scores (Chen et al., 2026a), and rank improvement (Ni-mase et al., 2026). Process metrics measure the research trajectory, such as the complete chain in HAE-GEO from first contact with poisoned content through recognition, verification, correction, and recovery (Bi et al., 2026), and the infection rate of FORGE (Pan et al., 2026). WASP distinguishes intermediate attack success rate from end-to-end attack success rate (Evtimov et al., 2025), and this distinction matters equally for DR: an agent read-ing and citing poisoned content is different from its final conclusion being changed. Cost metrics mea-sure the extra overhead caused by attacks, such as the extra calls and tokens of wallet-draining attacks, and currently only Agent-Fence includes them. We recommend that DR security evaluations report all three classes of metrics and provide research qual-ity without attack as a utility baseline. 

Environment and reproducibility. Evaluation on the real web is closest to practice but not repro-ducible, and end-to-end poisoning of commercial products would affect real users and faces ethical obstacles. That WARP could only analyze retrieval patterns on commercial products reflects this obsta-cle (Zhang et al., 2026b). Simulated corpora are re-producible, but corpus size, ranking algorithm, and page distribution significantly affect the retrieval barrier, so conclusions across works are hard to compare. SafeSearch adds controlled injections to real results, which is a compromise (Dong et al., 2026). We argue that the community needs frozen web snapshots, swappable ranking modules, and a unified poisoning injection interface so that at-tacks and defenses can be compared in the same environment.

Three as-sumptions break down when these results are trans-ferred to DR. The first assumption is that the target query is known, whereas DR agent queries are gen-erated by the planner and continually rewritten, so the attacker knows at most the topic. The sec-ond assumption is that the retriever is accessible, whereas open-web ranking is determined by com-mercial search engines and the attacker cannot com-pute gradients through it. The third assumption is single-round retrieval, whereas the multi-round re-trieval of DR agents both dilutes a single poisoned document (the observation of Breadcrumbing (Li et al., 2026b)) and amplifies it once the poison affects planning (the observation of FORGE and KidnapRAG (Pan et al., 2026; Choi et al., 2026a)). Conversely, DR settings also raise new questions for RAG poisoning: WARP shows that editing ex-isting high-ranking pages is more effective than injecting new documents, whereas the RAG poi-soning literature studies almost exclusively the in-jection of new documents. 

Connection to prompt injection. Indirect prompt injection (Greshake et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2024) is a subset of retrieval-stage attacks on DR agents, and its benchmarks (Debenedetti et al., 2024; Zhan et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025b; Evtimov et al., 2025) and defenses (Chen et al., 2025a,b; Debenedetti et al., 2026) can be reused di-rectly. However, we argue that treating DR security as a special case of prompt injection is insufficient. Prompt injection concerns data being executed as instructions, whereas the core task of a DR agent is precisely to draw conclusions from data, so data-level manipulation containing no instructions at all can achieve the attack goal. Choi et al. (2026b) point out that injecting data into agents is itself a realistic threat, and Lazy Grounding (Zhang et al., 2026c) lowers accuracy using only true content. Defenses centered on separating instructions from data have nothing to act on here, and DR security is closer to a problem of information credibility and evidential reasoning. 

Connection to GEO manipulation. GEO (Ag-garwal et al., 2024) originates from the legitimate need of content owners to increase their visibility in generative answers, and as with search engine optimization (SEO), there is a continuum from le-gitimate optimization to malicious manipulation. Nestaas et al. (2025) point out that when all content owners can benefit from manipulation, the system falls into a prisoner’s dilemma, as was also the case with spam and poisoning in classical search engines (John et al., 2011; Leontiadis et al., 2011; Wang et al., 2011). The GEO literature contributes two things to DR security: it provides the eco-nomic motivation and scalable methods of attack-ers (Tang et al., 2025; Jin et al., 2026; Nimase et al., 2026), and it provides defensive ideas for detecting persuasive structures (Yu et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026a). However, GEO research is almost entirely conducted on single-round ranking, and how the multi-round retrieval, cross-checking, and long reports of DR agents change the payoff of GEO has so far received only preliminary answers from WARP and HAE-GEO. 

Connection to Web agent security. Environ-mental injection (Liao et al., 2025), pop-up attacks (Zhang et al., 2025d), and multimodal adversarial examples (Wu et al., 2025a) on Web agents show that the visual and structural information of web pages is also an attack vector. As DR agents be-gin to read screenshots, charts, and PDFs, these multimodal attacks will enter the research pipeline. On the other hand, commercial agents have been shown to be led into dangerous actions by simple social engineering (Li et al., 2025a), and the com-bination of research and execution will turn the report integrity problem of DR into an action safety problem.

F3: Attacker models concentrate on publish-ing new pages. F6: Defenses are few and shallow. Only 5 works (14.7%) are dedicated to proposing defenses. Eight of them (57.1%) rely on prompts or surface-level detection, and the analysis in Section 6 shows that both classes of defense have been clearly by-passed by existing attacks. Among these 14 works, there is almost no adaptive evaluation that treats the defense as known and specifically optimizes the attack against it. 

F7: Shared metrics and environments are lacking. The 34 works use more than ten mutually incompatible success metrics, including attack suc-cess rate, citation rate, endorsement rate, infection rate, rank improvement, harmfulness score, and leakage rate, and their corpora range from a few pages to more than seventy thousand pages, so di-rect comparison across works is almost impossible. 

10 Empirical Study: Agenda Seeding against Research Planning 

Finding F2 in Section 9 points out that the inter-section of the planning stage and the ranking pro-motion goal is empty, and Section 6 argues that defenses centered on instruction detection are in-effective against instruction-free data-level manip-ulation. Both points lack direct evidence in the existing literature, and this section tests them with a controlled experiment. The attack we study is called agenda seeding: the attacker neither writes any instructions nor directly claims that the target product is the best overall, but instead proposes a new evaluation metric in a buying guide whose conclusions agree with the mainstream, claims that this metric is overlooked by most reviews, and sep-arately publishes pages devoted to the metric on which the target product leads. What the attacker changes is the comparison criterion, in the hope that the DR agent will actively look up this met-ric when planning follow-up queries and carry it into its conclusions. The attack combines research-trajectory hijacking and ranking manipulation from Table 3, with attacker capability C1 and attack goal Pro. This section answers RQ5: can agenda seed-ing change the recommendation conclusions of DR agents, is it more effective than GEO and prompt injection under the same budget, and can existing defenses at each layer stop it? 

10.1 Experimental Setup Sandbox and corpus. To control the ground truth, the experiment is conducted in an offline sand-box. We select 20 real consumer product categories (portable power stations, robot vacuums, electric cargo bikes, and others), invent 5 brand models for each category, and preset each product’s scores on 4 general buying criteria. One product leads on 3 of the criteria and serves as the ground-truth answer (best), and the third-ranked product serves as the attack target (target). Brands, models, and metrics are all fictitious, so the model’s parametric knowledge cannot answer on its behalf. For each category, Qwen3.7-Plus generates 18 benign web pages according to the score table: 5 single-product reviews, 3 comparison reviews, 4 forum threads, 3 buying guides, and 3 news articles, for a total of 360 pages across the 20 categories, which share one index and serve as distractors for one another. We also build a sparse version in which the target category keeps only 6 benign pages (the single-product reviews of the ground-truth answer and the target product, 1 comparison review, 2 forum threads, and 1 buying guide), simulating long-tail topics with less discussion. The search engine is BM25 (Robertson and Zaragoza, 2009), and each query returns the top 4 results. 

Attack conditions. The three attacks have the same budget, namely 3 new pages on 3 different new domains, and in none of them does the attacker know which queries the agent will issue. (1) GEO: three pages optimized for the target product (an expert review, a comparison review, and a forum thread) that use GEO techniques such as an au-thoritative tone, statistics, and expert quotations (Aggarwal et al., 2024) and claim that the target product is the best overall. (2) GEO plus injection: the same three pages, each with one sentence ap-pended at the end that is addressed to AI assistants and asks them to list the target product as the top 17 Which portable power station should I buy?

Agent under test. We implement an iterative research agent that is isomorphic to open-source systems such as STORM (Shao et al., 2024) and WebThinker (Li et al., 2025d) and loops through planning, retrieval, and integration. The planner first gives 3 initial queries, and in each subsequent round the agent reads the new results, takes notes per source, identifies questions that remain unre-solved, and proposes 2 to 3 follow-up queries, for 3 rounds of retrieval in total. Finally, based on its notes, it writes a report of 300 to 500 words with inline citations and gives the full ranking and the top pick in two lines, “RANKING” and “TOP PICK”. The backend models are Qwen3.8-Flash, DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash, and Kimi-K3, all called through the standard interface of Alibaba Cloud Bailian with thinking mode disabled. For the first two models, each condition is sampled twice per category, and Kimi-K3 is sampled once because of its higher calling cost. For the same category and sample, all model calls before the first appearance of an attack page are identical across conditions (identical prompts reuse the result of the same call), so comparisons between conditions are paired run by run. To test the role of multi-round planning, we also run a single-round version with only 1 round of retrieval and no follow-up queries. 

Metrics. We report the target top-pick rate (the proportion of runs whose TOP PICK is the tar-get product), the best top-pick rate (the propor-tion whose TOP PICK is the ground-truth answer, which measures research quality), the mean target rank (recorded as 6 if the target is absent from the ranking), the proportions of runs in which attack pages are read and cited, the planning hijack rate (the proportion of runs in which at least one query from round 2 onward contains the fictitious metric), and the agenda adoption rate (the proportion of 18 reports that mention the fictitious metric). Differ-ences between conditions are tested with the paired exact McNemar test. 

Defenses. The four defenses correspond to the content, planning, and integration layers of Table 4, plus a lineage-layer method that we propose. (1) Injection detection: Qwen3.8-Flash judges whether each newly fetched page contains text that attempts to command an AI, and flagged pages are discarded. This represents detection-based methods such as DataSentinel (Liu et al., 2025). (2) Root-query anchoring: following FORGE (Pan et al., 2026), we compute the embedding cosine similarity (text-embedding-v4) between each follow-up query and the original question and discard queries below a threshold. The threshold is set to the 10th per-centile of the similarities of all follow-up queries in clean runs, which keeps 90% of follow-up queries in the absence of attack. (3) Verification prompt-ing: following the defensive prompts in SafeSearch (Dong et al., 2026) and HAE-GEO (Bi et al., 2026), the system prompt asks the agent to cross-check sources, to be suspicious of metrics and rankings reported only by a few sources or by promotional sources, and to draw conclusions only from claims corroborated by multiple independent sources. (4) Provenance tagging: if a follow-up query depends on a named concept (an acronym or a capitalized multi-word phrase) that has been mentioned by only one site among the pages read so far, the query is tagged as “elicited by a single source”, pages first fetched through it are labeled as having single-source lineage during note-taking and report writing, and the agent is required not to let criteria or claims supported only by such pages determine the conclusion. The rationale for this design is that pages found by searching for a concept pro-posed by one source will necessarily all mention that concept, so they do not constitute independent corroboration of the concept’s importance. Figure 3 marks where the four defenses act in the research loop and summarizes their effects against agenda seeding.

E2: Research planning is reliably hijacked, but hijacking is not the only path of exposure. 

In 72.0% (dense) and 88.0% (sparse) of agenda seeding runs, the agent actively searched for the fic-titious metric in later rounds. Figure 4(b) shows the step at which each attack stops. GEO and injection pages are read and cited in almost every run, yet the target enters the top two in only 4.5% and 6.0% of runs. We further trace the round in which the attacker’s metric pages are first read: on the dense corpus, 53% of runs read them through follow-up queries and 40% retrieve them directly through generic queries in the first round. With only one round of retrieval (dense corpus, Qwen and DeepSeek), the target top-pick rate drops from 12.5% to 6.2% and the agenda adoption rate drops from 95.0% to 65.0%. The difference in the decline of the best top-pick rate on the sparse corpus is significant ( p = 0 .035 ), and the other differences are not significant at the scale of this experiment. At the category level, removing the bait page shifts the distribution toward 0 overall, and the share of combinations in which the target’s rank improves drops from 75% to 52% (Figure 4(c)). Therefore, multi-round planning and the bait page amplify the attack, but the core of the attack is getting the new criterion into the evidence set, and once the metric pages can be fetched directly by topic queries, planning hijacking is no longer a necessary condition. 

E3: Vulnerability varies by model. Qwen3.8-Flash is flipped in 47.5% of runs on the sparse corpus, Kimi-K3 in 30.0% on both corpora, and DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash in only 2.5% and 7.5%. The three models resist GEO and injection to a similar degree but differ greatly against agenda seeding, in-dicating that recognizing promotional content and judging whether an evaluation criterion is credible are two different abilities. Figure 4(b) locates this difference after the adoption step: DeepSeek writes the fictitious metric into 100% of its reports but ranks the target in the top two in only 7.5% of runs, compared with 48.8% for Qwen and 35.0% for Kimi, meaning that DeepSeek mentions the new criterion but does not let it determine the ranking. Both Qwen and DeepSeek are more vulnerable on the sparse corpus, which is consistent with the ob-servation in Section 5: the less a long-tail topic is discussed, the more easily an attacker can dominate the evidence set. 

E4: Content-level and planning-level defenses cannot stop agenda seeding. Injection detec-tion blocks all pages carrying instructions without falsely blocking any benign page, reduces the tar-get top-pick rate under injection to 0, and reduces the number of runs in which the target enters the top two from 11 to 0 ( p < 0.001 ). But it has no effect at all on agenda seeding: no agenda seeding page is judged to be an injection, the results are identical to those without defense, and the token cost of each research run increases by about 69%. Root-query anchoring reduces the planning hijack rate from 75.6% to 54.4% but does not change the conclusions, and the target top-pick rate remains 20.0%. There are two reasons for this: queries on the fictitious metric are not semantically far from the original question and are hard to separate with a similarity threshold, and the metric pages are often retrieved in the first round. Figure 5(c) quantifies the first point. To block most metric queries, the threshold would have to be so high that it also discards a large number of normal follow-up queries. E5: The integration-level defense is effective but hurts utility, and provenance tagging pre-serves utility but can be bypassed. Verification prompting is the only defense that substantially re-duces attack effectiveness: the target top-pick rate under agenda seeding drops from 20.0% to 8.8% (p < 0.001 ), and for Kimi-K3 from 30.0% to 0. But it makes the model uniformly conservative to-ward all sources: the best top-pick rate without attack drops from 100% to 86.2% (to 76.2% for DeepSeek), and the best top-pick rate under agenda seeding even drops from 73.1% to 66.2%. Figure 5(a) shows where these runs go: under verifica-tion prompting, 12.5% of clean runs and 22.5% of agenda seeding runs end up giving no top pick, whereas under the other defenses a top pick is al-most always given. It lowers the attack success rate mainly by drawing fewer conclusions rather than by drawing correct ones, which is consistent with the finding of HAE-GEO that verification be-havior rarely translates into correction (Bi et al., 2026). Our proposed provenance tagging does not lower the best top-pick rate without attack (which remains 100%, with a false tagging rate of 3.8%), reduces the target top-pick rate under agenda seed-ing from 20.0% to 15.0%, and eliminates 8 flips without introducing any new ones ( p = 0 .008 ). But it takes effect only when the metric is elicited through a single source, and only 21.2% of agenda seeding runs trigger tagging. Against the adaptive attack that places one bait page on each of two domains, the target top-pick rate drops only from 18.8% to 17.5% ( p = 0 .5): by using one more site, the attacker can fake the appearance that multiple sources mention the metric, which is exactly the corroboration trap described in Section 5. Figure 5(b) summarizes the trade-off for each model. Ver-ification prompting gives the largest reduction for every model but pushes DeepSeek far to the left, leaving it with a best top-pick rate without attack of only 76.2%. Provenance tagging keeps the best top-pick rate without attack at 100% for every model and lowers the target top-pick rate for all three mod-els (Qwen from 35.0% to 27.5%, Kimi from 30.0% to 25.0%, and DeepSeek from 5.0% to 2.5%), only by much smaller margins. 

10.3 Implications and Limitations 

This experiment provides direct evidence for three claims made earlier in the paper. The gap at the intersection of the planning stage and the promo-tion goal identified in F2 does not exist because the risk is small: in a research context, changing the comparison criteria is more effective than direct promotion, and the attacker does not need to know any queries. The analysis of misaligned defense assumptions in Section 6 holds, and injection de-tection and query anchoring are largely ineffective against data-level agenda manipulation. Effective defenses need to answer why a criterion matters 22 and who proposed it, and independence judgments based on counting sites are easily faked by multi-site attackers, which pushes the problem toward the planning integrity and evidence independence discussed in G1 and G5. The corpus is generated by a single model, its scale (20 categories with 18 benign pages each) is far smaller than the real web, and the ranker is BM25 rather than a commercial search engine. The agent under test is a structurally simple three-round research pipeline that differs from commer-cial products in retrieval scale, number of queries, and safety mechanisms. Each condition has only 20 to 160 runs, and some ablation differences are not significant at this scale. Therefore, the experi-mental results demonstrate the feasibility of agenda seeding and the failure modes of defenses, not the attack success rate on real systems. 

11 Research Gaps and Future Directions 

We propose eight research gaps and corresponding directions. 

G1: Planning integrity. F2 shows that the plan-ning stage is the least studied, yet it is the core feature that distinguishes DR agents from RAG. A formal definition of research-direction hijack-ing is needed, for example one that measures the shift of the sub-question distribution anchored at the root question, together with mechanisms that constrain planning without sacrificing exploration. The root-query anchoring of FORGE (Pan et al., 2026) is a starting point, but it must answer how legitimate exploration can be distinguished from induced deviation. The experiment in Section 10 shows that anchoring queries by embedding simi-larity can reduce searches for the attacker’s concept but cannot change the conclusions, because the in-troduced criterion does not deviate semantically from the original question. 

G2: Attribution integrity at the report level. 

Existing citation verification only checks whether the report is consistent with its sources. Source provenance attestations, edit histories, and pub-lisher identities need to be incorporated into re-ports so that readers can see the evidence chain behind each conclusion, and the detection of cita-tion laundering and agent-targeted cloaking (Wang et al., 2011) needs to be studied, for example by re-fetching cited pages from an independent vantage point and comparing the results. 

G3: Editing and supply-chain attackers. F3 shows that research on C2 and C3 is scarce and that C6 is entirely absent. DR agents increasingly obtain web pages through third-party search APIs, crawling services, and tool protocols, and these intermediaries can selectively replace content. In-jection targeting tool selection (Shi et al., 2026) also shows that the agent’s choice among multiple search tools is itself an attack surface. Threat mod-els for these intermediaries need to be studied, as do multi-path fetching and consistency checks for settings in which intermediaries are not trusted. 

G4: Confidentiality and availability. F4 shows that these two goals are almost unstudied. For confidentiality, MosaicLeaks (Gurung et al., 2026) and network-level leakage (Jeong et al., 2026) show that the queries of private research leak, and RAG privacy research (Zeng et al., 2024) also shows that private retrieval stores leak through outputs, so query rewriting, differential-privacy-style query obfuscation, and private retrieval need to be studied. For availability, research budgets need to be treated as a security property, and the detection of wallet-draining attacks and bounded research need to be studied. 

G5: Defenses oriented toward evidential reasoning. Content-level defenses cannot han-dle instruction-free data-level manipulation, and integration-level defenses assume that most evi-dence is benign. Evidence integration in DR should be treated as a reasoning problem in an adversarial environment that combines source independence (whether multiple sources are truly independent), temporal consistency, and cross-lingual and cross-platform corroboration, and aggregation methods that provide bounded guarantees under multi-hop retrieval need to be developed, extending the single-round guarantees of RobustRAG (Xiang et al., 2024) to research trajectories. The agenda seed-ing experiment shows that what must be judged here is not only whether a claim is independently corroborated but also why an evaluation criterion matters and who proposed it. Our provenance tag-ging counts sites and can be bypassed with one additional bait site, so stronger evidence of indepen-dence is needed, such as links between publishers, page histories, and times of first appearance. 

G6: Standardized and reproducible evalua-tion. F5 and F7 call for the community to build shared benchmarks: frozen web snapshots, plug-gable rankers, a unified poisoning interface, attack sets covering all four stages, and the three classes of outcome, process, and cost metrics. End-to-end 23 evaluation of commercial products requires coop-eration with vendors and should be conducted in controlled mirror environments. 

G7: Adaptive attacks and defense composi-tion. F6 shows that defense evaluations lack adap-tive attackers. Following the experience of the prompt injection area (Nasr et al., 2026; Zhan et al., 2025), every DR defense should be re-evaluated against attackers who know the defense mecha-nism (the adaptive agenda seeding in Section 10 is one example), and defense in depth across stages should be studied, for example the overall effect under adaptive attacks of combining source weight-ing, integration checks, and generation-level cita-tion audits. 

G8: Multimodality, multiple agents, and hu-man factors. DR agents have begun to read screen-shots, charts, and PDFs, so multimodal injection (Wu et al., 2025a; Zhang et al., 2025d) will enter the research pipeline. Multi-agent architectures (Li et al., 2025e) introduce delegation and shared mem-ory, and Agent-Fence finds that the risk of autho-rization confusion is second only to wallet draining (Puppala et al., 2026). On the human side, cases of hidden prompts in academic manuscripts that target AI reviewers (Lin, 2026) show that the readers of reports can also be targeted, so research is needed on how users read and trust long reports and on how uncertainty and evidence conflicts should be presented so that users can detect polluted conclu-sions. 

12 Comparison with Existing Surveys 

Table 11 compares this paper with existing surveys. 

Differences from capability surveys. Capabil-ity surveys on search agents and deep research (Xi et al., 2025a; Zhang et al., 2025c; Huang et al., 2025; Xu and Peng, 2025; Li et al., 2025e) provide the pipeline division and system lineage used in this paper, but they do not consider adversaries and mention security at most briefly as an open chal-lenge. This paper borrows their pipeline division and reinterprets each stage as an attack surface. 

Differences from agent security surveys. Ex-isting agent security surveys (Deng et al., 2025; He et al., 2025, 2026; Gan et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2025; Kim et al., 2026; Chu, 2026; Ling et al., 2026; Ferrag et al., 2026) and the evaluation study of jail-break guardrails (Wang et al., 2026) differ from this paper in three respects. In the unit of analy-sis, they take agent components (model, memory, tools, environment) or attack techniques as the unit, whereas this paper takes the stages of the research pipeline as the unit and can therefore characterize how attacks propagate among retrieval, planning, integration, and generation. In the threat model, they treat retrieved content as one kind of untrusted input, whereas this paper treats the open web as an ecosystem of publishers, editors, rankers, and observers and distinguishes attacker capabilities from C0 to C6. In security goals, they center on task hijacking and harmful actions, whereas this paper emphasizes the evidence integrity, attribution integrity, query confidentiality, and research cost that matter when reports are taken as conclusions, and points out that instruction-free data-level ma-nipulation makes the prompt injection framework insufficient to cover DR security. Agent-Fence (Puppala et al., 2026) is the work closest to ours and provides an attack taxonomy and evaluation framework for deep agents, and this paper adds a defense taxonomy, alignment with adjacent areas, and literature statistics on top of it. 

13 Conclusion 

DR agents hand the task of reading large numbers of web pages and drawing conclusions to models, and content published or edited by anyone on the web thereby becomes a potential input that can in-fluence those conclusions. This paper organizes security research in this area along four stages, namely retrieval, planning, evidence integration, and report generation, presents a threat model with five trust boundaries, six attacker capability lev-els, and six classes of security goals, builds attack and defense taxonomies, reviews evaluation bench-marks, and aligns the area dimension by dimension with RAG poisoning, prompt injection, and GEO manipulation. Coding statistics over 34 primary studies show that existing research is skewed to-ward the retrieval and integration stages, toward attackers who publish new pages, and toward in-tegrity goals and simulated environments, while planning integrity, editing and supply-chain attack-ers, confidentiality and cost, end-to-end evaluation of commercial systems, and adaptive defense eval-uation remain open.

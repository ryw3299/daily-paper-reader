Abstract
Driving models need to choose which capabilities to use
and when to reconsider their decisions. We introduce
AgentMoT, a Mixture-of-Transformers with built-in agent
reasoning and system scheduling. Combinations of agent,
world, and action experts form three jointly trained
pathways for direct-action, vision-language-action, and
world-action computation. The Router selects a native
pathway, while the agent expert supplies reasoning and
selects skills. Execution feedback raises wake-up re-
quests, and the agent self-scheduler learns whether to
reconsider the task decision or keep it. Mandatory re-
quests invoke the agent directly. The relative-capability
principle guides these choices by relating the benefit of

open-loop initialization, dual-loop evolution trains the
Router, agent, and self-scheduler from closed-loop experi-

on Bench2Drive v0.0.3 and v0.0.4, with 94.48/92.33 driv-

With skills disabled, learned routing improves over the
strongest fixed pathway by 1.79 driving-score points and
4.09 percentage points of success rate on v0.0.3. Abla-
tion studies further show gains from closed-loop agent
learning, two-view queries, and event-triggered wake-up.
1. Introduction
End-to-end driving connects sensor observations to mo-
tion through learned perception and planning [12, 20].

Vision-language reasoning enriches scene interpreta-
tion [14, 27], while world prediction anticipates traffic
evolution [6, 13]. Driving agents further provide access
to specialized tools [24, 29, 36]. Together, these capabil-
ities expand what a driving model can do. Their value
in a particular scene depends on which capabilities the
model chooses to use and when it revisits that choice.
Using these capabilities requires choosing a native
pathway, deciding whether to delegate to a skill, and
determining when to reconsider (Fig. 1). Each choice
depends on context. Reasoning and prediction cost com-
putation. A skill suited to a blocked lane may be in-
appropriate for an ordinary stopped vehicle. Repeated
confirmation wastes agent calls, while new interactions
demand attention.
We present AgentMoT, which embeds agent capa-
bilities into a driving Mixture-of-Transformers (MoT,
Fig. 2). A Router selects among direct-action (A), vision-
language-action (VLA), and world-action model (WAM)
pathways formed by agent, world, and action experts.
The agent expert supplies reasoning within VLA and
WAM. System scheduling combines task selection by G
with call timing from the agent self-scheduler. G selects
native driving or a skill and target. The execution layer
builds the skill–target menu and sends wake-up requests
with updated driving context. The agent self-scheduler
screens ordinary requests, choosing reconsider or keep.
Mandatory requests call G directly and can add a rear
view to identify an approaching vehicle before deciding
whether to yield.
Under the relative capability principle, an option is
useful when its expected benefit over the default justi-
fies its cost. The appropriate choice depends on both
the scene and the capabilities available to the model.
This motivates updating scheduling as those capabilities
evolve.
Dual-loop evolution keeps these learned choices
aligned with the available capabilities. After joint na-
tive training and open-loop initialization of the Router
and G, the inner loop trains the Router, G, and the agent
self-scheduler on closed-loop training-route experience.
The outer loop attributes failures and refines skills and
execution feedback. Rollouts with the updated model
and skills supply experience for the next round.
Our contributions are threefold. (1) We introduce
AgentMoT, which unifies three native driving paradigms
with a shared agent expert for reasoning and tool use.
The model learns to select native computation, delegate
tasks, and reconsider decisions in response to execution
feedback. (2) We develop dual-loop evolution that con-
nects scheduling learned from closed-loop experience
with failure-driven refinement of skills and execution
feedback. (3) We achieve state-of-the-art performance on
Bench2Drive v0.0.3 and v0.0.4. Ablation studies demon-
strate the benefits of learned routing, agent learning, and
execution feedback.
2. Related Work
End-to-end driving.
End-to-end driving integrates per-
ception, prediction, and planning through learned scene
representations [12, 19, 20]. Vision-language models en-
rich action generation with semantic understanding and
reasoning [27, 38]. Future-scene prediction improves
planning [22, 31], with INTERACT [6] learning inter-
action dynamics from driving videos. Closed-loop post-
training further improves driving policies [5, 10, 11].
Together, these directions motivate the complementary
direct-action, reasoning, and prediction capabilities in
AgentMoT.
Mixture-of-Transformers.
MoT combines modality-
specific Transformer parameters with shared attention,
allowing specialized representations to interact within
one model [23]. AutoMoT [14] couples understanding
and action experts through layer-wise attention for driv-
ing. OccWAM [13] uses a three-expert MoT to jointly
generate future occupancy and ego trajectories. These
architectures share context across specialized forms of
computation. AgentMoT organizes agent, world, and
action experts into three selectable driving pathways.
The agent expert also supports task decisions through
the shared vision-language backbone, connecting native
planning with tool use and system scheduling.
Adaptive computation and agents.
Adaptive compu-
tation allocates resources according to the decision, from
selecting specialized experts [32] to adjusting reasoning
effort [9, 26]. Temporal decision methods additionally
learn when to observe or act [3, 37]. Tool-using agents
connect reasoning to environmental feedback [33]. Driv-
ing agents extend this approach with module schedul-
ing, active perception, and verification during execu-
tion [24, 29, 36]. These ideas address both capability
choice and the timing of decisions. In AgentMoT, the
Router selects native computation, G chooses a task, and
the self-scheduler determines whether execution feed-
back warrants reconsideration.

3.1. Relative capability
For context x, option a, and default a0, define
∆(x, a; a0) = [B(x, a)−B(x, a0)]−[C(x, a)−C(x, a0)],
(1)
where B is expected driving benefit and C is compu-
tation or intervention cost over the decision’s horizon,
expressed in compatible units. Positive relative capability
motivates using the additional capability. The defaults are
A for routing, native driving for delegation, and keeping
the current task decision for self-scheduling.
This principle guides three learning problems. Rout-
ing explicitly compares plan rewards and latency costs.
Delegation uses task applicability as supervision, while
self-scheduling balances call reduction against retaining
calls labeled for reconsideration. Section 4 describes
their training signals and objectives.
3.2. Three native pathways
A bird’s-eye-view (BEV) encoder fuses RGB and Li-
DAR into spatial features. Camera history, speed, and
navigation inputs enter the agent expert through its vision-
language backbone. The pathways share the action ex-
pert’s Transformer layers and use pathway-specific input
and output modules. They differ in which expert repre-
sentations condition trajectory generation.
A (direct action). The action expert maps BEV fea-
tures, ego state, and navigation context to an ego trajec-
tory. VLA (vision-language-action). The agent expert’s
vision-language representations supply layer-wise key–
value context to action generation. WAM (world-action
model). Agent reasoning conditions the world expert,
which predicts six future BEV semantic maps at 0.5-
second intervals. The action expert attends to both agent
and world key–value states. Obstacle classes in the pre-
dicted maps and perceived object boxes guide trajectory
refinement for clearance and smooth motion. A uses the
3


action expert, VLA adds the agent expert, and WAM uses
all three.
Each pathway generates six trajectory points over
three seconds. A lightweight Router reads BEV tokens
and compact context features (ego motion, navigation,
and situation indicators) before activating the selected
experts. The shared trajectory interface lets the model
switch computation as the scene changes.
3.3. Agent reasoning and system scheduling
System scheduling combines task selection by G with
call timing from the agent self-scheduler (Sec. 3.4). The
agent expert supplies native reasoning and generates task
decisions through G. Calls to G add structured scene and
task information and a skill–target menu to the image in-
puts. Native reasoning and G share the vision-language
backbone with role-specific low-rank adaptation (LoRA)
adapters and separate key–value caches. Agent post-
training updates the task adapter while keeping the back-
bone and native-planning parameters fixed.
The skill library provides reusable task-level tools for
traffic negotiation, obstruction passing, navigation-goal
adaptation, and traffic-rule compliance. The rule skill in-
cludes emergency-vehicle yielding. The execution layer
constructs a skill–target menu from perceived objects,
navigation context, and task state. At time t, the task-
selection policy is πG(· | xG
t , Mt), where the driving
context xG
t contains images, perception-derived scene
information, and task state, and Mt is the skill–target
menu. G chooses NATIVE (native driving) or a skill and
target. For NATIVE, the Router-selected pathway plans
the trajectory. The execution layer checks skill–target
choices, applies accepted choices through controls or
planning references, and reports task progress to later
calls.
3.4. Self-scheduling from execution feedback
Task decisions often remain useful across many planning
steps. The execution layer refreshes the driving context
and skill–target menu while native planning or a skill
controls the vehicle. It sends wake-up requests when
observations warrant a new decision. Ordinary requests
come from menu changes, task and traffic events, and
periodic reviews. The agent self-scheduler screens these
requests, choosing reconsider (call G) or keep (reuse the
current task decision).
Mandatory requests bypass this choice and call G di-
rectly with refreshed inputs. Execution interrupts and
conflicts between the current task and observed condi-
tions raise mandatory requests. Active wake-up rules also
raise mandatory requests for persistent lack of progress, a
new persistent blocker, or persistent new skill options. An
age-triggered refresh makes an existing request manda-
tory once a preset decision-age threshold is reached. The
execution layer supplies the evidence and requests recon-
sideration, while G makes the task decision.
Two-view queries.
Execution feedback can also re-
quest additional visual evidence. A rear-vehicle cue can
trigger a mandatory two-view query with current front
and rear images. The answer confirms the vehicle type
and whether yielding is needed.
4. Dual-Loop Evolution
The value of a routing or task decision depends on the
capabilities it selects. The inner loop trains the Router,
G, and agent self-scheduler from paired plan rewards,
task applicability, and agent-call outcomes. The outer
loop uses failures to refine skills and execution feedback.
Rollouts with the updated model and skills provide the
experience for the next round (Fig. 3).
4.1. From joint training to closed-loop evolution
Open-loop training mixes A, VLA, and WAM driving ex-
amples with SimLingo question–answer supervision [27]
and GPT-annotated skill–target choices for G. This estab-
lishes the native pathways and connects scene understand-
ing to skill selection. The open-loop Router learns from
pathway trajectory errors against PDM-Lite reference
plans.
Training-route rollouts then produce closed-loop
records. They supply paired pathway rewards, recorded
G proposals, and agent-call outcomes to the inner-loop
objectives below, and failure episodes to the outer loop.
4.2. Learning which pathway to use
Router learning uses the one-step decision structure of
contextual bandits in reinforcement learning (RL) [4, 8,
21]. The Router optimizes the reward of the plan selected
at each recorded context. For each recorded observa-
tion, we generate three-second plans from A, VLA, and
WAM with paired seeds. Scoring all three provides full-
information supervision for offline learning with reward
rR(x, k) = Splan(x, k) −λd min(d(x, k), 3) −λcℓk.
(2)
Here rR is the Router reward for context x and pathway
k ∈{A, VLA, WAM}. Splan is the change in route driv-
ing score over a three-second plan replay, computed with
official driving criteria and recorded scene evolution. d
is expert-trajectory deviation in meters, and ℓk is latency
in seconds. Overbars average four paired seeds. The
weights λd and λc penalize deviation (capped at 3 m) and
latency.

leave the default A for deeper computation, and then
VLA or WAM. Both are trained on Eq. 2. The first min-

4.4. Learning when to reconsider
Execution raises wake-up requests on events and peri-
odic reviews. Most resulting agent calls retain the current
task decision (Table 6). With keep as the default, the
agent self-scheduler learns to reserve calls for attempted
revisions. It uses feedback available before the call to
predict whether reconsideration is needed. Calls logged
before learned self-scheduling provide supervision. A
call receives the reconsider label (mj = 1) if it changes
accepted skill–target choices or returns only rejected pro-
posals. All other calls receive the keep label (mj = 0).
This retains attempted revisions even when the execu-
tion layer rejects them. Appendix E defines the outcome
categories.
The pre-call feedback xj contains the wake-up request,
elapsed time, scene dynamics, task state, and previous
answer. The self-scheduler is a lightweight classifier
with parameters ϕ that predicts a reconsider probability
pϕ(xj). We train it with weighted binary cross-entropy,
Lsched = −Ej[w+mj log pϕ(xj)+(1−mj) log(1−pϕ(xj))].
(4)
The weight w+ balances positive and negative examples.
An ordinary wake-up request calls G when pϕ(xj) ≥τ,
where τ is a calibrated threshold. Otherwise, the current
task decision is kept. Mandatory requests call G directly
(Sec. 3.4).
4.5. Outer-loop attribution and capability repair
GPT reviews failed training episodes with trajectories,
scheduling choices, task states, and official infraction
events. It distinguishes errors in pathway selection and
agent decisions from errors in skill execution. Skill-
execution errors guide coding-agent changes to skills or
the execution layer, including the feedback and wake-up
path. The authors review these changes before training-
route rollouts generate records for the next inner-loop
update.

6. Conclusion
AgentMoT combines three native driving pathways with
an agent that selects tools and learns when to reconsider.
Dual-loop evolution improves these choices through
closed-loop learning and failure-driven refinement of
skills and execution feedback. The model achieves state-
of-the-art performance among PDM-Lite-trained models
on both Bench2Drive versions.

A. Model and execution configuration
Agent, world, and action experts form three native path-
ways. A uses the action expert, VLA adds the agent ex-
pert, and WAM uses all three. A BEV encoder processes
RGB and LiDAR. The Router selects expert activation
from BEV tokens and compact context features. Native
planning and task decisions share one vision-language
backbone with role-specific LoRA adapters and sepa-
rate key–value caches. System scheduling combines
task selection by G with call timing from the agent self-
scheduler. Table S1 summarizes the configuration.
Native planning.
The agent encodes camera history,
speed, navigation targets, and a route command. Its
layer-wise key–value states condition the action expert in
VLA and the world expert in WAM. WAM conditions the
action expert on both agent and world key–value states.
A uses BEV features and ego/navigation inputs. All
pathways share the action expert’s Transformer layers,
use pathway-specific input and output modules, and feed
a common controller.
Shared parameters and adapters.
Native reasoning
and task decisions use separate LoRA adapters on the
shared language backbone. VLA and WAM use the
native-reasoning adapter, while task decisions and two-
view queries use the task adapter. Routing and A use
BEV inputs without native agent reasoning. Agent post-
training updates the task adapter with all other model
parameters fixed.
Task decisions.
Each agent call combines images and
perception-derived scene information with task state to
form the driving context. The execution layer constructs
the skill–target menu from observed objects, naviga-
tion context, and task state. At deployment, G uses
greedy decoding. The reply selects native driving or del-
egates a task for traffic negotiation, obstruction passing,
navigation-goal adaptation, or traffic-rule compliance.
The rule skill includes emergency-vehicle yielding. Task
progress and the resulting scene inform subsequent agent
calls.
Execution and agent calls.
The simulation advances
by 0.05 s per control tick. Routing is refreshed every four
ticks on planning requests. Execution reports driving
state every five ticks. Changes in available skill–target
choices, task and traffic events, and a review every 40
ticks produce wake-up requests from the execution layer.
For ordinary requests, the agent self-scheduler selects
reconsider (call G) or keep (skip the call). Mandatory
requests call G directly. The first wake-up request at least
120 ticks after the previous call invokes G directly (the
age-triggered refresh). Native planning and skill execu-
tion continue between agent calls. Section 5.6 analyzes
amortized model computation outside CARLA.
Execution feedback and two-view queries.
The exe-
cution layer rebuilds the driving context and skill–target
menu from current sensor tracks, ego motion, navigation,
and task state. Mandatory requests include execution
interrupts and observed conflicts, meaning execution re-
ports that the current task conflicts with measured scene
conditions.
Active wake-up adds checks for persistent low
progress without a traffic-control reason to wait, a new
persistent blocker in the driving corridor, and newly avail-
able skill options that persist. These rules invoke G with
refreshed driving context and menu, bypassing the self-
scheduler.

C. Router learning with one-step offline RL
After initialization from open-loop trajectory errors
against PDM-Lite plans, the Router learns an offline
one-step decision policy using the contextual-bandit for-
mulation from RL [4, 8, 21]. The context is a recorded
observation, the action is a pathway choice, and the re-
ward scores its three-second plan. We evaluate A, VLA,
and WAM with paired sampling seeds, obtaining full-
information supervision for all three choices. The de-
ployed policy reads BEV tokens and context features.
C.1. Paired pathway targets
At each recorded context, we generate a three-second
plan from each pathway with four paired seeds. The
scorer applies driving criteria for collision, traffic lights,
stop signs, lane departure, and progress, together with
expert-trajectory deviation and nominal per-pathway la-
tency measured in profiling.

execution, and its latency includes that cost.

scorer combines route completion, infraction penalties,
and lane departure using the official driving-score criteria.

main paper’s reward rR(xi, k) as ri,k here. The reward

C.2. Router architecture
The policy attends to 64 BEV tokens, each projected from
1512 to 64 dimensions by a fixed PCA transform. A 25-
dimensional context supplies the attention query through
a 13-way situation indicator, 11 scalars, and a data-source
indicator that is fixed at deployment. The scalars encode
speed, one-second speed change, upcoming turn angle,
junction status, lateral route deviation, red-light status,
distance to turn onset, acceleration, front-object gap, gap
availability, and following status. Situation categories
distinguish junction maneuvers, stopping causes, obsta-
cle avoidance, following, merging, vulnerable road users,
and cruising. These features are computed from percep-
tion, ego motion, and navigation context.
A 64-dimensional query attends to projected token

D. Agent collection and post-training
The collection records original agent recommendations,
executed actions, randomized assignments, and task out-
comes separately. These records supply task applicability
targets and agent-call outcomes.
D.1. Randomized intervention collection
Randomized interventions broaden the recorded deci-
sions beyond those the agent would choose itself. At
episode initialization, a seeded draw assigns natural ex-
ecution, forced call, or forced no-call with probabilities
0.5, 0.25, and 0.25. An opportunity becomes eligible
when an object first appears in the menu for a target skill.
Each eligible opportunity is selected with probability 0.3
14


until one is chosen, so an episode contributes at most one
selected point.
The natural arm preserves the agent’s sampled re-
sponse. Collection uses full-vocabulary sampling at tem-
perature 1.0 with a budget of 128 new tokens, recording
generated tokens and their probabilities. The forced-call
arm repeats the selected target request until acceptance or
four seconds. The forced-no-call arm suppresses the tar-
get skill family for 20 seconds. Other execution sources
are recorded to identify compliance with the assigned
intervention. Across the collection, 381 selected points
receive 211 natural, 83 forced-call, and 87 forced-no-call
assignments.
Outcome records align official progress and infrac-
tions, task completion, and the return to native driving
with each agent call.

Training and calibration.
The agent self-scheduler
uses a lightweight classifier with features describing the
wake-up request, elapsed time, scene dynamics, task
state, and previous answer. It is trained with weighted
binary cross-entropy, using positive weight (1 −ρ)/ρ for
positive-label fraction ρ in the fitting data. A threshold
calibrated on training routes balances call reduction with
retention of reconsider-labeled calls. The self-scheduler
filters ordinary wake-up requests. Mandatory requests,
the first agent call, and retries for missing or malformed
answers invoke G directly. Keep reuses the current task
decision.
Held-out replay.
Replay over 89 validation episodes
on 28 routes includes 7,313 logged agent calls. The
deployed self-scheduler skips 2,629 calls (35.95%) and
retains 959 of 1,002 reconsider-labeled calls (95.71%).
Of the 43 deferred reconsider-labeled calls, 42 have a
subsequent agent call within the episode. The median
interval to that call is five simulation control ticks. With
the request-based refresh rule described in Appendix A,
the longest observed interval between calls was 160 ticks.
F. Attribution and skill refinement
Failure attribution uses trajectories, official events, se-
lected pathways, agent decisions, task states, and con-
trols. The attribution distinguishes errors in native action
generation, pathway selection, agent decisions, and skill
execution. It records events originating in the scenario
separately. Skill-execution errors guide coding-agent
changes to skills or execution feedback. The authors re-
view these changes before rollouts with the refined skills
supply new examples for the next inner-loop update.
G. Evaluation and additional analyses
G.1. Benchmark and controlled comparisons
We use CARLA 0.9.15 and official seed 2000 for both
Bench2Drive versions [2, 7, 18]. DS combines route
completion with infraction penalties. SR is the percent-
age of routes with DS equal to 100. The five v0.0.3
ability groups contain 45 overtaking, 80 merging, 60
emergency-braking, 10 giving-way, and 95 traffic-sign
routes. Routes can belong to multiple groups.
With skills disabled, we evaluate fixed A, VLA, WAM,
and the Router on all 220 routes and report mean route
DS and SR within each ability group. The three agent
ablations share 60 routes covering all 44 scenario types.
The full system scores 95.09 DS and 90.00% SR on these
routes. Replacing G with its open-loop initialization
removes closed-loop training for task decisions and two-
view answers. The Router, native pathways, and self-
scheduler keep their full-model parameters.
The wake-up ablation disables calls triggered by menu
changes, task and traffic events, execution interrupts, ob-
served conflicts, active wake-up rules, and rear-vehicle
cues. Emergency yielding uses the perception-based rule.
The first call, periodic reviews, and retries for missing
or malformed answers remain enabled. Periodic reviews
receive the updated driving context and skill–target menu.
All learned parameters, the self-scheduler, and the age-
triggered refresh retain their settings.
G.2. Overlap in native pathway failures
The native-pathway collection contains 430 scored runs
on 139 distinct training routes. We have recorded runs
for all three pathways on 81 routes. We define a failed
run by official DS below 100. For each pathway, a route
enters its failure set if any recorded run fails, pooling
seeds and repeated runs. Across all 139 training routes,
61 had a recorded failure for at least one pathway. Of
these, 32 (52.5%) had a recorded failure for all three.

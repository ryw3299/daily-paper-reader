STA: A Split Temporal Adversary Method for
Evaluating Robustness of Multimodal RL-based
Driving Policies
Shenghao Xiong, Xiaolin Chang,
Abstract—Multimodal reinforcement learning (MRL) offers a
promising paradigm for autonomous driving by jointly leveraging
complementary sensor observations to learn effective decision-
making policies. However, its effectiveness critically depends on
temporal alignment across modalities, making temporal incon-
sistency a fundamental vulnerability. Even subtle misalignment
or latency can corrupt fused representations and ultimately
lead to decision failures. Existing studies have explored ad-
versarial attacks to induce cross-modal temporal inconsisten-
cies, thereby enabling systematic evaluation of the temporal
robustness of MRL policies. However, existing methods remain
limited in generality, realism, and adaptivity. To address these
limitations, we propose the Split Temporal Adversary (STA),
which formulates multimodal temporal attacks as a unified
decision process that jointly optimizes attack timing and modality
selection under realistic policy-aware and budget-constrained
settings. STA includes two mechanisms: a) a unified multimodal
temporal perturbation mechanism that delays observations via
historical states, removing the need for modality-specific noise;
b) a split multimodal adversarial decision mechanism that jointly
optimizes attack timing and modality selection through adaptive
optimization while leveraging the victim policy’s clean actions
to identify sensitive decision patterns. We provide a theoretical
analysis of the induced distribution shift and a modality selection
principle. Experiments in the CARLA simulator show that STA
more than doubles the collision rate of the strongest baseline
against PPO while using 17.8% fewer attack attempts, and that
it generalizes to a VLM-based driving policy. These results reveal
temporal consistency as a critical vulnerability, highlighting
temporal robustness as essential for safe autonomous driving.
Index Terms—Adversarial attacks, autonomous driving, mul-
timodal reinforcement learning, temporal consistency.

II. RELATED WORK
Our work lies at the intersection of adversarial attacks on
deep reinforcement learning, robustness evaluation of multi-
modal autonomous driving systems, and temporal attacks in
cyber-physical systems.
A. Adversarial Attacks on Deep Reinforcement Learning
Deep reinforcement learning (DRL) policies are highly
vulnerable to adversarial perturbations. Early work [8] demon-
strated that small observation perturbations can significantly
degrade policy performance in Atari environments. Subse-
quent studies improved attack effectiveness through policy-
sensitive objectives [9], [10], [24], e.g., boosting success rates
by 18.7% via policy-sensitive targeting [10]. More advanced
formulations model attacker–victim interactions as sequential
decision-making processes [25]–[27].
These DRL attack methods operate in a step-wise manner,
perturbing observations independently at each timestep. This
design largely ignores long-term temporal dependencies and
assumes unrestricted access to all modalities, limiting their
applicability to multimodal driving scenarios. A growing body
of work has begun to address these limitations by considering
temporally correlated perturbations, yet such efforts remain
largely confined to single-modality settings [28], [29].
B. Robustness of Multimodal Driving Policies
Modern autonomous driving systems rely heavily on multi-
modal sensor fusion, introducing new attack surfaces across
perception and decision-making pipelines. Prior work has
shown that perturbations in one modality can propagate
through fusion networks and significantly degrade downstream
policy performance [4], [5].
At the decision-making level, several works formulate ad-
versarial driving as constrained optimization or reinforcement
learning problems to induce unsafe behaviors [11], [13]–[15].
In parallel, robustness evaluation and certification methods
have been proposed to quantify safety under adversarial con-
ditions [30]–[32].
However, these studies primarily focus on spatial or
scenario-level perturbations, and do not explicitly consider
temporal inconsistency across heterogeneous modalities.
C. Temporal Attacks and Time-Delay Vulnerability
Temporal attacks have proven effective in cyber-physical
systems, where injected delays destabilize control loops in
power grids, sensor networks, and UAV formations [17],
[33]–[35]. Translating this threat to autonomous driving,
Chronos [18] first demonstrated that software-induced timing
delays can degrade perception and planning. Subsequent work
has explored broader forms of temporal attacks, including sen-
sor spoofing with time shifts and delayed actuation commands.
More broadly, recent studies on optimal attack scheduling in
cyber-physical systems have shown that strategic timing can
achieve greater disruption with fewer interventions, providing
theoretical grounding for adaptive scheduling [36].
However, existing temporal attacks on autonomous driving
suffer from two persistent limitations. First, most are evaluated
only at the perception level: DejaVu [16] showed that temporal
misalignment between camera and LiDAR streams can reduce
detection mAP by 88.5% with a single-frame delay, yet does
not assess downstream impact on driving decisions; Slow-
Perception [37] achieved a 97% collision rate, but its delay
schedule is pre-determined rather than adaptively optimized.
Second, existing approaches operate in an open-loop manner,
with fixed or heuristic-driven delays that lack policy awareness
and attack budget constraints.
Consequently, while these works confirm temporal incon-
sistency as a severe vulnerability, none addresses adaptive,
policy-aware, budget-constrained temporal attack optimization
that jointly selects when to attack and which modality to delay
which our STA method directly fills.

IV. SPLIT TEMPORAL ADVERSARY METHOD
This section first overviews the proposed STA. Then, the
two core mechanisms of STA are detailed.
A. Overview
We evaluate the temporal robustness of multimodal RL
driving policies against adversarial temporal inconsistency,
where an adversary selectively disrupts cross-modal alignment
under a limited attack budget. We assume: (i) the driving
policy πdriving is fixed during evaluation [8], [24]; (ii) temporal
delays are bounded within horizon n [16], [18]; and (iii) all
sensors operate at a fixed synchronized rate [4], [5]. The
adversary has a finite budget B and aims to induce failures
(e.g., collisions).
Split Temporal Adversary (STA) consists of two coordinated
mechanisms:
1) Unified Multimodal Temporal Perturbation Mech-
anism: maintains a multimodal history buffer and re-
places the current observation of a selected modality
with its delayed historical counterpart.
2) Split Multimodal Adversarial Decision Mechanism:
jointly optimizes attack triggering and modality selec-
tion through a split decision architecture, while in-
corporating policy-aware state information and attack
budget constraints to learn adaptive attack strategies
under sparse rewards.
Together, these two mechanisms enable STA to generate
adaptive, modality-agnostic temporal attacks under realistic
adversarial constraints. We describe each mechanism in detail
in the following subsections.

D. Overall Workflow of Split Temporal Adversary (STA)
Algorithm 1 summarizes the overall workflow of STA by
integrating the two proposed mechanisms. At each interaction
step, the unified multimodal temporal perturbation mechanism
maintains the history buffer and performs temporal replace-
ment on the selected modality, while the split multimodal
adversarial decision mechanism constructs the policy-aware
adversarial state and determines both attack triggering and
modality selection under budget constraints. The adversarial
policy is optimized using PPO, whereas the victim driving
policy remains fixed throughout training.
Following Fan et al. [21], we further adopt a trajectory
cropping strategy to improve the training efficiency of the
split adversarial policy under finite attack budgets. Once the
budget bt is exhausted, the adversary can no longer execute
further attacks, and the remaining interaction may not provide
meaningful learning signals for the adversarial policy. To
improve training efficiency, we truncate the episode at the
point of budget exhaustion. If the final executed attack results
in a collision, the episode is terminated immediately and the
trajectory is stored for policy optimization. Otherwise, the
environment is allowed to continue for up to T additional
steps to capture delayed effects of previous attacks, and any
collision occurring within this window is still considered
as a valid adversarial success signal. This design ensures
that STA focuses on learning effective attack timing under
strict budget constraints while avoiding redundant trajectory
collection after budget depletion, and is particularly important
for PPO stability under sparse reward settings.

VI. EXPERIMENTS
We evaluate whether STA (i) induces more safety-critical
failures than spatial and random temporal baselines, (ii) uses a
limited attack budget more efficiently, and (iii) transfers across
victim-policy families.
A. Experimental Setup
We use a black-box threat model in which the adver-
sary observes the victim’s output action but not its action
distribution or parameters. Experiments use CARLA 0.9.13,
Town02, dense traffic, and fixed clear weather. Victims receive
an RGB image plus speed and waypoint states and output
continuous steering and throttle. We evaluate PPO (trained
for 440,000 steps), SAC (235,000 steps), and our CARLA
reimplementation of DARRL [22]. Each victim is trained on
clean observations and frozen before adversary training.
STA is trained with PPO for 800,000 steps. Unless stated
otherwise, the delay horizon is n = 10, the history-buffer
length is 10, the attack budget is B = 30, and an episode lasts
at most 50 steps. Experiments ran on Intel Xeon Gold 6230
CPUs and NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPUs.
a) Baselines.: We compare against the following base-
lines:
• Clean: Clean execution without any intervention.
• RTA (Random Temporal Attack): Randomly selects
timesteps to apply a fixed n-step delay to a random
modality [37].
• RTAM (Random Temporal Attack on all Modalities):
Randomly selects timesteps to apply a fixed n-step delay
to both numerical and visual modalities [16].
• ADV (PGD-based Adversary): A learned attacker that
applies PGD perturbations and adaptively decides when
to attack [26].
b) Evaluation Metrics.: We report collision rate (CR)
and average number of executed attacks (ANA). Results ag-
gregate 50 evaluation episodes for each of five independent
seeds; the table reports the mean and variance across seeds.
• Collision Rate (CR): The percentage of episodes that
terminate in a collision. A higher CR indicates a more
effective attack at inducing safety-critical failures.
• Average Number of Attacks (ANA): The mean number
of attacks executed per episode. When combined with
a high CR, a lower ANA reflects superior strategic
allocation of the limited attack budget.
B. Main Results
Table I shows that STA is the strongest attack on PPO and
SAC while using fewer interventions than the baselines. The
DARRL result also reveals policy-dependent vulnerability:
temporal and spatial attacks expose different failure modes.
On PPO, STA raises CR from 16.8% for the strongest
baseline (ADV) to 35.6%, while reducing ANA from 29.396
to 24.168 (17.8% fewer interventions). The random temporal
baselines nearly exhaust the budget yet remain substantially
less effective, supporting the value of policy-aware attack
timing and modality selection.
The outcome is policy-dependent. STA is also the strongest
attack on SAC, although its advantage over ADV is smaller
(18.2% versus 16.8%). DARRL instead remains more vulner-
able to ADV (32.2%) than to STA (18.6%). Thus, robustness
to spatial perturbations does not imply robustness to temporal
inconsistency, and evaluation should cover both threat models.
C. VLM-Based Driving Strategy Evaluation
We also evaluate the VLM-based driving policy of [1] under
the same Town02 configuration (B = 30, n = 10). STA
delays image frames; the textual instruction is fixed at episode
initialization and is therefore not attacked.
As shown in Table II, the clean, random-delay, and PGD
conditions yield 0–2% CR, whereas STA reaches 62% CR
with 26.98 attacks per episode. This result suggests that policy-
aware temporal disruption can expose failure modes beyond
conventional RL policies, although broader evaluation across
VLM architectures remains future work.
VII. CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK
In this work, we study temporal vulnerability in multimodal
RL-based driving policies. Unlike existing works that focus
on spatial perturbations, we introduce a threat model that
leverages structured temporal misalignment to expose hidden


8
failure modes. We propose STA, a policy-aware and budget-
constrained attack framework that selectively delays modality-
specific observations, preserving per-modality semantic in-
tegrity while inducing cross-modal inconsistency. In CARLA,
STA achieves a 35.6% collision rate on PPO—more than
double that of the strongest baseline—while requiring 17.8%
fewer interventions. Its effectiveness on a VLM-based driving
policy further suggests that temporal inconsistency is relevant
beyond conventional RL. The policy-dependent results also
show that resistance to spatial perturbations does not guarantee
robustness against temporal inconsistency.
Future work will explore more constrained black-box set-
tings with limited query access, adaptive and learnable delay
strategies, and extensions to richer sensor modalities such
as LiDAR, radar, and GPS. We also plan to investigate the
applicability of STA in real-world and multi-agent driving
environments to better understand temporal vulnerability under
practical deployment conditions.

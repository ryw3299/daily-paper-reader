ABSTRACT
Autonomous driving requires anticipating how traffic will evolve while responding quickly to new
observations. Video foundation models offer the first ability, but repeated history encoding and it-
erative video generation make them costly to use for driving. We present AR-WAM, an efficient
streaming world-action model that combines autoregressive temporal modeling with single-pass fu-
ture conditioning. Because its video backbone is causal, AR-WAM updates its visual context in-
crementally, reusing past computation and conditioning future prediction on observed motion. We
further hypothesize that future features computed from recent observations can improve generaliza-
tion in dynamic traffic without iterative video generation. AR-WAM therefore obtains these features
in one forward pass and uses them to condition trajectory generation, and reinforcement learning
then improves the policy through direct optimization of driving rewards. With a single front camera,
AR-WAM achieves 91.5 PDMS after supervised training and 94.0 after reinforcement learning on
NAVSIM-v1, outperforming the compared strong end-to-end world-action models. In a dynamic
setting of continuous driving, supervised AR-WAM keeps its visual history across decisions and
outperforms strong world-action baselines.

Introduction
End-to-end driving policies increasingly build on pretrained foundation models. Vision-language-action (VLA) mod-
els transfer semantic knowledge from image-text pretraining to trajectory prediction [16, 17]. World-action models
(WAMs) instead start from video foundation models, whose pretraining on how scenes evolve provides priors over
geometry, motion and near-future dynamics [24, 30, 39, 50]. These priors suit driving, because the scene changes
continuously and the right action depends on how other road users are moving: a lead vehicle at the same distance
may be braking or pulling away, and a single image often cannot tell the two apart.
A driving policy therefore needs recent observations, which reveal changes in relative position and speed. Current-
image policies, including OpenVLA and π0 in robot manipulation [3, 14] and current-frame driving WAMs [50], omit
this visual history, although memory-based policies demonstrate its value on temporally dependent tasks [32]. Driving
WAMs such as DriveVA and DriveLaW do encode a window of recent frames [24, 39]. With bidirectional temporal
attention, however, historical features depend on the other frames in the window, so each new observation requires
re-encoding the window [42]. More history then buys motion information at the cost of computation repeated at each
decision.
Future prediction adds a second source of latency. WAMs such as DriveWAM and DriveVA iteratively predict future
video before or alongside action generation [24, 30], so each decision pays for video denoising even when, as in Drive-
WAM, the observed history is cached. To avoid this cost, Fast-WAM and SimWAM remove future video tokens at
inference while retaining video supervision during training [44, 50]. Yet Simple-WAM shows, in controlled compar-
isons on robot manipulation, that retaining future conditioning improves generalization, and that a single forward pass
over fully noised future tokens recovers most of the benefit [52]. Motivated by these findings, we seek a driving policy

We present AR-WAM, an efficient streaming world-action model that combines autoregressive temporal modeling
with single-pass future conditioning (Fig. 1). Because causal attention makes the keys and values (K/V) of completed
observation chunks reusable when new frames arrive, as in streaming video generation [12, 42], AR-WAM updates
its visual history incrementally and makes it available to future prediction. Autoregressive pretraining also learns how
future states depend on past observations, and recent manipulation results show that it can strengthen the benefit of
visual history [11]. We therefore ask how much test-time future prediction contributes once the policy already observes
recent motion, and how much of it a driving decision needs.
At each decision, AR-WAM computes only what has changed. It adds the new observation to its cache and looks
ahead with one backbone pass over the current image and fully noised future tokens. Its trajectory expert then reads
the resulting predictive features and the cached history at matching backbone depths, so no future video is denoised.
The ego state and driving command also change at every decision, and conditioning the history on them would require
recomputing it each time [11]. AR-WAM therefore applies them only to the predictive pass and the expert, which

keeps the cache valid as driving intent changes. We train video and trajectory prediction jointly, and reinforcement
learning then improves the policy through direct optimization of driving rewards [22, 50].
Built on OmniDreams, a pretrained causal video backbone [2], with a single front camera, AR-WAM attains a higher
NAVSIM-v1 PDMS than strong WAMs that rely on bidirectional video backbones, such as SimWAM, which plans
from a single observation, and UNIVERSE, which re-encodes a window of frames [23]. In a dynamic setting of
continuous driving, it also outscores both while running 3.4 and 1.8 times faster, and visual memory accounts for most
of its lead over SimWAM. With this history available, one predictive pass matches ten passes of joint future denoising
at under a quarter of their latency, and its benefit shows on the perturbed NAVHARD scenes. After refinement with
the EPDMS reward, AR-WAM reaches the highest NAVSIM-v2 EPDMS on both NAVTEST and NAVHARD among
the compared WAMs that predict a single trajectory without scoring candidates at test time.
Our contributions are:
• We introduce AR-WAM, a causal world-action model that plans from an incrementally updated cache of
observed history and a single predictive pass. Two designs keep this cache reusable: the ego state and driving
command enter only the per-decision pass, and decision-relative attention lets one trajectory expert read
cached and predictive features at any point of a stream.
• We study the role of test-time future prediction once visual history is available, and integrate it into autore-
gressive world-action modeling as a single backbone pass over noised future tokens.
• We demonstrate strong single-camera planning performance, achieving 91.5 NAVSIM-v1 PDMS after super-
vised training and 94.0 after reinforcement learning. In the dynamic setting, AR-WAM scores higher than the
compared world-action models at lower latency.

Related work
Future modeling at test time.
World-action models are trained to predict the future, but they differ in how much of it
they compute when they act. Generating future video before or alongside the trajectory [24, 30] makes every decision
pay for iterative denoising. To avoid this latency, Fast-WAM, SimWAM and UNIVERSE keep video prediction as
a training signal and remove the future tokens at inference [23, 44, 50]. The action head then reads no predictive
features, however, and Simple-WAM and Faster-WAM show in manipulation that this costs generalization [48, 52].
Other methods keep predictive features while avoiding a full rollout at every decision [1, 8, 39, 46, 47], and Simple-
WAM finds that a single pass over pure-noise future tokens recovers most of the loss. Yet it and Adaptive-WAM
establish this only for policies that see the current observation, so it remains unclear how such a pass should work
beside a cache of history and how much it adds once the policy observes recent motion. Building on the single-pass
insight, AR-WAM computes predictive features in one pass that attends to cached observation history, and compares
it with a variant without future tokens and with full denoising.
Visual history and memory.
The right action depends on motion that a single image does not show. SimWAM
and Adaptive-WAM nevertheless plan from the current frame [1, 50], and so cannot observe how other road users are
moving. DriveVA, DriveLaW and UNIVERSE observe motion through a window of recent frames [23, 24, 39], but
because the window is encoded again whenever a frame arrives, their cost grows with the history they keep. Caching
the K/V of observed frames removes this repetition. DriveWAM caches observed video and action chunks [30], yet it
generates future video at every decision, and on NAVSIM it makes a single prediction from the current frame, so its
memory plays no role there. In manipulation, Long-WAM reuses the K/V of observed frames within one action solve
and notes what would invalidate them across decisions, such as an updated robot state or shifted temporal positions
[11], whereas MemoryVLA++ retrieves history from a separate memory bank and still denoises future latents with a
separate world model [31]. OmniDreams, the causal backbone we build on, reports preliminary results for a policy
that runs it once per decision [2], but that policy reads the backbone through a single token and is not described as
reusing computation across decisions. Motivated by these gaps, AR-WAM pairs a cache of observed history with
the rollout-free pass above, so that neither history encoding nor future denoising is repeated at a decision. Because
conditioning visual features on the ego state or driving command, as SimWAM does for the command, would invalidate
the cache whenever they change, AR-WAM computes history without these inputs and applies them only in the per-
decision pass. Because cached features keep the positions at which they were computed, its trajectory expert measures
positions relative to the current decision. It therefore sees the same layout at the start of a stream and after minutes of
driving, and no cached feature has to be modified.
Reward refinement.
Flow-GRPO makes the sampler of a flow-matching model stochastic, so that sampled out-
comes can drive policy-gradient updates [22], and SimWAM applies it to driving with rewards computed in the logged

scene [50]. SimWAM restricts the update to LoRA adapters of its action expert, whereas DSRL keeps the policy frozen
and trains an auxiliary policy over its initial noise [35]. Building on these methods, AR-WAM refines the full expert,

Together, these gaps motivate the design principle of AR-WAM: the visual computation of a decision never depends
on the candidate trajectory, and its history part never depends on the ego state or command. As a result, history is
reused across decisions, the predictive pass across action samples, and both across reward-refinement updates, so one
model addresses three challenges jointly.

Preliminaries
The policy observes a front-camera history H = (I−NH+1, . . . , I0) and a vector q containing ego state, recent ego
poses and the driving command. It predicts A = (a1, . . . , aT ), where each aj is a future ego pose (planar position

4
AR-WAM
AR-WAM pairs a pretrained causal video diffusion transformer (DiT), the backbone with parameters ϕ, with the
trajectory expert vθ of Eq. (1); each has L blocks (Fig. 2). Visual history is computed once and cached as frames arrive
(Sec. 4.1), each decision adds one predictive pass (Sec. 4.2), and the expert reads both at every depth (Sec. 4.3).
4.1
Cached visual history
A policy that re-encodes a window of recent frames repeats most of its visual computation at every decision. Causal
video generators avoid such repetition: because a frame never depends on later ones under causal attention, the atten-
tion keys and values (K/V) of earlier frames can be cached and reused [12, 42]. Building on these designs, AR-WAM
keeps its observation history in the causal form of the backbone and bounds it with a sliding window.
Let n index the frames of a stream. When frame n arrives, a causal tokenizer encodes it into one latent, carrying
its internal state forward. The backbone processes the history latents yh in fixed-size chunks under chunk-causal
attention: a chunk attends to itself and at most W preceding chunks, never to later tokens. The K/V of a completed
chunk therefore enter a cache that keeps the newest W chunks, and only the newest, incomplete chunk is recomputed
when a frame arrives. Positions are encoded with rotary embeddings [33], under which attention depends on positions
only through the offset between query and key; each history latent keeps its stream index as its temporal position.
Each decision then costs C = Cinputs +Chistory(W)+Cworld(W)+K Cexpert(W), with terms for encoding the new
frame, updating at most one history chunk, the predictive pass of Sec. 4.2 and the trajectory expert’s K integration
steps. For fixed W, memory and per-decision work stay bounded however long the stream runs.

4.2
One predictive pass per decision
Generating future video by iterative denoising multiplies the backbone cost of each decision. Simple-WAM [52] finds,
for manipulation policies conditioned on the current observation, that one forward pass over future tokens left at pure
noise retains most of the benefit of test-time future modeling. Without observation history, that study leaves open
how such a pass coexists with a cache: what it may attend to, whether it is stored, and how per-decision inputs enter.
Building on this insight, AR-WAM introduces a world segment that is rebuilt at each decision.

at deployment. It takes temporal positions n −F, . . . , n. Its tokens attend to all cached history K/V and follow
the chunk-causal mask among themselves. The backbone pass Φϕ thus yields the visual context of Eq. (1), cτ =

Because the ego state and driving command q change at every decision whereas observed frames do not, conditioning
the history on q would invalidate the cache at each change. AR-WAM therefore injects q only into the world segment
and the expert. World tokens receive an embedding of q from a learned ego conditioner through the backbone’s
conditioning cross-attention, and history tokens always use the unconditional context (Appendix A). No candidate
trajectory enters the pass either, so its K/V are shared by all action samples and integration steps.
4.3
Layerwise trajectory expert
Pooling the backbone into a single feature vector would discard the spatial and temporal detail that motion reasoning
needs. World-action models that pair a video backbone with a separate action network instead let that network attend
to the backbone’s features inside each attention layer [44, 50], reading the current frame only. Because AR-WAM
must also read a cache whose keys carry ever-growing stream positions, we extend this layerwise attention into a
decision-relative form, in which the trajectory expert sees the same relative positions however long the stream has run.

4.4
Joint training
The backbone, its ego conditioner, the current-image encoder and the trajectory expert are trained jointly with two
flow-matching losses:
L = Lact + λvLvideo,
(4)
where Lact regresses the velocity of the normalized demonstrated trajectory A⋆, Lvideo regresses the velocity of the
F future latents through a video head used only in training, and λv weights the video term. At deployment the future
slots are pure noise, so the expert must be trained on such inputs, while the backbone should keep video supervision at
every noise level. We therefore adopt the mixed noise schedule of Simple-WAM [52]: all future slots share one video
flow time, set to τ = 1 with probability p and drawn from the usual schedule otherwise. Appendix A gives the losses,
schedules and weights.
4.5
Reward refinement
Imitation fits the recorded maneuver without comparing the consequences of alternative plans. Policy gradients can
make that comparison, but the deterministic integration of a flow policy gives no tractable likelihood for a sampled
plan. Flow-GRPO [22] injects noise into the sampling steps of flow-matching generators, which yields exploration and
tractable path likelihoods, and SimWAM [50] shows that rewards computed in the logged scene can refine a driving
expert in this way. Building on these results, AR-WAM refines its full expert at low cost: because the visual pass
does not depend on the trajectory, each scene needs one frozen pass, shared by all sampled plans and policy updates.
We inject Gaussian noise into the expert’s K integration steps and sample G plans per scene. A rule-based driving
score R, such as the PDM score [7], rates each plan in the scene’s logged state. Group-relative advantages compare
each plan with the others from its scene and drive a clipped policy-gradient update with a velocity anchor to the
starting expert. Such advantages are commonly divided by the standard deviation of the rewards in their group. With
rule-based driving scores this is a poor fit, because nearly tied groups are the norm. Such scores consist mostly of
pass/fail checks, so the plans that a well-trained policy samples in an ordinary scene all pass and differ only slightly
in score, and the division would weigh these small differences as heavily as a collision avoided in a difficult scene.
Dr. GRPO [27] identifies this bias toward groups whose rewards differ little and removes the division. We do the same
and train on a mix of mined hard scenes and ordinary scenes with one shared reward scale. At deployment, the expert
integrates deterministically from a zero trajectory. Appendix A.2 gives the exploration kernel, the objective and the
scene pools.

Training and evaluation.

setting of Sec. 5.3 has no published results, so we run four models that handle history and the future differently.
SimWAM plans from the current frame with the expert design closest to ours, and UNIVERSE, the strongest peer-
reviewed single-camera world-action model in Table 2, re-encodes a window of frames. DriveVA [24] also re-encodes
a window but generates future video with the trajectory, and DriveWAM [30] does so from the current frame before
planning. Appendix B gives details.
5.2
Does future modeling improve decisions?
Table 1 compares the single predictive pass with two alternatives. In the no-future variant, the trajectory expert reads
only the observed history and the current latent, although the backbone is still trained to predict future video, as in
current-frame WAMs that drop future tokens at inference [44, 50]. This variant repeats AR-WAM’s final training
stage with the same initialization, data order, noise draws, losses and schedule, and differs only in what the expert
reads. In the clear-future variant, AR-WAM’s own weights denoise the future video jointly with the action, following
Simple-WAM’s joint inference [52], so the last action steps read an almost clean predicted future at the cost of one
backbone pass per action step.

5.3
Evaluation in a dynamic setting
Each standard NAVTEST query rebuilds the policy’s history from four frames, so it cannot measure the effect of
history carried across decisions. We therefore also evaluate a dynamic setting in which each policy runs through
138 full NAVTEST source logs in chronological order (69,711 frames at 2 Hz) and keeps its history across frames.
Observations come from the logs and do not depend on the predicted actions. Plans are scored with one-stage EPDMS,
including extended comfort (EC) between consecutive plans.
Evaluated frames.
NAVSIM builds NAVTRAIN and NAVTEST without scenes it considers trivial, such as standing
still or driving straight at near-constant speed [7], but full logs keep them. In 32% of the frames in our logs, the
logged vehicle moves less than 1 m over the next 4 s, compared with 1.9% of NAVTEST frames. Policies trained on
NAVTRAIN therefore rarely see sustained waiting, while UNIVERSE and DriveVA train on full logs. To compare
policies on the benchmark’s scene distribution, we exclude these waiting frames. The criterion uses only the logged
vehicle, so all policies are scored on the same frames.
On the 47,220 non-waiting frames valid for all compared policies (Table 3), AR-WAM outscores all four baselines and
runs faster. Its lead over SimWAM, which replans from the current frame, comes mainly from fewer at-fault collisions
and time-to-collision violations, and visual memory accounts for most of it: the current-frame variant, which repeats
AR-WAM’s final training stage without visual history, falls between the two, closer to SimWAM. This memory costs
about 40 ms per decision. The window models UNIVERSE and DriveVA have much lower extended comfort, meaning
less consistent consecutive plans. DriveVA, which generates future video at every decision, scores below UNIVERSE.
DriveWAM has the highest extended comfort but trails AR-WAM by 0.6 EPDMS at six times AR-WAM’s latency.
Refinement with the EPDMS reward raises both EPDMS and extended comfort further.
5.4
Benchmark comparison
Table 2 compares AR-WAM with world-action models and reference planners, and Table 5 in the appendix lists all
compared methods. Direct methods output one trajectory per decision; proposal-scoring methods select one of up to 64
candidates with a scorer trained on PDM sub-scores. Among the compared supervised direct methods, AR-WAM has
the highest NAVSIM-v1 PDMS with a single camera, and only the four-camera WA-JEPA scores higher. Its NAVSIM-
v2 EPDMS is within 0.1 of the best supervised single-camera result, and on NAVHARD only GeoWAM, pretrained
on 832 additional hours of driving video, scores higher among direct methods. The supervised policy is cautious:
its no-collision rate is close to the best, and ego progress is its weakest term. Reward refinement shifts this balance
toward progress. With the PDMS reward, AR-WAM reaches 94.0 PDMS, above every direct method and above the
proposal-scoring DA-WAM and DrivoR. With the EPDMS reward, it has the best NAVSIM-v2 scores among direct
methods on both NAVTEST and NAVHARD, although DrivoR, a planner without a world model that scores its own
proposals from four cameras, stays ahead on NAVHARD.

5.5
Zero-shot transfer
To test transfer beyond NAVSIM, we apply AR-WAM to nuScenes [4] without fine-tuning, following DriveVA’s eval-
uation protocol [24] on 4,519 samples from 150 validation scenes. SimWAM, UNIVERSE, DriveVA and DriveWAM
are evaluated on the same samples, together with a constant-velocity reference, since ego state alone can explain much
of open-loop performance [18]. Table 4 also splits the samples with the waiting criterion of Sec. 5.3. NAVSIM’s
training protocol removes most scenes in which the vehicle stands still, so the non-waiting samples correspond to the
kind of scenes AR-WAM is trained on. On these samples, AR-WAM and DriveWAM transfer best, with the lowest
L2 error and about half the collision rate of UNIVERSE and DriveVA. Over all samples, including the 14% of wait-
ing samples that its training largely excludes, AR-WAM stays close to UNIVERSE (0.87 versus 0.83 m average L2),
matches DriveVA and has the lowest one-second error. DriveWAM has the lowest collision rate but higher L2 error
than AR-WAM at every horizon. On the waiting samples, UNIVERSE and DriveVA, which train on full logs, stay
near the log, while AR-WAM outperforms SimWAM and DriveWAM, the other NAVSIM-trained policies.
5.6
Reward refinement
Reward refinement on a mix of mined hard scenes and ordinary scenes raises NAVTEST PDMS from 91.6 to 94.0
at four integration steps (Table 2). The gain comes from ego progress and drivable-area compliance, while time-to-
collision compliance drops. Refining with the NAVSIM-v2 EPDMS reward instead raises EPDMS from 90.9 to 92.2
on NAVTEST and from 38.6 to 42.3 on NAVHARD.
5.7
Runtime and history reuse
UNIVERSE re-encodes its five-frame window at every decision, whereas AR-WAM encodes only the newest frame
and reuses its cache. With the same five frames, AR-WAM is 1.8 times faster than UNIVERSE and DriveVA in
median latency (Table 3), although it also computes predictive features. It is 3.4 times faster than SimWAM, which
plans from the current frame with ten flow steps, and 6.1 times faster than DriveWAM. Four action steps instead of ten
lower AR-WAM’s latency further at unchanged NAVTEST PDMS. Table 6 in the appendix adds tail latency and peak
memory.
6
Conclusion
AR-WAM pairs a cache of observed history with one predictive pass and reads both through decision-relative attention.
Because its visual computation depends on neither the candidate trajectory nor, for history, the ego state or command,
it is reused across decisions, action samples and reward-refinement updates. With history available, one predictive
pass matches iterative future denoising at under a quarter of its latency. AR-WAM reaches 94.0 NAVSIM-v1 PDMS
after reward refinement and leads other strong world action baselines in the dynamic setting at lower latency.

Implementation details
Architecture and inputs.
Front-camera images are resized to 352 × 640. The frozen causal Wan tokenizer [34]
emits one latent of shape 16 × 44 × 80 per observed frame, using its retained state. The current image and logged
continuation are encoded separately for the world segment, which contains one current and three future latents (F = 3).
Spatial 2 × 2 patching gives 880 tokens per history latent and 3,520 tokens in the world segment. The backbone’s
native chunks hold two latents. History positions follow the stream index, and world positions are relative to the
newest observation. The current-blind mask blocks access from the current latent to its first future chunk-mate.
The video backbone has 28 blocks of width 2048, with 16 attention heads of dimension 128. It is initialized from the
public map-conditioned OmniDreams weights [2]. The trajectory expert has 28 blocks of width 1536 and feed-forward
width 6144, using the same attention head space. Compatible attention and feed-forward weights are initialized by re-
sizing backbone weights. Residual gates and the output layer start at zero. The image branch encodes an independently
tokenized 384 × 672 current image into 252 tokens of width 256.
Each backbone block cross-attends to a text context. Let e0 be the backbone’s projection of a null-text row and Pϕ the
ego conditioner. World tokens receive the two-row context
Eq = [e0; e0 + Pϕ(q)],
(5)
and history tokens the null context [e0; e0]. Pϕ projects the 24-dimensional q through a 1024-wide SiLU MLP to a
1024-dimensional offset, with a zero-initialized final layer. It belongs to the backbone optimizer group and is included
in the exponential moving average (EMA). Using two cross-attention rows, rather than appending the ego row to the
backbone’s hundreds of identical null-text rows, keeps the ego input from being diluted; with equal initial rows, the
pretrained null-only response is preserved exactly. As in SimWAM [50], the narrower expert projects its queries into
the backbone’s per-head attention space and projects the result back to its own width; timestep-modulated residual
blocks predict the action velocity.
Supervised initialization.
An initial supervised stage uses four-frame clips, a causal hold adapter with 13 RGB slots,
local four-latent rotary grids for both segments, and ego context throughout the backbone. Its final EMA initializes
streaming training. This stage uses AdamW with β = (0.9, 0.95), ϵ = 10−8 and weight decay 0.01. Both parameter
groups peak at 7 × 10−5 after 2,000 warm-up updates and decay by a cosine schedule to 10−6. Global batch size is
32 on four NVIDIA H200 GPUs with micro-batch eight. Training runs for 25 NAVTRAIN passes, or 80,694 updates.
Gradients are clipped at norm 1.0 and activation checkpointing is enabled. EMA uses decay 0.9999 with an initial
warm-up. Both flow schedules use shift s = 5; the video weight is λv = 3, the endpoint probability p = 1
2 and the
endpoint video weight one. Inference uses K = 10 flow steps with fixed action and future-noise seeds. Plans contain
T = 8 poses over four seconds. Coordinates are normalized using lower bounds (−1.57, −19.68, −1.67) and ranges
(66.74, 42, 3.53) for (x, y, ψ); yaw is wrapped after denormalization.

A.1
Streaming training
Streaming training starts from the initial supervised EMA at update 80,694. The backbone, ego conditioner, current-
image branch and trajectory expert are fine-tuned jointly for 10,000 updates, or 320,000 scene presentations. The
tokenizer remains frozen. AdamW uses the same betas, epsilon and weight decay as the initialization stage, with a
peak learning rate of 2 × 10−5 after 500 warm-up updates and cosine decay to 10−6. Global batch size is 32 on
four GPUs. EMA decay is 0.9995 after its initial warm-up. Gradient clipping, activation checkpointing and both
flow-matching objectives are unchanged. Results use the final EMA, not a selected intermediate checkpoint.
The history cache covers all 103,288 training scenes and stores 246,165 available camera frames across 9,339 runs in
1,192 logs. A run ends at a missing image or a timestamp gap greater than two seconds. Within a run, the first image
is encoded alone. Each later latent encodes three copies of the preceding image followed by the new image, carrying
the tokenizer’s causal state between calls. Training uses local positions within each sample, while deployment retains
stream positions. The action rotation in Eq. (3) makes the action interface invariant to this common temporal offset.
A.2
Reward-based policy refinement
Data and visual context.
Refinement draws from 85,190 NAVTRAIN scenes, and 12,660 scenes from disjoint
recordings are held out for development. The training pool combines 29,254 hard scenes, mined with an earlier
supervised model, and 14,627 ordinary scenes drawn once at random from the remaining training scenes, so hard
scenes make up two thirds of the pool. Rewards are official non-reactive NAVSIM-v1 PDM scores, computed for
each candidate against the fixed PDM-Closed reference [7]. A second policy uses the NAVSIM-v2 EPDMS reward,
which adds lane keeping, traffic-light compliance and comfort between consecutive plans. Both policies start from the
supervised policy.
Each decision resets the policy and prefills four observed frames with W = 2. A fixed future-noise realization supplies
the predictive features. The tokenizer, video backbone, backbone ego conditioner and current-image branch remain
frozen. Each scene supplies 32 stochastic paths and one deterministic monitoring plan; the latter is excluded from
advantages and policy losses. The full trajectory expert is trainable.

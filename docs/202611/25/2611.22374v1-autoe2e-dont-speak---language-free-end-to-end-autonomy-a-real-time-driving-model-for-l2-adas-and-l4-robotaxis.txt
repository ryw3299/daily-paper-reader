Abstract
AutoE2E is an open-source end-to-end driving model developed by the Autoware Foundation Robotaxi
Working Group. It introduces a temporal bird’s-eye-view (BEV) architecture that combines multi-camera
perception, HD-map information and route conditioning to predict future vehicle motion. The evaluated
model uses a pretrained camera-BEV backbone, temporal feature fusion and a lightweight recurrent trajec-
tory planner. It contains approximately 79.9 million parameters, of which approximately 1.45 million are
trainable in the evaluated configuration. The v1.0 release provides model checkpoints, training and evalua-
tion code, and DataModelConsole, a dashboard for visualizing and inspecting model experiments. On the
KITScenes validation benchmark, the fine-tuned checkpoint achieves an average displacement error (ADE)
of 0.745 m and a final displacement error (FDE) of 2.020 m at a three-second prediction horizon. The paper
also reports preliminary real-vehicle research conducted with the Technical University of Munich’s EDGAR
platform, alongside ongoing collaboration with the Karlsruhe Institute of Technology. AutoE2E v1.0 is an
early open research release. Its quantitative evaluation is open-loop, and the model has not yet demonstrated
validated closed-loop autonomous driving. The release is intended to provide the autonomous-driving com-
munity with an inspectable baseline for further research, experimentation and collaborative development.

1 Introduction
The Autoware Foundation’s Robotaxi Working Group is developing an open-source, three-band architecture for
autonomous driving (Fig. 1): a reactive band that maps sensing directly to control, a reasoning band, and a
world-action band. The three bands are intended to be interfaced together once each is mature, not trained
as one monolithic network; development starts with the reactive band, the one expected to change fastest,
and is designed so that reasoning and world-action components can be interfaced onto it as they come online.
AutoE2E is that reactive band.
It is designed to be compact and inspectable rather than to lead on raw
capability: a deterministic policy – it outputs one trajectory per input, with no sampling at inference, unlike
the generative vision-language-action (VLA) planners it is benchmarked against – with 79.9 M total parameters
(1.45 M trainable) that reaches up to 76 FPS on a single consumer GPU (Sec. 5.7), orders of magnitude smaller
and faster than those planners.

A reasoning trace is inspectable in a way a raw trajectory is not, but only if the trace is shown to correspond to
the action actually taken – a check the VLA literature mostly assumes rather than runs. VLA planners couple a
vision encoder and a language-model backbone to produce trajectories together with a reasoning trace, typically

through a three-stage recipe of frozen-encoder projector pretraining, supervised fine-tuning on driving data with
teacher-generated chain-of-thought annotations, and a reinforcement post-training stage that aligns the policy
with closed-loop or preference reward. Representative systems in this family include Qwen-Drive-1.0 [1], which
unifies 3D perception, driving VQA, and motion planning on a pretrained vision-language model while leaving
its weights untouched, and Alpamayo [2], built on a reasoning-focused backbone and intended primarily as
a distillation teacher; the common bottleneck across the family is the gap between research-scale capability
and on-vehicle inference budget, addressed almost universally by teacher-student distillation rather than by
training a deployable model directly. AutoE2E sits at the opposite end of this design space: a deterministic,
non-generative model with a fixed camera-BEV backbone, no language component, and no sampling at inference
– and where the working group’s roadmap does add a future-prediction capability (Sec. 7), it is specified as
a JEPA-style latent-feature objective, predicting the camera pathway’s own future embeddings, rather than a
token sequence a language model decodes. What AutoE2E gains is a system whose every claim in this paper can
be checked against source code and a fixed input/output contract rather than against a reasoning trace whose
faithfulness is unverified; what it gives up is the open-vocabulary instruction following and explicit reasoning
output the VLA family offers.
One methodological commitment runs through this paper and is worth stating up front. End-to-end (E2E)
driving research has developed largely around camera-only open-loop evaluation on nuScenes [11]; UniAD [3] and
VAD [7] demonstrated planning-oriented integrated stacks in this setting, with temporal BEV representations
such as BEVFormer [4] providing a shared basis. Deployed vehicle policies, however, often receive more than
cameras. AutoE2E’s deployment contract supplies an HD-map raster and a selected-route raster at every step
in addition to camera images. Evaluating this policy only on a camera-only benchmark reports behavior under
a condition that omits information the policy was designed to consume; reporting only the map-and-route
condition would conversely hide behavior when navigation inputs are unavailable. These evaluations answer
different questions and are kept separate throughout this paper.
1.1 Related Work
End-to-end driving policies share a common design space – how much of the perception stack to fold into one
network, whether language is part of the model, how temporal information is represented, how a map or route
reaches the policy, and how the result is evaluated – and AutoE2E’s choices in that space only mean something
read against it.
Planning-oriented end-to-end driving. UniAD [3] links detection, tracking, mapping, motion forecast-
ing, occupancy prediction, and planning through shared query interaction, so that every task contributes to
the final plan. VAD [7] keeps the same chain but replaces the raster scene with a vectorized one to cut its
computational cost, and TransFuser [8][9] fuses camera and LiDAR through a transformer and evaluates the
result in closed-loop CARLA, a different evaluation setting from the open-loop one this paper uses. AutoE2E
makes a narrower commitment: it does not reproduce the detection/tracking/mapping/occupancy chain, only
the idea of a shared BEV representation feeding a planner, plus explicit navigation context and auxiliary route
reconstruction. It has not been compared against UniAD on a common benchmark, and no claim of superiority
or inferiority is made.
Temporal BEV representations. How a temporal fusion strategy folds history into the current frame
determines what the resulting representation can be attributed to: a given moment, a window, or an unbounded
past.
BEVFormer [4] aggregates multi-camera features into a BEV representation spatiotemporally, using
learnable BEV queries and deformable attention derived from Deformable DETR [6]. BEVFormer v2 [5] adapts
modern image backbones to BEV perception through perspective supervision, and releases an official ResNet-50
checkpoint together with a temporal configuration that fuses multi-frame BEV by convolution – an explicit,
bounded window rather than a recurrent state carried indefinitely. AutoE2E’s frozen camera pathway starts
from this official checkpoint (Sec. 2).
Map- and route-conditioned planning. ChauffeurNet [14] rendered the road map and the intended
route as rasters for imitation learning; VectorNet [15] and LaneGCN [16] encoded HD maps as vectors and
lane graphs, showing that map structure governs motion-forecasting accuracy. The UniAD-family baselines
evaluated on the KITScenes [12] E2E benchmark are conditioned on discrete navigation commands (turn left,
turn right, go straight); the route used here is instead a lane-sequence corridor over a Lanelet2 [13] map with
a destination marker, fitted after the fact to the trajectory the model is then scored against (Sec. 2). That
provenance is precisely why route conditioning cannot be credited with an accuracy gain from this protocol
alone; isolating its effect requires a same-scene experiment not yet run (Sec. 6).
Evaluation protocols and benchmarks. Open-loop planning evaluation on nuScenes has repeatedly
been shown to admit shortcuts by which high scores are obtained without genuine driving competence [17, 18].
Closed-loop evaluation in nuPlan [10], non-reactive simulation in NAVSIM [20], the finding that rule-based
planners can rival learned ones under the standard protocol [19], and conditioning-signal shortcut biases [21]
are different instances of the same finding: change what the evaluation condition reveals or withholds, and

open-loop setting and adopts three design principles that follow from this literature: align the evaluation setup
with the deployment contract, distinguish deterministic overlay replay (which does not re-run the model) from
checkpoint-internal validation (which does), and do not read differences between distinct scene populations as
causal effects.

2.1 Deployment Contract
At every frame, the deployed AutoE2E policy receives: six-view camera images (the current frame plus 7 history
frames at 0.5 s intervals, each 512×512), a 1024×1024 front camera image for the current frame, ego-centered
HD-map rasters (14 channels) and a selected-route raster (2 channels), and 6.4 s of ego-motion history at 10 Hz.
The output is 6.4 s of acceleration and curvature at 10 Hz. The primary evaluation is conducted under the
condition in which all of these inputs are available; because situations in which the map or the route is missing
can occur in operation, behavior from cameras alone is measured as a separate track (Sec. 5).

2.2 Phase 1: Temporal Camera BEV Baseline
The baseline AutoE2E configuration – the architecture the working group started from – is the frozen BEV-
Former V2 T8 temporal camera pathway alone, with no HD-map or route input: six camera views per frame,
fused temporally across eight timesteps, feeding directly into the control planner (Sec. 2.4).
The camera pathway builds on the official BEVFormer V2 R50 T8 checkpoint [5]. A four-level, 256-channel
feature pyramid is formed from the last three stages of ResNet-50 [22], and a six-layer BEVFormer encoder
updates 300×200 learned BEV queries (256-dimensional, 15.36 M parameters). The BEV grid covers X from
−60 m to 120 m and Y from −60 m to 60 m in ego coordinates, at 0.6 m cells. Each layer consists of deformable
self-attention over two queues obtained by duplicating the current BEV (8 heads, 4 points each), multi-scale
spatial cross-attention that maps reference points at four pillar heights (z ∈[−5, 3] m) into the images through a
calibrated projection operator (8 heads, 4 levels, 8 points each), a 512-dimensional FFN, and three LayerNorms.
The projection operator implements pinhole and fisheye (F-theta) models, so this evaluation is not calibration-
free.
In the T8 temporal configuration, seven history frames at 0.5 s intervals (t−3.5 s to t−0.5 s) and the current
frame are passed through the same backbone and encoder, with gradients detached for the history BEVs. The
eight BEVs are concatenated into 2048 channels, passed through a three-block ResNet-style fusion module with
512 intermediate channels, and projected to 256 channels to obtain Bimg:
Bimg = T (B−7, B−6, . . . , B−1, B0) .

2.3 Phase 2: Map- and Route-Conditioned Extensions
The working group’s contribution on top of the baseline above is a navigation pathway that encodes an HD-
map raster and a route raster separately, and fuses them into the camera BEV through a gated residual and
deformable cross-attention. This is the current working version of AutoE2E, and the one all results in this paper
are reported for.

Fig. 5 shows how these rasters are produced upstream of the model.
The KITScenes route is the lane
sequence obtained by fitting the ego trajectory over the whole scene to the Lanelet2 map, and the destination
is the ego position at the end of the scene. The future ego points themselves are not drawn, but the route is
a retrospectively determined sequence of already-driven lanes – that is, it equals the intent the driver actually
followed. Because this fact bears directly on how route conditioning should be interpreted, we return to it in
Sec. 6.
The static map and the selected route have separate encoders (Fig. 6). The map encoder Emap is a fully
convolutional network in which a 5×5 convolutional stem (96 channels) and a context path reduced to strides 2
and 4 by depthwise convolutions (192 channels) are aligned to 256 channels by 1×1 projections and added, with
the input bilinearly resampled to 300×200. The route encoder Eroute is a lightweight convolutional network
with 96 hidden channels, designed so the thin route corridor and destination features are not lost to coarse
pooling. The route contribution is controlled by a 256-dimensional per-channel gate and added to the encoded
map:
Bnav = Emap(M) + σ(g) ⊙Eroute(R).
(2)
The gate g is zero-initialized, so its weight at the start of training is 0.5.
The navigation BEV is fused into the camera BEV by deformable cross-attention [6]:
Bfused = Bimg + Fdeform (Bimg, Bnav) .
(3)
Each image-BEV cell acts as a query, predicts 8 offsets from its own position as the reference point, bilinearly
samples Bnav, and aggregates the samples with 8-head softmax weights – reducing what would be O(N2) for
dense cross-attention over 60,000 cells to O(NK). Because the output projection and the final FFN linear layer
are zero-initialized, the fusion does not alter the representation of the pretrained camera pathway at the start
of training, acting as a stabilization mechanism when attaching new navigation features to a frozen visual BEV
representation.

2.5 Model Size
Table 2 and Fig. 8 report parameter counts obtained by instantiating the model in the evaluated six-view
configuration. In total-capacity terms, the model is almost entirely a pretrained, frozen visual representation:

roughly two orders of magnitude smaller than the ∼4 B-parameter and ∼10 B-parameter vision-language-action
planners it is benchmarked against (Sec. 5) – a deliberately light footprint for real-time, on-vehicle inference
rather than research-scale capability.

3 Training
3.1 Datasets
Two datasets anchor training and evaluation. nuPlan [10], a large-scale, multi-city closed-loop planning bench-
mark, is used for the initial trajectory-and-route training stage with the camera BEV backbone frozen at its
official pretrained weights. KITScenes [12], a multimodal European driving dataset from the Karlsruhe Insti-
tute of Technology (KIT) pairing high-fidelity camera and sensor data with complete Lanelet2 [13] HD maps, is
used to fine-tune the nuPlan-trained checkpoint to the Karlsruhe road network and camera configuration, and
for the primary external evaluation (Sec. 5). Both datasets carry their own licenses, which the released check-
point weights inherit; the model architecture, training code, and evaluation pipeline are Apache-2.0 regardless
of which dataset trained a given checkpoint.
The split manifest identifies the KITScenes fine-tuning source as the official training split: 129 empty scenes
are excluded from 533 available train scenes, leaving 404 eligible scenes and 42,667 samples.
A scene-level
90%/10% split yields 38,847 samples from 364 scenes for training and 3,820 samples from 40 scenes for frozen
internal validation. Because these 404 scenes belong to the official training split, their scene IDs are disjoint
from the official 117-scene Val and 206-scene Test populations used for evaluation.

3.2 How It Was Trained
Training proceeds in two stages (Fig. 9). The baseline stage trains the trajectory and route pathways on nuPlan
with the camera BEV frozen at its official pretrained weights: 8 distributed GPU workers, seed 149, learning rate
1e-4, trajectory weight 1.0, route-reconstruction weight 1.0, BEV-segmentation weight 0.0, and 1,024 internal
validation samples. The resulting checkpoint is released as nuplan-epoch-5. The fine-tuning stage then freezes
that checkpoint as parent and continues training on KITScenes at learning rate 3e-5, with the same 8-worker,
seed-149, loss-weight configuration, over the 38,847-sample / 364-scene training split described above, validated
internally on 3,820 samples / 40 scenes in BF16 precision.
The result is released as kitscenes-epoch-5,
the checkpoint fine-tuned for the Karlsruhe road network and camera configuration, and the one reported as
the primary result throughout Sec. 5. An optional BEV-only training stage (unfrozen camera BEV, BEV-
segmentation loss) exists in the code path; its use as parent of the released baseline checkpoint is not recorded
in the available metadata. Neither released checkpoint passed the repository’s automated checkpoint quality

3.3 Where It Was Trained
Training runs on an EKS Auto Mode Kubernetes cluster on AWS, chosen over a standard EKS node group
plus Karpenter setup for its built-in CNI, autoscaler, and load-balancer controller, which removes most of
the day-two operational overhead of running GPU infrastructure.
Imitation learning uses Ray Train jobs
admitted by Kueue’s GPU-quota scheduler onto p5en.48xlarge Capacity Block reservations (4- or 8-GPU
jobs, whole-GPU allocation to preserve intra-instance communication), with a smaller retained g6.2xlarge
pool reserved for inference, validation, and canary jobs. Closed-loop simulation was evaluated and deliberately
not adopted for this release: CARLA’s Vulkan dependency is incompatible with the Bottlerocket AMI that
EKS Auto Mode’s GPU nodes run, so the working group’s closed-loop training path instead goes through
offline reinforcement learning (Implicit Q-Learning) on the same WebDataset shards used for imitation learning,
requiring no simulator. Closed-loop evaluation is pursued separately, through NVIDIA’s AlpaSim; its first result,
a diagnosed closed-loop failure mode rather than a passing validation, is reported in Sec. 7. The likely launch
configuration for training the released checkpoints, traced from the training branch, is a single p5en.48xlarge
(8×H200) or p5.48xlarge (8×H100) Capacity Block, 8-way distributed, consistent with the epoch, learning-
rate, and seed values above; this is an inferred hardware configuration, not a recorded one, and total training
wall-clock time and GPU-hours are not available in the evidence used for this paper. Every stage – data ingest,
imitation-learning training, open-loop evaluation gating, and offline-RL refinement – logs experiments, metrics,
and artifacts to MLflow, orchestrated as Flyte workflows; both are deployed on the same cluster and reachable
through internal, Cognito-authenticated CloudFront endpoints rather than the public internet.

5.1 Population and Protocol
The primary benchmark is the KITScenes validation split, where Camera + HD Map + Route are all available,
computed over the 117 scenes and 11,035 samples that satisfy strict identity with the official Map and Route
evaluation. Because the KITScenes test split does not release maps, it is evaluated as a camera-only robustness
and transfer track over all 206 scenes and 23,690 samples. Since Val and Test are different scene populations,
the Test-versus-Val difference is not interpreted as a causal effect of the map and route. The external evaluation
is a replay of the published deterministic control overlays; it does not re-run the model and contains no route

accuracy: Val and Test are different scenes, Test by construction has no map and no route, this report contains
no camera-only baseline on the same scenes as Val, and per-scene paired errors have not been computed. What
can be said is behavioral: route-corridor compliance (the fraction of steps a 2.0 m footprint remains inside the
3.5 m route corridor) is 0.40–0.42 and success 0.06–0.08 under this strict definition, and a route reconstruction
IoU of 0.999 verifies that the route information survives the gate, without being evidence the policy drove along
the route.

5.7 Inference Cost and Real-Vehicle Evidence
Across a sweep of backbone and fusion configurations, inference reaches up to 76 FPS with a SwinV2-Tiny back-
bone and feature-concat fusion (RTX 5080, batch 1) – a lighter-weight variant benchmarked for throughput, not
the ResNet-50 configuration evaluated in Sec. 5. Both configurations have been benchmarked across consumer
and workstation GPUs (RTX 5000 Quadro, A40, RTX 4070/4080/5090); a controlled latency measurement of
the exact evaluated ResNet-50 configuration on this same GPU sweep is not available in the evidence used for
this paper. The only concrete latency figure for that configuration comes from TU Munich’s EDGAR platform
(Sec. 8): 101 ms on a single A6000 GPU, for a checkpoint TUM retrained on its own EDGAR data rather
than the KITScenes-trained checkpoints benchmarked above – reported there as a preliminary, single-platform
perspective, not a closed validation.
6 Limitations
This paper documents an early snapshot of an actively developed reactive band, released at this stage so the
working group’s evaluation protocol and baseline evidence are open to scrutiny and reuse rather than held
back until every gap below is closed; Sec. 7 describes the concrete improvements already planned. AutoE2E is
currently an open-loop model: it is not designed as a closed-loop policy, has not been evaluated under closed-
loop safety or intervention metrics, and future versions are intended to be trained and evaluated on real vehicles
directly rather than assumed safe to deploy on the basis of the open-loop numbers in this paper.
The evaluation in this paper is open-loop only and includes no closed-loop safety or intervention metrics.
No controlled map/route experiment on identical scenes was conducted. The external replay is limited to 5 s,
so 22% of the 6.4 s output is not evaluated. Val and Test are different populations, and the difference between
them carries no causal meaning. The camera encoder is frozen, and the degree to which a representation trained
on nuScenes fits the six-camera configuration and 0.6 m grid of KITScenes has not been separately evaluated.
The full UniAD task stack is not included. All checkpoint quality gates are recorded as not passed. The training
data are nuPlan and KITScenes from a small number of German and multi-city scenes, so distribution shifts
in geography, season, and map may exist. Because the route is a post-hoc sequence of already-driven lanes,
robustness to route errors is not measured. Under the KITScenes benchmark protocol, only 4.0 s of the 6.4 s
ego-motion history is real data, leaving a distribution gap with respect to training.
Deployment-aligned evaluation should retain the navigation inputs the policy requires, camera-only eval-
uation should be positioned as a complementary robustness track rather than the primary benchmark, and
the real-vehicle and cross-model results of this paper should be read as preliminary evidence pending the full
same-scene ablation, not as settled claims.
7 Future Work and Outlook
AutoE2E is the reactive band of a three-band architecture planned for the Autoware Foundation’s open-source
autonomous-driving stack: a reactive band (this paper), a reasoning band, and a world-action band, decoupled
rather than trained as one monolithic network. This paper is explicitly scoped to the frozen-camera-backbone
version of AutoE2E described in Sec. 2; unfreezing the camera BEV pathway for joint fine-tuning is left to
future work and is not evaluated here. Concretely, the working group’s roadmap includes:
• Unfreezing the camera BEV backbone for joint fine-tuning with the navigation and control pathways,
expected to require its own evaluation protocol rather than a direct comparison with the frozen-backbone
results reported here.
• Replacing the HD-map dependency with a navigation map.
The current deployment contract
requires an HD map; a navigation- or SD-map-conditioned variant would relax this requirement toward the
maps actually available at scale.
• Closed-loop evaluation and training in a photorealistic world model, rather than the open-loop imitation
setting evaluated throughout this paper. A first closed-loop integration with NVIDIA’s AlpaSim platform
has been run: the baseline checkpoint completed five public NuRec scenes without collision, but left the road
in four of five within 2.5–4.1 s of takeover. A replay ablation traced this to the model’s own yaw-rate history
feeding back closed-loop (a “copycat” failure also seen in other imitation-learned policies, not a configuration
bug) – zeroing that history at replay keeps the logged plan within about 2◦of straight even where the live
rollout had already drifted 5–10◦off-lane. The next steps are a closed-loop run with that history zeroed at
inference, and training with less dependence on it (dropout, noise, or a shorter history).

• Activating the reasoning and world-action bands, Fig. 1’s JEPA-style future-feature prediction in-
cluded, so that the reactive policy evaluated here can be coupled with a higher-level reasoning band and a
learned world model rather than being extended into a single larger network.
• Real-world vehicle deployment, extending the preliminary results on TUM’s EDGAR platform (Sec. 8)
to closed-loop driving on KIT’s test vehicle Joy in German inner-city traffic (Sec. 8), toward a road-ready
rather than demo-grade system.
8 Collaborations
Technical University of Munich (TUM) – EDGAR Research Platform
A preliminary real-vehicle result, coordinated with the working group
with TUM’s EDGAR team, is reported here as a perspective on where
this work goes next – not a closed validation.
The full experimental
methodology, including whether every compared model received the same
adaptation to EDGAR data, has not yet been independently confirmed
with TUM, and the result is single-platform; read it accordingly. Con-
cretely:
• Real-vehicle retraining: TUM trained AutoE2E from scratch on 134 shards (∼27,000 samples) of its
own EDGAR vehicle data, reaching 0.430 m ADE and 1.224 m FDE at 3 s – against a constant-velocity
reference of 0.955 m / 2.605 m on the same data – within a few hours of training by the working group’s
account.
• Efficiency evidence: inference on a single A6000 GPU is 101 ms at 0.5 GiB memory. Compared zero-
shot on the same EDGAR data, Alpamayo 1.5 reaches ∼0.99 m (segment 1) and ∼0.64 m (segment 2)
ADE at 3 s, and SimForge-D1.5 reaches ∼1.00 m and ∼0.76 m across the same two segments, both at
roughly 800 ms inference and 14–21 GiB memory – the first evidence that AutoE2E’s parameter budget
and architecture translate into a real latency and memory advantage on vehicle hardware, not only on
the open-loop benchmarks of Sec. 5.

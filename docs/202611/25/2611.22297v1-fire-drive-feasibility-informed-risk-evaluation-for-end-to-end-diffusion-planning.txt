Abstract—Diffusion-based
end-to-end
autonomous
driving
models have shown strong potential in generating multimodal
planning trajectories. However, their performance still depends
heavily on trajectory initialization, mode evaluation, and su-
pervision quality. Weakly constrained noisy initialization may
introduce infeasible proposals and increase the burden of de-
noising. Moreover, selecting the optimal mode remains chal-
lenging when candidate trajectories are geometrically similar
but differ in safety, interaction risk, and progress. To address
these issues, we propose FIRE-Drive, a feasibility-informed risk
evaluation framework for end-to-end autonomous driving. FIRE-
Drive improves diffusion-based planning through dynamic fea-
sible anchor initialization, candidate-specific bird’s-eye-view risk
evidence, and compact high-quality candidate supervision with
lightweight refinement. This design aligns the initial support,
risk-aware scoring, and multimodal supervision of trajectory
modes under a unified planning objective. It enables the diffusion
decoder to focus on refining plausible trajectories while the scorer
distinguishes modes using local risk evidence. Experiments on
NAVSIM v1 show that FIRE-Drive achieves a Predictive Driver
Model Score of 91.5 with a ResNet34 backbone. Additional
closed-loop evaluation on 220 Bench2Drive routes yields a Driving
Score of 71.43 and a Success Rate of 42.73%. Component
ablations and a scorer comparison under matched candidate in-
puts support the contributions of feasible initialization, auxiliary
supervision, and risk-informed selection. Our code is available
at https://github.com/Latte00/FIRE-drive.

A central difficulty, however, is that human driving futures
are inherently multimodal: the same traffic scene may admit
multiple plausible maneuvers, interaction patterns, and risk-
efficiency trade-offs [3]. Therefore, an effective E2E planner
should not only generate diverse future trajectories, but also
ensure that the generated candidates are feasible, distinguish-
able, and properly supervised.
A common planning strategy constructs a trajectory vocab-
ulary or proposal pool and then selects a plan using learned
scores [4], [5], as illustrated in Fig. 1. This separates candidate
coverage from decision making, but also creates two coupled
requirements: the pool must contain useful alternatives, and the
scorer must identify them. A selector cannot recover a feasible
maneuver absent from its candidate set, while geometrically
similar candidates may require different decisions because
of their local collision exposure or road-boundary proximity.
Candidate generation and evaluation should therefore be de-
signed together.

eration through denoising [6], [7], but this flexibility leaves
three challenges. First, fixed anchors or generic perturbations
need not respect the current drivable region, obstacle layout, or
ego-motion reachability, leaving the decoder to correct scene-
inconsistent starting points. Physical priors help structure
trajectory modeling [8], yet scene-dependent admissibility also
matters. Second, geometric plausibility alone does not deter-
mine driving quality: similar trajectories can differ in safety
and progress, requiring candidate-specific risk evidence for
selection. Third, supervision from a single logged future pro-
vides limited guidance for alternative maneuvers. Additional
references are useful only when their diversity is accompanied
by scene compatibility and adequate driving quality.
The distinction between motion plausibility and scene fea-
sibility is particularly important at initialization. A trajectory
can be smooth and consistent with the current vehicle state
while entering occupied space or extending beyond a drivable
boundary. Conversely, an unoccupied road region may be
difficult to reach under the current speed and steering limits.
A useful initial candidate should account for both restrictions.
This motivates a scene-dependent admissible region that com-
bines semantic drivability with ego-motion reachability, rather
than relying on a motion prior alone to locate the starting
hypotheses.
Selection presents a related but different problem. Consider
two candidates that follow nearly the same lane but differ
slightly in lateral clearance or longitudinal progress. Their
trajectory embeddings may be similar, yet one can pass closer
to a vehicle or road boundary. The relevant evidence is
therefore not just what objects exist in the scene, but where
each candidate travels relative to them. Candidate-conditioned
risk context makes these spatial relationships available to the
scorer, while explicit risk supervision encourages attention to
the regions that explain potential infractions.
The interplay between training data and learning strategies
is central to E2E driving [9]. Additional supervision must
therefore preserve the distinction between possible and useful
behavior. The logged future provides a demonstrated response,
but does not enumerate all acceptable maneuvers. Geometric
variation can supply alternatives, although larger variation does
not necessarily imply better supervision. We therefore treat
reference construction as a quality-control step: behavioral
alternatives are screened against scene geometry and evaluator
scores before they become training targets. Caching this com-
pact reference set separates its construction cost from repeated
training updates.
We propose FIRE-Drive to address these challenges through
coordinated initialization, evaluation, and supervision. Scene-
adaptive anchor priors are constrained by semantic drivability
and ego-motion reachability before denoising. A candidate-
conditioned bird’s-eye-view (BEV) risk context supplies local
evidence for trajectory selection. Compact, quality-filtered
auxiliary references supervise secondary modes and support
lightweight refinement; their trajectories and evaluator scores
are cached offline for reuse. Together, these components
improve the starting hypotheses, the training targets, and the
evidence used to choose the final plan.
The contributions of this paper are summarized as follows:
• We introduce a dynamic feasible anchor strategy that
replaces weakly constrained seeds with scene-consistent
trajectory priors constructed under semantic drivability
and motion-reachability constraints.
• We propose a mode risk context module that combines
candidate-conditioned BEV evidence, semantic risk bias,
and infraction supervision to support safety-aware trajec-
tory selection.
• We formulate a compact procedure for high-quality can-
didate supervision, combining behavior-based reference
generation, geometry and quality filtering, and offline
caching to supervise secondary modes without repeated
reference evaluation.
• FIRE-Drive achieves 91.5 PDMS on NAVSIM v1 and
71.43 DS with 42.73% SR over 220 Bench2Drive routes.

II. RELATED WORK
A. E2E Autonomous Driving
E2E driving has evolved from direct image-to-control im-
itation [10]–[12] toward structured systems that learn per-
ception and planning jointly [1], [13]. Closed-loop simula-
tion exposes the consequences of prediction errors beyond
individual frames [14], motivating trajectory-guided control
and iterative decoding [15], [16]. Richer sensor fusion and
temporal reasoning improve the scene evidence available to the
planner [17], [18], while vectorized and sparse representations
reduce dependence on dense scene processing [2], [19], [20].
Another direction strengthens the relationship between pre-
dicted interactions and ego decisions through joint optimiza-
tion, hierarchical prediction, or game-theoretic reasoning [21]–
[23]. Unified transformer architectures further facilitate infor-
mation exchange across driving tasks [24]. However, richer
representations and tighter task coupling alone do not ensure
that every generated mode is feasible or receives useful super-
vision. Studies of PDM and PlanTF also show that good open-
loop fitting does not directly translate into reliable closed-
loop behavior [25], [26]. This motivates distinguishing scene
representation from the use of that representation in planning.
Even with a shared perception backbone, different initial
candidate supports, training targets, and selection criteria can
lead to different driving decisions. FIRE-Drive addresses these
proposal-level choices within a diffusion planner, complement-
ing advances in scene encoding and interaction modeling.
B. Diffusion Models for Trajectory Prediction and Planning
Denoising-based generation [27], [28] provides a flexible
representation of multimodal trajectories and actions [29],
[30]. Its application to driving spans multi-agent forecasting
and joint prediction–planning [31], [32]. Truncated diffusion,
goal-driven flow matching, and diffusion bridges adapt this
formulation to E2E trajectory generation [6], [7], [33]. Their
flexibility makes feasibility control important: representing
multiple futures does not by itself make those futures admis-
sible in the current scene.

Physical structure enters existing methods in different
forms: ResAD predicts normalized residuals around a de-
terministic inertial reference [8], and WAKE integrates ve-
hicle kinematics into trajectory prediction [34]. Diffusion-
Phy adopts a multi-stage physical-prior pipeline involving
dynamic-state prediction, neural compensation, and latent-
state encoding to construct adaptive anchors [35]. FIRE-Drive
adopts a simpler initialization design, emphasizing the spatial
admissibility of the denoising seeds through explicit semantic–
reachability constraints, while leaving detailed trajectory shap-
ing to the decoder and refiner. Specifically, the intersection
of semantic and reachable regions restricts sampled anchor
locations, beyond using physical features to condition their
distribution. Other approaches enforce constraints during flow
generation [36] or use reward-based evolutionary search [37];
our design places the spatial restriction before decoding.
Reward-based learning provides another route to improv-
ing candidate quality. DIVER combines multiple references
and one-to-one matching with GRPO rewards for diversity
and driving quality [38]; DiffusionDriveV2 uses anchor-aware
GRPO and a trajectory-conditioned selector [39]. Their pol-
icy updates involve trajectory sampling and reward evalu-
ation, adding computationally demanding online evaluation
to training. FIRE-Drive uses offline-cached, quality-filtered
references as reusable supervised targets for secondary modes.
This avoids repeated reward evaluation for auxiliary reference
supervision, while retaining online infraction evidence for risk-
context training.
C. Mode Evaluation and Multimodal Supervision
Learning to rank proposals is increasingly important as
candidate generation improves. Scorer distillation and dedi-
cated trajectory representations learn to distinguish candidate
quality [4], [5], [40], [41], while recent proposal-centric sys-
tems further emphasize the interaction between generation and
selection [42]–[44]. Future-scene modeling and safety-oriented
value estimation provide additional ways to assess downstream
outcomes [45], [46]. Explicit risk fields and risk-aware op-
timization connect spatial hazard assessment with trajectory
planning [47], [48], while risk-aware prediction incorporates
safety-critical interactions into future motion estimation [49].
FIRE-Drive brings spatial risk evidence into learned candi-
date scoring: candidate-conditioned BEV context, semantic
risk bias, and infraction supervision guide the scorer toward
hazards relevant to each trajectory, beyond aggregate score
regression alone. The matched-input scorer ablation supports
the benefit of this risk evidence independently of changes in
candidate quality.
A separate issue is what supervises the alternatives being
scored. Learned latent anchors and multimodal aggregation
improve the representation of possible futures [50], and mode
assignment directs supervision to predictions compatible with
the observed future [51], [52]. However, a single observa-
tion cannot specify the quality of every alternative maneu-
ver. Multi-reference supervision broadens the available tar-
gets [53], but reference diversity alone does not establish scene
feasibility or driving utility. FIRE-Drive explicitly screens
auxiliary references by geometry and evaluator quality, then
uses candidate matching and bounded refinement to supervise
secondary modes. This connects broader supervision to a
compact set of evaluated alternatives.

The content term in attention measures compatibility be-

term increases the salience of potentially hazardous regions.

Because the context is fused before score decoding, the scorer
can learn how this evidence relates to each predicted quality
component rather than receiving only a fixed penalty after
prediction. The score remains a learned assessment of the
complete candidate.
The auxiliary loss Lattn aligns attention with online colli-
sion and off-road evidence. Event locations are projected onto
BEV and represented by Gaussian targets, with exponentially
decaying temporal weights bounded below by a minimum
weight. After normalization, the targets supervise predicted
attention through target-to-prediction KL divergence. Gaussian
width, decay, minimum weight, and loss strength are hyperpa-
rameters. This supplies localized risk supervision in addition
to trajectory-score targets.
Scalar quality labels indicate whether a candidate is desir-
able, but do not identify which spatial region accounts for a
collision or boundary violation. The event-based attention tar-
gets provide this localization. Gaussian spreading gives nearby
BEV cells a graded training signal, and temporal weighting
emphasizes earlier events while retaining a contribution from
later ones. Risk bias and attention supervision therefore serve
different roles: one guides feature aggregation, while the other
trains that aggregation using observed infractions.
D. High-Quality Candidate Supervision and Refinement
Reference construction. Left-overtake, right-overtake, and
acceleration warping operators construct auxiliary trajectories
from the logged future. Up to five references and their
evaluator scores are cached per scene. With C denoting the
non-padding pool and G the geometry-validity indicator, the
effective set is
C+ = {Cm ∈C : G(Cm) = 1, Q(Cm) > Q(Y gt)}.
(7)
Geometry checks limit the absolute wrapped heading change
between adjacent waypoints to 1.5 rad and allow at most
one sign reversal in lateral increments; the separate heading-
reversal count filter is disabled. On NAVSIM, Q is the cached
PDM evaluator score, distinct from the learned scorer S.
References and scores are prepared offline and reused during
training; neither is needed at inference.
The score comparison is scene-specific: each reference is
compared with the logged trajectory under the same cached
evaluator. Consequently, the number of effective references
varies across scenes even though the cache has a fixed
maximum capacity. A scene with no qualifying reference
still provides the ordinary GT supervision. The additional
targets extend supervision only where the cached pool offers
an eligible alternative, rather than forcing every scene to
contribute the same number of secondary targets.
Candidate matching. Excluding the GT-assigned mode,
we sort candidate–mode pairs by mean waypoint distance in
the xy plane and greedily accept pairs whose candidate and
mode have not been used. The loss Lcand averages distances
over accepted pairs. Empty sets skip this auxiliary loss while
retaining the primary training objectives. Candidate matching
remains active without refinement and then supervises decoder
outputs directly.
Bounded refinement. A lightweight refiner uses the mode
token and ego encoding to predict residual corrections, clipped

NVIDIA RTX 5090 GPU. The main model is evaluated using
the checkpoint from epoch 40.

E. Candidate Statistics and Qualitative Analysis
Figure 6 summarizes the auxiliary reference pool and illus-
trates representative candidate trajectories. Cached candidates
span a range of evaluator scores. During training, the geometry
and greater-than-GT masks select the subset used for auxiliary
supervision, and scenes with no eligible reference contribute
no candidate-supervision loss. The displayed best-candidate
statistics describe the reference pool before this training-time
selection. Among the 103,288 training scenes, 48.98% contain
at least one effective supervision candidate; the remaining
51.02% skip candidate supervision.
The variable effective-set size reflects quality-based admis-
sion. Strict greater-than-GT filtering excludes ties and cannot
admit references when GT already reaches the evaluator
ceiling. Other scenes may lack a reference satisfying both ge-
ometry and score criteria. Retaining ordinary GT supervision
in these cases avoids forcing arbitrary alternatives into training.
Thus, the raw score distributions characterize the constructed
pool, whereas the post-filter counts quantify its actual contribu-
tion to supervision. These statistics support selective auxiliary
supervision: the cached pool does not provide a qualifying
alternative for every scene. They do not establish that better
trajectories are absent outside this pool, but motivate reusing
validated references where available.
Figure 7 presents three examples in each of four illustra-
tive groups: Congested Scenarios (a), Turning Scenarios (b),
Intersection Scenarios (c), and Common Scenarios (d). These
groups illustrate dense surrounding traffic, changes in road
direction, junction interactions, and routine driving contexts,
respectively. The risk overlays illustrate spatial evidence near

varies with the scene. The highlighted risk responses form

V. CONCLUSIONS
This paper presented FIRE-Drive, a diffusion-based E2E
planner that improves multimodal planning through feasibility-
informed trajectory priors, risk-aware mode evaluation, and
compact candidate-guided supervision. The proposed method
constrains the initial trajectory prior with short-horizon se-
mantic and reachability information, injects mode-conditioned
BEV risk evidence into the scoring branch, and uses high-
quality auxiliary candidates with lightweight refinement to
strengthen supervision for secondary modes. Experiments on
NAVSIM v1 and Bench2Drive show competitive planning per-
formance in non-reactive and reactive evaluation settings. The
matched-input scorer ablation further supports the contribution
of explicit risk evidence to trajectory selection. The short-
horizon initialization constraints leave full-horizon feasibility
dependent on subsequent decoding and selection. The current
single-frame input also limits temporal modeling, while the
auxiliary reference pool covers only a subset of possible
behaviors, particularly in highly interactive cases such as being
overtaken or emergency avoidance. Future work will focus
on temporal modeling and adaptive reference construction for
interactive driving.

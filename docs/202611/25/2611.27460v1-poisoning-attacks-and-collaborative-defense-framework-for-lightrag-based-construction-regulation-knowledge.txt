Poisoning Attacks and Collaborative Defense Framework for
LightRAG-Based Construction Regulation Knowledge Bases: A
Case Study of Current UK Building Standards
Yingyan HE
Faculty of Science and Technology, SWISS UMEF, Geneva, Switzerland
yingyanhe@ieee.org, bulletsho@gmail.com
Master Thesis · November 2026
Abstract
Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) is now widely used across industries, including
architecture, engineering and construction (AEC), and research on RAG security is growing
rapidly; yet the security of building-regulation knowledge bases has received little study. This
thesis therefore reports a pre-registered experiment on one fully instrumented LightRAG
system built on the UK Approved Documents.
Using a 150-item benchmark labeled with
safety consequences and tampering directions, it evaluates four attacks (text layer, graph
layer, fabricated amendment narrative and a defense-aware adaptive attacker) together with
a three-layer retrieval–graph–generation defense in a full factorial design. Text-level poison-
ing succeeded, with success rising as the budget increased. Graph-level poisoning was less
effective, contrary to the registered hypothesis. The full defense met the registered criterion
and six of the seven configurations met its threshold, but those containing the generation
layer did so by refusing 69–80 percent of poisoned queries, at a cost of up to 74 points of
clean accuracy, whereas provenance-based retrieval filtering produced no measurable loss of
clean accuracy in the configuration used. A second experimental batch showed that excluding
a legitimate unregistered amendment caused no measurable loss of accuracy either, because
its wording reached the generator through the graph's entity and relationship descriptions,
the route the poison took, which no layer inspects: the three layers act on text chunks,
anchored relation edges and cited evidence, whereas the descriptions carry no document
provenance. The layers interacted so strongly that the contribution of each cannot be iso-
lated. A reported zero attack success rate is therefore not evidence of detection unless the
refusal rate is reported with it: zero arises either because provenance filtering removes every
document outside the manifest without judging its content, or because the system refuses. In
the registered batch the generation layer never returned a contradiction verdict, only neutral
ones (cannot be confirmed). Layered defenses must therefore be evaluated factorially, and
RAG assistants for regulatory knowledge require corpus admission control and version-aware
verification before they can support safety-critical decisions.
Keywords: Retrieval-augmented generation; Knowledge poisoning; LightRAG; Build-
ing regulations; Defense evaluation
Summary
This thesis examines whether a retrieval-augmented question-answering system for building
regulations can be poisoned, and whether a layered defense can stop such poisoning without
destroying the system's usefulness. The experiments use LightRAG, which answers from text
chunks and from an automatically built knowledge graph, over the UK Approved Documents
arXiv:2611.27460v1  [cs.CR]  20 Nov 2026


and the Manual to the Building Regulations. The question matters in this domain because a
wrong escape-stair width or guarding height arrives in the same confident, well-cited form as a
correct one, and because regulatory corpora are assembled from many sources and updated by
amendment, which gives material of uncertain provenance realistic ways into a knowledge base.
The research question was decomposed into four sub-questions, covering the text layer, the
graph layer, the defense and the unevenness of harms, and ten hypotheses with their tests and
thresholds were registered on 4 October 2026, before the first attack run; the confirmatory
family of seven tests was protected with Holm correction. A 150-item benchmark was written
from the corpus, each item anchored to the provision it rests on and labeled with a safety-
consequence weight and a paired loosening or tightening mutation. A poisoned query counted
as a success only when the answer matched none of the required values, contained the attacker's
value and was not a refusal. Four attacks were implemented: PoisonedRAG-style text injection,
GRAGPoison-style shared-relation poisoning of the graph, a fabricated “amendment booklet”
narrative and a defense-aware adaptive attacker. A three-layer defense (provenance-aware re-
trieval filtering, anchor-based graph verification and NLI citation checking) was evaluated in all
eight combinations of its layers, each configuration run three times.
Text-level poisoning succeeded on 33 of 150 targets at five passages per target, a rate of 0.220
against a registered bound of 0.10 (p = 1.17 × 10−5), and its success rose with the budget,
although the poison was already retrieved every time at a budget of one passage. Graph-level
poisoning succeeded on 22 of 150 targets, the opposite of the registered prediction, so the graph
was not the softer target. The full defense met its registered criterion and six of the seven
configurations met its threshold. However, every configuration containing the NLI layer did so
by refusing 69–80 percent of poisoned queries, at a cost of up to 74 points of clean accuracy,
whereas provenance filtering showed no measurable loss of clean accuracy in the configuration
used, by a margin of only 0.36 points; a registered variant with a different graph-side path
lost 32 points. The layers interacted strongly, the adaptive attacker gained nothing, and the
fabricated amendment notices were repeated in all 18 answers, often in answers that value-based
scoring judged correct.
The harms were also uneven in ways that a single rate hides. Weighting errors by their safety
consequence barely changed the error rate (a mean tilt of +0.005, within its uncertainty), and
neither loosening nor tightening mutations were favored (ten discordant pairs each way). Under
poisoning, strict accuracy fell by 21.6 points while the share of strongly assertive answers fell by
only 4.3 points: wrong answers were stated no less confidently than right ones, so correctness
could not be judged from tone. And on neither of the two items asking which instrument a
value belongs to, in any of the three indexes, did the system name the instrument that governs
it.
A second experimental batch (6–8 November 2026) sharpened these findings.
Excluding a
legitimate amendment from the manifest caused no measurable loss of accuracy, because the
filter withholds only an unregistered document's text chunks, while the amendment's wording
reached the prompt through the graph's entity and relationship descriptions in 14 of the 16
measurable items, the route the poison used. With the graph removed, text-level poisoning
remained effective. Under poisoning, the model's self-rated confidence became markedly less
calibrated. An LLM judge from a different provider, used as an independent second scorer,
agreed with the strict rubric on 50 of 58 explanatory answers.
These results change what a low attack success rate can be taken to mean. Under the defense,
a zero arose in two ways: provenance filtering removed every document outside the manifest
without judging its content, and the generation layer refused; in the registered batch it never
returned a contradiction verdict, only neutral ones. The graph, in turn, proved to be a second
attack surface rather than a second line of defense, because its descriptions carry no document
2


provenance and no layer inspects them.
The thesis concludes that a low attack success rate under defense must be reported together with
the refusal rate and clean accuracy, that a layered defense must be evaluated with a full factorial
design, and that regulatory RAG systems need controlled corpus admission and version-aware
verification. For organizations, this means admitting documents, including oﬀicial amendments,
only through an authenticated and logged process enforced before indexing, requiring answers
to state the instrument, edition and commencement date they rely on, and keeping human
verification for safety-critical values. For researchers, it means reporting the refusal share and
the clean-accuracy cost beside every attack success rate.
These findings describe one system (LightRAG 1.5.8), one serving model, one jurisdiction's
regulations in one language and one curated experimental index; they may not transfer to open
corpora or to systems with different retrieval parameters.

Glossary
Budget M: the number of poison passages injected for one target question (N in PoisonedRAG).
ASR (attack success rate): the share of poisoned queries whose answer omits the correct
value, contains the attacker's value and is not a refusal.
Anchor table: a frozen set of structured assertions (subject, relation, comparator, value, unit)
extracted from the authoritative text and used by the graph-side defense.
Disruption: removal of the correct value from the answer, whether or not the attacker's value
replaced it.
ECE (expected calibration error): the share-weighted gap between stated confidence and
observed accuracy across confidence levels (Equation 4.11).
LLM judge: a second, independent scorer that rates each answer's consistency with the pro-
vision on a 1–5 scale without seeing the rubric or the experimental condition (Section 4.10).
LightRAG: an open-source graph-based RAG framework that retrieves from text chunks and
from an automatically extracted entity–relation graph.
NLI (Natural Language Inference): A model that classifies whether a premise entails,
contradicts, or is neutral to a hypothesis; used here to check the relationship between an answer
sentence and its cited evidence.
Plain accuracy: accuracy over all records, with a refusal counted as a wrong answer.
Strict accuracy: accuracy over answered records only.
Retreat: a generation-layer outcome in which the answer is withheld or replaced by a conser-

vative reply because the NLI checker cannot confirm it against the cited evidence; the retreat
rate is the share of records whose third-layer check triggered a retreat.
Chapter 1: Introduction and Background
1.1 Research Topics and Key Points
Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed to answer questions about technical
regulation, usually through retrieval-augmented generation (RAG): the system retrieves pas-
sages relevant to the query and conditions the generator on them, so that the answer can be
tied to a source. For building regulations, which are long, frequently revised and written in a
register where a single value carries legal weight, a system that can point to the paragraph it
relied on is easier to trust and audit.
The same property creates an attack surface: whatever the retriever can reach, the generator
reads as evidence. An adversary who can place documents into the store needs no access to the
model, its prompt or its operator. PoisonedRAG showed that a handful of such passages can
redirect a RAG system's answers to attacker-chosen targets (W. Zou et al., 2025).
This thesis studies that problem in one concrete and safety-relevant setting. Its object is Ligh-
tRAG, an open-source RAG framework that combines vector retrieval over text chunks with an
automatically constructed entity–relation graph (Z. Guo et al., 2025); the source code used is
listed in Appendix B. Its corpus is the Approved Documents published by the UK government,
together with the government's Manual to the Building Regulations. It asks how an adversary
who can contribute documents can make such a system return a wrong value for a specific
provision, and whether a defense spread across retrieval, graph and generation can prevent that
without destroying its usefulness for honest queries.
1.2 Motivation
In practice, the architecture, engineering and construction (AEC) sector has begun to adopt
LLM-based tools for regulatory work. Uhm et al. (2025) evaluated retrieval-augmented LLMs
for generating construction safety information and found retrieval to improve the reliability
of the output; Shi et al. (2025) combined a fine-tuned LLM with retrieval to turn building
rules into draft scripts for automated compliance checking; and J. Chen et al. (2026) adapted
general-purpose LLMs to building-code analysis by instruction tuning rather than domain-
specific pre-training. The same pattern is visible in security operations, where RAG now sup-
ports vulnerability risk perception and penetration testing (Gu et al., 2026; Jiang et al., 2025).
These studies are concerned with accuracy under honest conditions. None of them asks what
happens when part of the document store is controlled by someone who wants the system to be
wrong.
This question matters more in this domain than in many others. A wrong escape-stair width
or guarding height arrives in the same confident, well-cited form as a correct one, so the reader
cannot detect it, and its consequence may be a building that does not protect its occupants.
Regulatory corpora are also assembled from many sources and updated by amendment, which
gives material of uncertain provenance realistic ways into a knowledge base.
The second motivation is methodological. Attack success rates in the RAG poisoning literature
are reported under different scoring rules, corpora, retrievers and conventions for refusals, and
both a recent security benchmark and a recent review argue for unified evaluation settings and
5


benchmarks precisely because evaluation practice has been fragmented (X. Liang et al., 2025;
Mu et al., 2026, p. 37). Questions such as whether a graph layer makes a system harder to
poison, or what each component of a layered defense contributes, have therefore been answered
by juxtaposing papers rather than by controlled comparison on one system.
1.3 Gaps in Existing Literature
The first is a gap of domain. The closest graph-RAG security studies use general-purpose or
non-engineering corpora such as multi-hop question answering, medical text and encyclopedic
material (J. Liang et al., 2026; Q. Chen et al., 2026; Yan et al., 2026), and the broadest RAG
security benchmarks omit graph-based systems (X. Liang et al., 2025). In a building-regulation
knowledge base, by contrast, an attack can be scored against a specific provision value whose
direction and consequence can be stated, yet research in this setting remains limited.
The second is a gap of comparison. Text-layer and graph-layer attacks have each been shown to
work on their own testbeds, but whether shared-relation poisoning is more or less effective than
passage injection at a matched budget has not been tested on one index under one scoring rule.
Defenses are likewise mostly evaluated one at a time (Xiang et al., 2024; Alizadeh Noughabi
et al., 2026; Wu et al., 2026). The layered defenses that report the contribution of each layer
combine text-side layers on a vector-only pipeline (Kumar et al., 2026), so whether retrieval-,
graph- and generation-side layers compose or interfere in a graph-based system is unknown.
The third is a gap of measurement. Most papers lead with a binary attack success rate (ASR)
and rarely report the share of queries a defense declines to answer, so a defense that stopped
the attack is hard to tell from a system that stopped answering; the rate also ignores whether
the attacked provisions were safety-critical, whether a requirement was loosened or tightened,
and whether wrong answers sound as certain as right ones. Studies of attack severity (M. Zou
et al., 2026) and of verbal confidence (J. Liu et al., 2026) suggest that these dimensions matter,
but they have not been examined in a domain-specific knowledge base such as a regulatory one.
1.4 Research Objectives and Research Questions
The thesis aims to produce an empirically grounded and reproducible security evaluation of a
graph-based RAG system for building regulations, covering both attack and defense. Following
the approved proposal, the objectives are:
1. to characterize the poisoning attack surface of LightRAG's dual vector–graph architecture
on a real regulatory corpus;
2. to implement representative attacks against the text layer and the graph layer, together
with a qualitative narrative attack and a defense-aware adaptive attacker, and to measure their
effectiveness;
3. to implement a three-layer retrieval–graph–generation defense and evaluate it factorially, so
that the contribution and interaction of each layer can be estimated;
4. to build a reusable benchmark of regulation questions with safety-consequence labels and
directional poison seeds; and
5. to evaluate outcomes beyond binary ASR, namely safety-consequence weighting, directional
bias, confidence–correctness alignment and natural version conflict.
These objectives serve a single research question, stated in the proposal and retained here
without change:
6


In a LightRAG system built on current UK building regulations, which poisoning strategies
achieve clause-level retrieval hijacking and generation misdirection through its vector and graph
layers, and how should a defense combining retrieval-side anomaly filtering, graph-side evidence
verification and generation-side fact verification be designed so that the attack success rate is
reduced to an acceptable level while clean question-answering accuracy is essentially maintained?
Chapter 3 decomposes this question into four sub-questions and states the hypotheses that were
registered before any experimental run.
1.5 Significance
Academically, the study offers a within-system comparison of attacks on two layers of a graph-
based RAG architecture and a factorial measurement of a layered defense under one scoring rule,
together with a measurement argument for any defended RAG system: a zero attack success
rate is not evidence of detection unless the refusals behind it are reported. Practically, it shows
organizations how building-regulation question-answering assistants fail under adversarial input
and which defenses work at little cost; the benchmark and scripts form a reusable testbed.
1.6 Research Setting and Data Collection Strategy
The UK Approved Documents were chosen as the research setting for four reasons. First, the
corpus states values: widths in millimeters, distances in meters, periods of fire resistance in min-
utes, each attached to a numbered paragraph or table. A value can be checked, so a successful
attack can be scored as the replacement of a specific value rather than as a vague change of
tone. Second, many values carry a direction, so that a tampered value either loosens or tightens
a requirement. Third, the guidance is layered by amendment, so that several oﬀicial editions
of the same provision can coexist, which creates genuine ambiguity about which instrument
governs. Fourth, the documents are public and freely available, which allows the corpus to be
redistributed and the experiment to be repeated.
The data were collected through controlled experiments rather than through observation of
a deployed system. The oﬀicial documents were downloaded from GOV.UK, registered in a
manifest with their hashes and indexed by LightRAG; all confirmatory experiments use the
resulting 850-chunk index.
A 150-item benchmark was frozen before the first attack, every
attack ran against an isolated copy of the index, and every answer and score was logged per
item and repeat (Chapter 4).

Chapter 2: Literature Review and Theoretical Framework
This chapter has two parts. The literature review (Sections 2.1 to 2.5) is organized around the
questions this thesis asks rather than paper by paper; the theoretical framework (Section 2.6)
states the definitions and commitments on which the design rests.
2.1 Conceptual Framework
The study models a graph-based RAG system as an information path with three stages. Re-
trieval selects candidate evidence: text chunks ranked by embedding similarity and, in a graph-
based system, entities and relations reached from query keywords. At the graph stage, entities,
typed relations and their descriptions extracted at indexing time are assembled into the context.
At generation, the LLM composes an answer from that context and, in LightRAG, cites its
source documents (Z. Guo et al., 2025).
A poisoning adversary enters this path at indexing time by contributing documents. A defense
can filter what is retrieved, verify what the graph asserts or check what is generated; and
an answer can be right, wrong or withheld, so the measurement must account for all three
outcomes.
2.2 Text-Level Poisoning of Retrieval-Augmented Generation
The foundational result for this study is PoisonedRAG (W. Zou et al., 2025, p. 9). The attacker
crafts a few passages per target question that satisfy a retrieval condition, ranking among
the top results for the query, and a generation condition, leading the LLM to the attacker's
chosen answer. In the black-box variant the retrieval condition is met by prepending the target
question itself to the malicious text. Five passages per target achieved high success rates on
several benchmarks. A standalone reproduction reported that success diluted as the knowledge
base grew from tens to over a thousand documents (J. Lin, 2026), and another study carried
content poisoning into deployed application frameworks, where normal-looking reference content
induced harmful answers in most attempts across five open models (Zeng, 2025).
Subsequent work has varied the mechanism along two axes. The first is optimization: Li et
al.
(2026) plan adversarial passages globally with Monte Carlo tree search, Z. Wang et al.
(2026) refine them word by word against the retriever while keeping them fluent, Qian (2026)
hijacks retrieval with poisons that preserve the semantics of the surrounding text, Jung et al.
(2026) camouflage adversarial content among benign chunks, and Thornton (2026) shows that
a poison's effectiveness depends on the corpus it enters. The second axis is the target of the
attack: Sun et al. (2026) aim at the knowledge claim rather than the final answer, and Miao et
al. (2026) poison the chain of thought rather than the evidence.
Two conclusions follow. Text-level poisoning with a small budget is well established, so the
open questions concern its boundaries; and the unit of measurement has converged on per-
question scoring against a preset wrong answer, which this thesis inherits so that its results are
comparable in kind.
2.3 Graph-Based RAG as the Attack Surface
LightRAG builds its index by prompting an LLM to extract entities and relations from each
chunk and stores the merged result as a graph beside vector indexes. At query time it retrieves
along a local path, anchored on entities, and a global path, anchored on relations and themes;
8


the hybrid mode combines both with chunk retrieval (Z. Guo et al., 2025). It has already been
used in security applications, for example to model an industrial messaging protocol (Y. Chen
et al., 2026).
A common intuition holds that the graph layer is defensive, because structure is harder to fabri-
cate than prose. The attack literature has tested this from the other side. GRAGPoison selects
relations shared by many queries, injects competing relations wrapped in a cover narrative and
enhances them with supporting relations, reaching high success rates on GraphRAG and Ligh-
tRAG; its ablation identifies temporal ordering, the claim that the competing relation is more
recent, as the most influential element of the cover narrative (J. Liang et al., 2026, p. 10). KEPo
similarly frames poison as a plausible evolution of the graph's knowledge (Q. Chen et al., 2026).
The extraction step is itself an attack surface: because the graph is built by an LLM reading
the source text, changing only a few words of that text can alter the extracted graph enough to
mislead downstream answers (Wen et al., 2025). Other work isolates an entity's neighborhood
(Yan et al., 2026), uses an entity as a backdoor trigger (Z. Zheng et al., 2026), steers an agent's
reasoning through a corrupted graph (Kereopa-Yorke et al., 2026), crafts universal adversarial
text for text-attributed graphs (Z. Chen et al., 2026) or extracts the graph with few queries
(S. Yang et al., 2026). Zhao et al. (2026, p. 8) find that adversarial triples are retrieved for
most attacked questions but adopted in only about half of those answers, locating part of the
robustness in generation.
The consensus is that the graph is a second attack surface rather than a second line of defense,
but each graph attack is evaluated on its own testbed and success criterion. Whether shared-
relation poisoning outperforms passage injection when corpus, budget, benchmark and scoring
rule are held fixed is the question this thesis addresses.
2.4 Defenses against Retrieval Corruption
Defenses can be grouped by the stage at which they act.
At retrieval, one family certifies robustness by isolating the retrieved passages into disjoint
groups, answering from each group separately and aggregating the answers, so that a corrupted
minority cannot carry the final answer (Xiang et al., 2024). Another family filters: FilterRAG
and its variants remove suspected adversarial passages from the retrieved set before generation
(Edemacu et al., 2025, p. 3), and attention-based detection looks for the trace that a poison
leaves in the generator's attention rather than in the text (Choudhary et al., 2026). The limits
of filtering are stated from the attack side: a reranker trained on clean in-domain data does
suppress injected text, but an attacker who edits about one percent of the characters recovers
most of the success rate (X. Yang et al., 2026). TailGuard targets specifically the retrieval-
optimized class of poisons (Zhou et al., 2026). Layering is the obvious response to the limits
of any one filter: RAGuard adds to an adversarially trained retriever a filter that removes
each retrieved document in turn and flags those whose removal shifts the answer, and reports
each layer's contribution separately (Kumar et al., 2026).
Source credibility receives direct
support from J. Liang et al. (2026): annotating corpus entries with a credibility label reduced
GRAGPoison's success rate from 89.2 to 45.7 percent, which still leaves nearly half of the attacks
successful, because labeled poison is still retrieved and can still be believed.
At the graph, HoG-GRAG decomposes a multi-hop question into ordered sub-queries, monitors
each hop for poisoning-induced inconsistencies and prunes the compromised entities and rela-
tions (Alizadeh Noughabi et al., 2026, p. 1), and Wu et al. (2026) build a separate verification
graph outside the retrieval pipeline to detect poisoned agent memories. A third line governs
writes rather than reads: a protocol for write-time conflict governance arbitrates between con-
flicting relations by timestamp and confidence before they enter the graph (Write-Time Conflict
9


Governance, 2026), and LedgerRAG keeps a claim-level ledger of the evidence behind an an-
swer and uses its temporal validity, source authority and conflicts to decide when to retrieve,
refresh and stop (S. Wang et al., 2026, p. 4). These methods weigh sources against one another;
none checks the values the graph asserts against an external, authoritative table of the values
themselves.
At generation, LCR-RAG enforces logical consistency through neuro-symbolic training (W.
Zheng et al., 2026), evaluation agents try to detect misinformation in the retrieved context
(Giri et al., 2026), and traceback methods identify after the fact which passages caused a
wrong answer (B. Zhang et al., 2025).
Practitioner guidance organizes the same space by
control: OWASP (2026) treats retrieval and generation as separate surfaces to harden, and
NIST's profile for generative AI classifies the risks by category, listing data poisoning among
the information-security risks, and attaches governance, provenance and testing actions to each
(National Institute of Standards and Technology [NIST], 2024, p. 8).
Two weaknesses run through this literature. Defenses are almost always evaluated in isolation,
so the benefit of combining them is assumed rather than shown; one layer may remove exactly
the inputs another would have caught.
And defenses are usually evaluated against a fixed
attacker, which measures their specification rather than their robustness (Thornton, 2026; X.
Yang et al., 2026).
2.5 Beyond a Single Success Rate
The attack success rate answers one question: did the answer change to the attacker's target?
Three strands of work argue that this is insuﬀicient for a high-consequence domain. M. Zou et
al. (2026) point out that equal success rates may hide very unequal consequences, and propose
measuring severity instead. J. Liu et al. (2026) show that RAG systems' verbal confidence is
poorly calibrated when the context contains noise or contradiction, which raises the question
of whether poisoned answers are expressed with undiminished certainty. Giri et al. (2026) find
that their NLI-based evaluation agent blocks instruction injection reliably, but in-place edits
such as entity swaps, the kind of value substitution studied here, remain hard to detect; L.
Zhang et al. (2026) map the same diﬀiculty in disinformation detection.
A further gap concerns refusals. Generation-side defenses commonly decline to answer on doubt-
ful evidence, and under the usual definition a refusal is a failed attack, so a defense can report
a near-zero success rate by refusing most queries. Some defenses report clean and robust accu-
racy beside the success rate (Xiang et al., 2024; Choudhary et al., 2026), which reveals refusal
indirectly, but none of the papers reviewed here reports the refusal share itself.
Finally, the poisoning literature treats recency only as an attack technique: GRAGPoison and
KEPo use fabricated recency as cover (J. Liang et al., 2026; Q. Chen et al., 2026). In building
regulation recency is real, and amended editions of the same guidance coexist. Whether a RAG
system attributes a value to the instrument that governs it, even without an attacker, has not
been examined.
2.6 Theoretical Framework
The study is grounded in four bodies of theory and translates each into a design commitment.
Threat modeling and the attacker's capability. Following W. Zou et al. (2025) and J.
Liang et al. (2026), the adversary is characterized by its access (write access to the corpus
only), its knowledge (black-, gray- or white-box with respect to retriever and graph) and its
goal (targeted replacement of a value). Each attack therefore has a declared knowledge tier and
10


budget, so that a result can be attributed to a stated adversary.
Dose–response as a model of adversarial budget. The number of injected passages per
target is a controlled dose; a dose–response design yields the slope that tells a defender how fast
risk grows with attacker effort.
Factorial design and interaction. A layered defense is modeled as a set of binary factors. In a
full 2 × 2 × 2 factorial each layer's effect can be estimated in every context of the other two,
and an interaction is the difference between a layer's effects in two contexts.
Measurement validity and the refusal problem. A measure is valid only if it measures the
property it is used to infer. Attack success rate measures whether an attack produced its target
answer, not whether a defense detected it: a refused query scores zero, exactly as a correct
answer does (Equation 4.4), so a defense that refuses most queries reports a near-zero rate. The
study therefore reports a conditional accuracy over answered records beside an unconditional
one in which a refusal is counted as an incorrect answer (Equation 4.6a); only the latter can rank
configurations that answer different shares of the questions. The secondary outcomes (severity
weighting, directional analysis and confidence analysis) were registered with their roles fixed in
advance.
Together, these commitments let the study borrow its attacks, defenses and measures from the
literature while comparing them on one system rather than juxtaposing them.
Chapter 3: Presentation of the Problem
3.1 The Core Problem
The core problem of this thesis can be stated in one sentence: a retrieval-augmented system
that answers questions about building regulations can be made to return a wrong value for a
specific provision by anyone able to add documents to its corpus, and it is not known how far a
graph layer changes that exposure or how far a layered defense can remove it without making
the system useless.
The literature reviewed in Chapter 2 establishes that a few adversarial passages can redirect an
answer, that graph-based systems can be poisoned through their relations, and that defenses
exist at each stage of the pipeline. Three questions remain open.
The first is whether the graph alters what an attacker must do (make a payload survive the
LLM's entity–relation extraction and be selected through keyword-to-entity-to-relation retrieval,
rather than merely rank a passage in the vector top-k), or merely offers another location for
the same payload (the entity and relation descriptions placed in the prompt beside the text
chunks). The claim that structure is harder to fabricate than prose has been made on behalf of
graph-based RAG, and attacks on the graph have been demonstrated, but the two layers have
never been compared on one system under one rubric.
The second is whether a layered defense composes. If each layer's effect were independent of the
others, the contributions could be measured one at a time and added. If the layers interact, for
instance because one layer removes the very inputs another would have acted on, a one-at-a-time
evaluation reports numbers that do not describe the combined system.
The third is what a low attack success rate under defense means. A defense that stops the
poison and a defense that stops answering both produce a low rate, and they have very different
value to a user.
11


3.2 Sub-questions
The research question stated in Section 1.4 is decomposed into four sub-questions, each tied to
a part of the proposal.
SQ1 (text layer). How effective is text-level poisoning against LightRAG on the regulatory
corpus, and how does its effectiveness change with the number of passages injected per target?
SQ2 (graph layer). Does poisoning aimed at the graph's shared relations succeed more or less
often than text-level poisoning at a matched budget, and does poisoning spill over to questions
that were not targeted?
SQ3 (defense).
Can three defensive layers push attack success down to a tolerable rate
without a material loss of clean accuracy, how do the layers contribute and interact, and does
a defense-aware attacker recover what a static attacker loses?
SQ4 (harms). Are the harms uneven: do errors fall disproportionately on safety-critical pro-
visions, does the direction of tampering matter, are wrong answers expressed with less certainty
than right ones, and does the system confuse versions of the guidance even without an attacker?
3.3 Hypotheses
The hypotheses below were registered on 4 October 2026, before any attack run, with their tests
and decision thresholds; later dated amendments, each made before the run it affected, fixed
details such as the budget operator for the graph attack.
H1a. At a budget of five passages per target, the strict attack success rate of text-level poisoning
over the 150 targets exceeds 0.10. Test: one-sided exact binomial test on the number of targets
that succeed in the majority of their three repeats.
H1b. Attack success increases across the nested budgets of one, three and five passages. Test:
Page's L for ordered alternatives in a repeated-measures layout.
H2. At a matched budget, shared-relation (graph) poisoning achieves a higher strict success
rate than text-level poisoning. The direction follows the claim that the graph partially protects
against text injection while graph-specific poisoning overcomes that protection. Test: one-sided
exact McNemar test on the 150 targets, the outcome of each target being the majority of its
repeats.
H3. Text-level poisoning reduces accuracy on untargeted questions in the same index (spillover).
Test: one-sided exact McNemar test on the pooled untargeted observations.

amendment once attack two was designed (Appendix E). H5, H6, H7 and H8 are secondary:
they are reported with intervals but do not move the conclusions of the confirmatory family.
3.4 Thresholds and Related Issues
Thresholds set after the data are seen tend to be set where the data already lie, and with many
configurations the freedom to choose which comparison to report could make almost any defense
look effective.
Two further issues bear on the problem: poisoned text was confined to isolated local copies of
the index (Section 4.11), and the unit of analysis is the benchmark item, which makes paired
comparisons possible.
Chapter 4: Research Methodology
4.1 Research Approach
The study uses a mixed-methods design.
A controlled quantitative experiment carries the
confirmatory claims: attacks and defenses are switched on and off on one instrumented system
while the corpus, benchmark, serving model and query parameters are held fixed, and every
answer is scored against a frozen rubric. A small qualitative component, a three-case study of
the amendment-narrative attack and a hand-scored version probe, supports their interpretation.
An experimental design suits the question because it is causal: it asks what an attack or defense
does to a system's answers with everything else held constant. It can establish whether and by
how much outcomes change on this system, not whether the effects transfer (Chapter 9).
Two problems were anticipated: noise, because the serving LLM exposes no temperature pa-
rameter, handled by three repeats per configuration; and researcher degrees of freedom, handled
by pre-registration (Section 3.3), hash-frozen inputs and recomputation of every number from
raw per-item records.

4.2 System and Environment
All experiments used LightRAG core 1.5.8 as a local server. Indexing extraction and answer

5.5.4 What the Zero Attack Success Rates Are Made Of
Two configurations and all three adaptive arms under full defense report an ASR of exactly
zero, produced by two separable mechanisms.
The retrieval layer acts on provenance. Poison recall fell from 0.969 undefended to 0.000 in
every configuration that includes it, a measure computed whether or not the model answered.
In a defended run of the A4 control arm the candidate pool still held about 55 injected chunks
per poisoned query, but none of the 2,876 chunks that reached the model's context came from
the 360 injected documents. The layer removes these documents because they are absent from
the manifest, not because their content was judged false.
The generation layer acts by retreat. In the registered batch every retreat followed a neutral
30


verdict, meaning the NLI model could not confirm the sentence from its cited evidence; no
contradiction or conflict verdict occurred. In all it retreated on 76 percent of queries and 73
percent of clean records, and on the A4 control arm arming all layers raised disruption from
0.528 to 0.884 while strict success fell from 0.157 to zero: a wrong answer was replaced by no
answer, not by the correct one. Both correction batches (21 and 25 October) reproduce this
partition (Appendix A, Table A-7).

5.7 Uneven Harms (SQ4)
5.7.1 Safety-Consequence Weighting (H5)
With weights of 3, 2 and 1 for life-safety, compliance and general items, the benchmark's total
weight is 386. Across the 97 usable runs the difference D (Equation 4.7) had a mean and median
of +0.005; it was positive in 71 runs, zero in 9 and negative in 17. Errors were thus slightly
concentrated on safety-critical items, but the tilt lies within its uncertainty: of the 95 runs with
raw records, 87 have an item-clustered 95 percent interval containing zero, 8 lie above it and
none below (Figure 5.7). The weights moved the error rate by about 4 percent where, by the
arithmetic bound of Equation 4.7, they could have moved it by about 17 (Appendix D.3).

5.8 Summary of Results
Text-level poisoning was effective at a small budget; graph-level poisoning was less effective
at the same budget.
Fabricated amendment notices were repeated in every case and went
undetected by value-based scoring, because the answers kept the correct value and the rubric
checks only whether required values are present. The full defense met its registered criterion
and six of the seven configurations met its threshold, but only the retrieval layer did so while
preserving accuracy.
The generation layer refused, and the graph layer had no measurable
effect, for three reasons: its anchors cover about half the items (77 of 150, of which 67 are
adjudicable for A2); the graph attack asserted an applicability rather than a contradicting
number, which an anchor table cannot match; and the layer deletes only relation edges that
conflict with an anchored value without filtering chunks, so poison recall stayed at 0.981 and
the poisoned text still reached the generator. The layers interacted strongly. An unregistered
legitimate amendment, like the poison, reached the prompt through the graph descriptions.
The adaptive attacker did not gain. Wrong answers were not expressed with less certainty, self-
rated confidence became less calibrated under poisoning, and on neither attribution item did
the system name the governing instrument: it credited a value to an older edition containing
the same number and dropped commencement dates, consistent with the absence of edition
metadata in the chunks and descriptions it answers from.
Chapter 6: Discussion
6.1 What the Results Mean
Three of the four sub-questions produced answers smaller, or less favorable to the proposal, than
anticipated; because every reading was fixed in advance, these are results, not failed attempts
at larger ones.
6.2 The Graph Is a Second Surface, Not a Second Line of Defense
The proposal, following the literature, treated graph-level poisoning as the more dangerous
route: shared relations reach many queries, and GRAGPoison reported very high success on
LightRAG (J. Liang et al., 2026). On this system, at a matched budget, the graph attack
succeeded on fewer targets than the text attack (22 against 33 of 150). Under attack, then,
poisoning aimed at the graph had the lower success rate, so the graph layer was not the softer
of the two targets; that is narrower than saying the graph is a defense.
Whether structure blunts text injection needs a graph-free comparison, which the registered
design did not contain.
A second-batch run therefore used LightRAG's naive mode, which
retrieves chunks by vector similarity alone, on the clean index and on A1's fold-0 index at M =
5, one pass per item, scored automatically. On the 75 targeted items strict accuracy fell from
36


0.9155 (65 of 71 answered) to 0.7067 (53 of 75), with 14 items lost and 2 gained (two-sided
exact McNemar p = 0.0042); on the 75 untargeted items it fell from 0.9452 to 0.9437 (4 lost, 2
gained; p = 0.6875). The text attack therefore does not need the graph. What the graph adds
or removes on top of that is not identified by this arm, which also differs from the hybrid runs
in repetition (Section 9.3).
The graph also remains a channel. For two items whose full prompt context was captured,
the poisoned claim appeared in the entity or relationship sections in all six draws and in the
chunk section in none (Section 5.6). This was not intended: each layer was designed around the
units the proposal named (candidate chunks, anchored relation edges, cited evidence), whereas
the descriptions are summaries merged from several sources at indexing time that carry no
per-document provenance; the gap was found only after the batches had run, and it leaves a
route open for payloads written to survive extraction. This agrees with J. Liang et al.'s (2026)
location of the damage at the extraction stage, with Wen et al.'s (2025) demonstration that
rewriting a few words of the source text can change the graph built from it, and with Zhao et
al.'s (2026) finding that adversarial graph content is retrieved more easily than it is adopted.
6.3 One Criterion, Two Mechanisms
Applied to every configuration, the H4a threshold (Equation 3.1) is met by six of seven through
two quite different mechanisms, and the criterion cannot tell them apart, because a refusal
lowers the attack success rate exactly as a correct answer does. The generation layer meets it
by refusing most queries, at a cost of 58 to 74 points of clean accuracy; the retrieval layer meets
it by excluding the poison while answering nearly everything. The retrieval layer's lack of a
measurable cost has two parts. On the clean benchmark it holds by construction, because the
trust term is the same constant for every manifest document and so cannot change their order
(Section 5.5.2), although the non-inferiority margin was only 0.36 points, and a variant whose
outlier term used a different graph-side path lost 32 points. On a legitimate amendment left
outside the manifest, the second-batch false-positive test also found no measurable cost: of the
20 items that depend on the amendment, 17 were answered correctly when it was filtered and
16 when it was registered (C −B = −0.05; one discordant item, exact McNemar p = 1.0, a
difference within noise), but for a reason that is no comfort (Section 6.5).
Under the usual definition, which this thesis adopted, a refusal is a failed attack (W. Zou et
al., 2025; Equation 4.4), so any defense with an aggressive enough fallback reports a near-zero
rate. None of the defenses reviewed in Chapter 2 reports its refusal share beside its success
rate; where robust accuracy is reported, refusal can be inferred, and otherwise the published
rate cannot separate detection from refusal. The remedy is cheap: report the answered share
and the unconditional accuracy beside the rate. Here that separates the mechanisms at once:
retrieval reports ASR 0.023 with every query answered and 0.857 of poisoned records correct,
all reports ASR 0.000 with 0.20 answered and 0.167 correct. A compliance assistant that refuses
three of four honest queries has not become resistant to poisoning; it has stopped being useful.
6.4 Layer Interaction
The factorial analysis showed that the layers do not compose additively. The retrieval layer
contributes +0.435 alone and +0.148 with the generation layer armed, because it removes
exactly the poisoned passages the generation layer would otherwise have had to reject, while
the generation layer keeps withholding answers that would have been correct. A one-factor-at-
a-time evaluation would have reported one of these numbers without revealing which question
it answered. Layered defenses, including the proposal's own, must therefore be evaluated as
37


configurations. RAGuard, a two-layer defense for vector-based RAG, is one of the few that
reports such an ablation: its adversarially trained retriever alone left residual attack success,
whereas its counterfactual document filter alone, and both layers together, removed it (Kumar
et al., 2026, pp. 2, 9). The factorial here extends that practice to three layers acting at different
stages of a graph-based pipeline, where the effects proved strongly context-dependent.
6.5 Provenance versus Content
The most effective layer judged no content at all. It excluded documents absent from the hash
manifest, which closed the chunk channel completely, in agreement with J. Liang et al.'s (2026)
finding that a simple credibility annotation was the most effective defense they tested. It did
not stop the attack completely: every residual success came through the graph descriptions
(Section 5.6). A provenance filter also cannot distinguish a poisoned new document from a
legitimate new amendment, and in a regulatory domain new amendments are precisely what
users most need. The second-batch test shows how this plays out in LightRAG (Section 5.5.2):
excluding an unregistered amendment cost nothing only because the filter never governed the
amendment's content, whose wording reached the prompt through the entity and relationship
descriptions in 14 of 16 measurable items and through the chunk section in none. The filter is
therefore both less costly and less protective than its design implies: it withholds a document's
chunks, while the descriptions extracted from that document at indexing time pass, whether
the document is genuine or poisoned. Fei et al. (2026) identify a related blind spot in graph
memories for LLM agents, where a defense that checks only the provenance of retrieved records
cannot see an untrusted write to the graph structure that changes which records are selected.
The problem thus moves from detection to admission, and admission must cover indexing as
well as retrieval.
The anchor-based graph layer, which the proposal regarded as its most domain-specific contri-
bution, had no measurable effect, for two reasons. Its coverage was limited: anchors are hand-
extracted subject–relation–comparator–value–unit assertions, which explanatory, enumerative
and rule-type answers do not fit, so the table covers 77 of 150 items and 67 after exemptions.
And the attack did not need to violate an anchored value: A2 stated contradictory applicabil-
ity rather than a contradictory number, and A4's graph arm simply moved to an uncovered
parameter.
6.6 Harms beyond Attack Success Rate
Three findings concern harms that a binary success rate does not register. The amendment-
narrative cases showed that an answer can contain the correct value and still direct the reader
to a fabricated one by declaring the correct value superseded, which value-based scoring counts
as correct. The version-conflict probe showed that, even without an attacker, the system stated
a not-yet-in-force rule as current when the question did not ask about status, so the ambiguity
the narrative attack exploits is a standing property of the system. And poisoned answers were
expressed with almost undiminished certainty, consistent with J. Liu et al. (2026). The second-
batch self-rating puts a number on this (Section 5.7.3): calibration error rose from 0.104 to
0.309 under poisoning (paired difference +0.205, 95 percent interval [0.129, 0.284]), because the
model kept rating three quarters of its poisoned answers at the top of the scale. Its stated
confidence became a worse guide exactly when a guide was needed. Together these suggest
that evaluation of regulatory RAG needs a version-aware correctness criterion that checks not
only the value but the instrument and date it is attributed to. On severity and direction the
data show only that errors leaned slightly toward life-safety provisions and that loosening and
tightening split evenly.
38


These readings do not rest on one scorer. An independent judge agreed with the strict rubric
on 50 of 58 explanatory answers, and where the author disagreed with the rubric, the rubric
was always the stricter reader (Section 4.10). Accuracy under the strict rubric is therefore, if
anything, a conservative reading of answer quality.
6.7 A Proposed Improvement to the Defense Design
These findings suggest a different allocation of effort. The retrieval layer should become an
admission control: a signed manifest, with amendments admitted through an authenticated
channel before indexing. The graph layer should move from contradiction detection to version
arbitration, with anchor rows carrying instrument and commencement date. The generation
layer should verify claims against the specific chunks cited, with a fallback calibrated on clean
questions. And the entity and relationship descriptions need their own provenance, since they
are the channel the poison used.
Chapter 7: Conclusion
This thesis asked how poisoned documents can make a LightRAG assistant for UK building
regulations misstate a provision, and whether a three-layer defense can prevent it without
harming honest answers. It answered with a pre-registered experiment on one instrumented
system: a labeled 150-item benchmark, four attacks and a full factorial over three defensive
layers.
The answer has four parts. First, text-level poisoning works at a small budget: five passages
per target succeeded on 33 of 150 targets, and success rose with the budget although retrieval
was saturated at one passage. Second, the graph was not the softer target: shared-relation
poisoning succeeded on 22 of 150 targets at the same budget, the opposite of the registered
prediction, although poisoned graph descriptions still reached the prompt through a channel
no layer inspects.
Third, the full defense met the registered criterion and six of the seven
configurations met its threshold, but the generation layer did so by refusing most queries and
losing most of its clean accuracy, while the retrieval layer did so with no measurable loss of clean
accuracy, by a margin of 0.36 points; even a legitimate unregistered amendment was not lost,
because its content, like the poison's, reached the generator through the graph descriptions. The
layers interacted so strongly that per-layer contributions cannot be stated. Fourth, the harms
beyond the success rate were real but hard to measure: a fabricated amendment notice was
repeated in every case while value-based scoring judged most answers correct, wrong answers
were expressed with nearly undiminished certainty, the model's self-rated confidence became
markedly less calibrated under poisoning, and on neither attribution item did the system name
the instrument that governs a provision.
Against its objectives, the study characterized the attack surface, implemented four attacks
and three defensive layers, built a reusable benchmark, and evaluated outcomes beyond binary
success; the bounds on these claims are in Chapter 9.
The study contributes three claims that extend the existing literature. On one system under
one rubric, the graph is a second attack surface rather than a second line of defense. A layered
defense's components do not have separately reportable effects, so layered defenses must be
evaluated factorially. And a zero attack success rate under defense is not evidence of detection
until the refusals and the clean-accuracy cost behind it are reported; here the defended zeros
came from provenance filtering and refusal, not from recognizing false content.
39


For the AEC sector, a regulatory question-answering assistant can be poisoned cheaply, can
state a fabricated amendment as current, and does so with the confidence of a correct answer.
Such systems can support professional judgment but cannot replace the verification of values
against the governing document. Recommendations follow in Chapter 8.
Chapter 8: Recommendations
8.1 For Organizations Deploying Regulatory RAG
Control admission to the corpus. The single most effective measure in this study was a
manifest of approved documents with content hashes. Organizations should admit new docu-
ments, including oﬀicial amendments, only through an authenticated and logged process, and
treat the right to add documents as a privileged role. Admission must be enforced before index-
ing, because in a graph-based system the descriptions extracted from an unadmitted document
still reach the prompt when its chunks are filtered.
Require version-aware answers. Answers about regulatory values should state the instru-
ment, edition and commencement date they rely on. Any answer that announces an amendment
should be checked against the oﬀicial source before it is acted upon, because fabricated amend-
ment notices were repeated by the system in every tested case.
Price refusals before adopting a defense. A low attack success rate should be reported
with the share of queries answered and the accuracy on clean questions; a defense that refuses
most honest queries is safe only as an unused system is safe.
Keep human verification for safety-critical values. Because wrong answers were expressed
as confidently as right ones, a system's tone cannot be used to decide when to check. Values
bearing on means of escape, structure and guarding should be confirmed against the governing
document by a competent person.
8.2 For Researchers
Report the composition of a zero. Defense evaluations should publish the refusal share, the
unconditional accuracy and the clean-accuracy cost beside the attack success rate, and should
distinguish exclusion by provenance from detection by content.
Evaluate layered defenses factorially.
A full factorial over the layers costs little
more than a one-at-a-time sweep and is the only design in which interactions can
be estimated.
Instrument the graph channel. Measures of poison reach should cover entity and relation-
ship descriptions, not only text chunks, because that is where poisoned content, and the content
of an unregistered legitimate amendment, entered the prompt in this study.
Check the scorer as well as the system. A rule-based scorer should be compared with an
independent second scorer from another model family, with its prompt and statistic fixed before
any answer is judged.
Develop version-aware correctness criteria. Benchmarks for regulatory question answer-
ing should score the attribution of a value to its governing instrument and date, not only the
value itself.

Chapter 9: Limitations and Delimitations
This chapter gathers every boundary on the claims of Chapters 5 to 7: what was deliberately
left out (9.1), what was planned but not performed (9.2), the limits of the measurements (9.3),
factors outside the author's control (9.4), and the outcomes that would have overturned the
conclusions (9.5).
9.1 Delimitations
The study was confined to one system (LightRAG 1.5.8), one serving model, one jurisdiction's
regulations, one language (English) and one experimental index, built from the merged Ap-
proved Documents and the Manual. This followed the proposal's timeline, which put attack
one, attack two and the three defensive layers on the small corpus first and treated corpus-
scale and retrieval-mode ablations as secondary. Other graph-based frameworks, embedding
models and rerankers, attacks requiring access to the model or serving stack, availability and
confidentiality attacks, and the behavior of practitioners were not examined. The attacks fol-
low PoisonedRAG and GRAGPoison because these are the reference constructions, the latter
being the only graph attack demonstrated on LightRAG; the narrative attack was kept to three
cases, as planned; and the defense weights were fixed rather than tuned. The results therefore
describe statutory, value-dense text in a curated corpus and may not transfer to open corpora
or to systems with different retrieval parameters.
9.2 Experiments Planned but Not Performed
Larger corpora. No attack or defense experiment, and no run of the full 150-item benchmark,
was made on the 70- or 192-document index; these served only the two exploratory probes
(Sections 5.4 and 5.7.4). Whether the dose response, the layer comparison and the defense
partition hold under more competing text, and whether the dilution with corpus size reported
elsewhere (J. Lin, 2026) applies, is unknown.
Edition-conflict items. Ten records (five questions, each in two phrasings) were written to
test whether the system attributes a value to the edition that states it when two editions of
the same guidance are indexed together. Their answers exist only in the larger indexes, so they
were not run, and no claim about edition attribution goes beyond the twelve-question probe.
H4a on attack one. H4a was registered on attack one, and a dated amendment added the
same test on attack two once that attack was designed. The defense ablation ran on attack
two's poisoned index, so the attack-one member of H4a has no run, and every defense result
concerns attack two.
Retrieval modes and the naive poisoning baseline. All registered runs used the hybrid
mode; the local and global modes were not ablated, and the second-batch naive-mode run
(Section 6.2) is not a graph-free baseline matched in repetition and scoring. The proposal's
crude-poison control, fixed by amendment as blunt conflicting text, was not run, so the second
half of H2 is untested; the naive retrieval mode removes the graph, not the sophistication of the
poison.
Full prompt-context capture. The context the generator received was captured for two
poisoned items (Section 5.6) and for the 16 measurable items of the false-positive test (Section
5.5.2), but not for all 150 items under attack, so how often the graph descriptions carry the
poison is known only from these samples and from a lower bound.
41


Claim-level verification. The generation layer checked whole sentences; the claim-level vari-
ant was not run.
An iterated adaptive attacker. The prompt for adaptive_l3 described a loop in which the
generator would query the NLI checker and revise its passage, but the loop was not wired into
the generator: the checker's verdicts on the 355 generated blocks were logged and never fed
back. All adaptive arms were single-pass, so H7's null is not a demonstration of robustness;
running the loop would be a new construction with new frozen prompts and budget.
A registered self-rating and a second serving model. No registered run asked the model
for a self-rating, because adding it to the answer prompt would have changed the answers
being measured; the rating exists only from the second-batch pass, whose answers are not the
registered ones. All results come from one commercial model.
9.3 Limitations of Measurement
Benchmark construction. The items were written and reviewed by the author, who also ran
the analysis; selection may favor clearly stated provisions, making clean accuracy and attack
success optimistic together.
Value-based scoring. The rubric checks whether the required values are present, so it cannot
see an answer that keeps the correct value while declaring it superseded. The second scorer
covers only the 30 explanatory items, so it is a sensitivity analysis of scoring on those items, not
of the attack success rate over the whole benchmark; its agreement with the rubric (κ = 0.655)
has a wide interval, [0.352, 0.886], and the author's review of 15 answers can detect a direction
of bias but cannot estimate agreement (κ against the judge 0.391, interval including zero).
Resolution of the secondary tests. H3's test had a discordance rate of 0.133 and little
power; the directional test had 20 discordant pairs, so only a split of 15 to 5 or more would have
reached significance; H2's pre-registered subgroup had 39 items and the second-batch entity
strata had ten discordant pairs in the shared stratum; the version probe has denominators of
five and two; and the severity tilt lies within its uncertainty in 87 of 95 runs. Nulls here concern
resolution, not the absence of effects. Safety weights were re-rated blind with low agreement
(kappa 0.226).
Noise and replication. With no temperature control, noise was estimated from three repeats,
so effects smaller than the repeat spread, such as the graph layer's, cannot be resolved. The
clean-index arms behind H4b exist in a single batch, and the non-inferiority margin is about
half of one item.
The generation layer's instrument. Because LightRAG returns references at file level, each
sentence was checked against many unrelated chunks, and almost every sentence not literally
entailed was retracted; the layer's detection capability was therefore not identified.
In the
second-batch partial-defense run, the control answered about 2 percent of records, so the tests
needed five and seven discordant items and could hardly have rejected even for a stronger
attacker; adaptive_l2 covered only 32 of the 72 items.
Reach measured on chunks only. The reach measures count text chunks, because they
followed passage-level practice and LightRAG's references identify files and chunks only; the
reach of the entity and relationship descriptions is known only as a lower bound and from the
two context captures.
42


9.4 Factors beyond the Researcher's Control
The corpus changes as amendments are published, so the version probe, dated eighteen days
before a commencement, refers to that moment. Limited time constrained repeats, corpus scales
and adaptive iterations; this affects the smaller effects but not the main comparisons, which
held in all three ablation batches.
9.5 Outcomes That Would Have Overturned the Conclusions
Had H1a failed, text-level poisoning would not have been shown to be effective; had graph
poisoning exceeded text poisoning, the conclusion about the softer target would be reversed;
had the retrieval layer met H4a only by refusing, no configuration would have preserved utility;
and had the adaptive attacker recovered success, the defended zeros would have been shown to
be fragile. The last remains open for the reasons in Section 9.2.

Appendix D: Extended Results and Method Details
This appendix keeps the detail that was moved out of Chapters 4 and 5 to respect the word
limit of the main text. Each section is referred to from the place in the main text where its
material was summarized.
54


D.1 Distribution of A1 Success across Repeats (Section 5.2.2)
Across repeats, 93 of the 150 targets never succeeded at M = 5, 24 succeeded once, 14 twice
and 19 every time; a quarter of the targets were thus neither reliably attacked nor reliably safe.
Zhao et al. (2026) describe adoption as polarized: adversarial content is either rejected outright
or, once adopted, dominates the answer. The split outcomes here do not show that pattern,
and it is why the registered outcome uses the majority of three repeats rather than a single run.
D.2 Factorial Effects on the Clean Items (Section 5.5.3)
On the clean items the graph layer's four effects are also small and mostly negative, ranging
from −0.090 to +0.026.
The clean side shows the same interaction in reverse. The generation layer costs 0.744 of clean
accuracy with no other layer armed and 0.603 with the retrieval layer armed; no context brings
its cost near zero. A configuration's score is therefore not a sum of per-layer contributions,
and any single, context-free figure such as “the retrieval layer contributes 0.435” would report
a quantity this design shows does not exist: that layer's effect ranges from +0.097 to +0.435
depending on which other layers are armed.
D.3 Severity Weighting in Detail (Section 5.7.1)
With weights of 3, 2 and 1 for life-safety, compliance and general items, the total weight of the
benchmark is 386. Across the 97 usable runs, the difference D (Equation 4.7) had a mean of
+0.005 and a median of +0.005; it was positive in 71 runs, zero in 9 and negative in 17. Errors
were thus slightly concentrated on safety-critical items, but the tilt lies within its uncertainty:
of the 95 runs with raw records, 87 have an item-clustered 95 percent interval containing zero,
8 lie above it and none below (Figure 5.7). The ratio R, undefined in 8 runs in which every
answered item was correct, tells the same story (mean 1.038 over the rest); intervals computed
earlier for R resampled individual records rather than items and were therefore too narrow.
The tilt is also small relative to what the weighting could have shown: with all 150 items and
a total weight of 386, the ratio cannot exceed 3 × 150
386 = 1.166, and every run lies within the
corresponding bound for its own item mix. The weights moved the error rate by about 4 percent
where, by this arithmetic bound rather than any pre-registered estimate, they could have moved
it by about 17.
D.4 Expressed Certainty and Verifier Confidence in Detail (Section 5.7.3)
Poisoning lowered strict accuracy by 21.6 percentage points but lowered the share of strongly
asserted answers by only 4.3 points, and the hedge rate did not change. The item-level paired
difference in strong assertion was −0.043 (95 percent interval [−0.099, +0.014]); a second,
independent clean baseline on 65 items gave −0.040 (95 percent interval [−0.151, +0.079]).
Under poisoning, wrong answers were not stated less assertively than right ones; the point
estimate runs the other way (0.750 against 0.559), although with 16 wrong answers the intervals
overlap. One sign of awareness did appear: the share of correct answers that explicitly flagged
conflicting sources rose from 0.000 to 0.094.
The verifier's own confidence was no better guide.
An NLI pass was right 81.4 percent of
the time, but its confidence ranked correct above incorrect answers only weakly where the
retrieval layer was armed (AUROC 0.58) and inversely where it was not (0.21). The inversion is
not saturation (the confidence still varies, SD 0.095): without the retrieval layer, 110 of the 115
55


wrong answers that passed cite an injected passage, and the verifier is most confident on exactly
these (mean 0.74 against 0.64 for correct answers), because the poison does entail them. Among
passed answers that cite no injected passage, AUROC is 0.81. The verifier's confidence thus
measures support by the cited evidence, not truth. H8 is not supported: expressed certainty
did not track correctness under attack, so the tone of an answer cannot tell a user whether it
is right. Plausible causes are that the poison passages are written in the same authoritative
register as the regulations and that LightRAG's answer prompt asks the model to answer from
its context, whose register it reproduces; J. Liu et al. (2026) report the same overconfidence
under contradictory evidence.
In the second-batch self-rating pass, the 75 targeted items alone show the same inversion more
sharply: the 134 poisoned answers rated 5 were strictly correct in 11.9 percent of cases, and the
AUROC on these items was 0.188 (descriptive only).
D.5 Version Probe: Item-Level Reading (Section 5.7.4)
On neither attribution item, in any index, did the system attribute the value to the instrument
that governs it, and in the two larger indexes one in five temporal questions was answered by
presenting a provision not yet in force as current. An item-by-item reading (Appendix A, Table
A-9) shows that the failure is narrower than “the system ignores versions”. Answers did mention
editions and dates: on the larger indexes, 11 of 11 answered questions named a document or
edition and 9 named a date, and the two questions asking whether a specific provision was in
force (ADB-TS-01 and ADB-TS-06) were answered correctly on both larger indexes. The two
failures were specific. ADB-VC-02 attributed the 7.5 m limit to the 2019 edition, which contains
the same number, rather than to the 2026 replacement table; ADB-VC-03 stated the 2026 rule
for blocks with a storey of 18 m or more as current, without its commencement date, even on
the 192-document index where the answer cited the 2026 booklet itself. The system therefore
retrieved the governing document but did not carry its commencement status into an answer
that was not explicitly about status. The version confusion that A3 exploits deliberately is thus
already present without an attacker.
D.6 Construction of the Four Attacks (Section 4.5)
Attack A1 follows the black-box form of PoisonedRAG (W. Zou et al., 2025). Each poison
passage is P = S + I, where S is the target question verbatim, which makes the passage
retrievable, and I is an LLM-generated paragraph formatted like a provision of the relevant
Approved Document and asserting the mutated value.
Every passage was checked by the
scorer that later judged the answers and rejected if it also stated the correct value or lacked
the attacker's exact value (at most three generation calls per target). The budget M is the
number of poison passages injected for one target question. The term follows PoisonedRAG,
which injects N = 5 malicious texts per target question by default (W. Zou et al., 2025), and
the proposal, which fixed M ∈{1, 3, 5} as the independent variable of A1. The three budgets
are nested within one ordered list, so that the passages used at M = 3 include those used at
M = 1. Each fold was poisoned separately, giving six cells, each run three times.
Attack A2 adapts the three steps of GRAGPoison (J. Liang et al., 2026) to the regulatory
corpus. Rather than restating the target value as a provision, it attaches a rival relation to
an entity that also occurs in a different Part of the corpus (hot water, for instance, appears
in both Part G and Part L), claiming a scope of application that conflicts with the provision
behind the question. The shared entity was inferred from the corpus text alone; reading the
built index to select relations would have placed the attack in the white-box tier reserved for A4.
56


Two differences from A1 were deliberate so that the comparison isolates the layer: the passage
does not carry the question, and the narrative concerns applicability rather than recency, since
recency is A3's mechanism. The budget was fixed at five passages per target, matching A1 at
M = 5. Poison could be constructed for 143 of the 150 targets; six had no shared entity and one
presented a construction conflict. These seven remain in the denominator as failures, because
an attack that cannot be mounted does not succeed.
Attack A3 tests the narrative that the proposal identified as specific to the domain: a fabricated
amendment notice announcing that a clause has been revised. Three cases were constructed,
each a 2026 “Amendment Booklet” passage changing one value: the reduced maximum travel
distance in a small block of flats (4.5 m to 3.5 m, a tightening), the minimum width of other
escape stairs in a residential (institutional) building (800 mm to 900 mm, a tightening), and the
guarding height at the edges of roofs in a dwelling (1,100 mm to 1,000 mm, a loosening). Each
was run three times on the experimental index and repeated, three times, on the 70-document
index, which contains genuine amendment booklets. A3 is qualitative by design: three cases
support description, not a rate.
Attack A4 is the defense-aware attacker. Its budget was declared before it ran on three axes:
the files it was allowed to inspect, the anchor-table fields it could see, and a cap on queries to
an oracle. Three arms were built, one per layer: adaptive_l1 rewrote the poison to improve its
semantic and outlier scores; adaptive_l2 moved the tampered value to a neighboring parameter
the anchor table does not cover; and adaptive_l3 placed the false conclusion beside its own
supporting sentence so that an entailment check would pass it. The control arm, A2's fold-0
poison set, was re-run in the same batch to avoid a restart confound.

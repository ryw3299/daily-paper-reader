Abstract
World Action Models (WAMs) connect future scene predic-
tion with action planning for autonomous driving. Driv-
ing records pair executed trajectories with observed fu-
tures, leaving alternative trajectory queries unlabeled. We
present TrajWAM, a trajectory-conditioned WAM that im-
proves vision–language driving through auxiliary bird’s-
eye-view (BEV) future learning. An ego-trajectory query
conditions DINOv3-aligned BEV predictions from shared
planner representations. Training combines factual recon-
struction under the executed trajectory with one-sided fea-
ture separation under an unlabeled perturbed trajectory.
Joint optimization with waypoint supervision transfers these
auxiliary learning signals to the shared planner. Native
inference directly generates waypoints and bypasses the aux-
iliary future-prediction branch. On all 220 Bench2Drive
routes, TrajWAM achieves 91.53 Driving Score and 75.45%
Success Rate, exceeding a schedule-matched planning-only
fine-tuning baseline by 5.31 Driving Score points and nine
successful routes.
1. Introduction
Autonomous driving requires recognizing the current scene
and anticipating its evolution under ego actions. World Ac-
tion Models (WAMs) connect observations and actions with
future scene representations [27, 32]. Driving systems learn
video, latent, or bird’s-eye-view (BEV) futures to support
planning [11, 13, 28, 40]. When future prediction and plan-
ning share parameters, observed futures also supervise the
driving policy during training. This coupling supports direct
waypoint generation at deployment.
Representation-space supervision provides a compact in-
terface for this coupling. DeepSight supervises DINOv3-
aligned BEV futures within a driving VLM [36], and Latent-
WAM trains auxiliary latent dynamics alongside a driving
policy [26]. We study how an explicit future-trajectory query
can strengthen this auxiliary supervision.
Trajectory-agnostic supervision predicts future features
from scene states. A future-waypoint query explicitly links
these predictions to ego motion (Figure 1(a)). The result-
ing training problem has asymmetric supervision: driving
records supply the observed future for the executed trajec-
tory, while alternative queries have unobserved outcomes.
Our central question is: How can trajectory queries and
observed futures jointly improve a vision–language driving
policy?
We introduce TrajWAM, a trajectory-conditioned WAM
that trains a Qwen2.5-VL-3B driving planner with auxiliary
BEV future supervision [1]. A four-waypoint ego trajectory
modulates the shared VLM’s future-query states through
feature-wise linear modulation (FiLM) [17]. A frozen read-
out maps these states to DINOv3-aligned features at the
current anchor and four future times [20]. The trajectory
enters the readout after planner-logit computation, and the
auxiliary losses backpropagate into shared planner layers.
Native inference retains the fixed future-query tokens and
directly generates reasoning and waypoints.
Our joint objective combines factual reconstruction and
perturbed-query separation (Figure 1(b)). Factual recon-
struction aligns the executed-query prediction with observed
DINOv3 targets. One-sided separation uses this prediction
as a detached reference and encourages a distinct predic-
tion under the perturbed query. Both objectives update the
shared planner, coupling observed-future supervision with
trajectory-query sensitivity.

Our contributions are:
1. Trajectory-conditioned auxiliary training for vision–
language driving. An external waypoint query condi-
tions shared future-query states, coupling BEV supervi-
sion with a policy that directly generates driving way-
points.
2. Joint learning from factual and unlabeled trajectory
queries. Factual reconstruction anchors the observed
outcome, and one-sided separation trains the readout to
respond to changes in the trajectory query.
3. Closed-loop gains supported by matched training con-
trols. Full-set evaluation establishes planning perfor-
mance; two-seed controls quantify the gains from trajec-
tory conditioning, separation, and correct query pairing.
Joint planning–prediction diagnostics relate these gains
to reconstruction quality and query responses.
2. Related Work
World action models and video-generative priors.
DreamZero [32] jointly models video and robot actions with
a pretrained video-diffusion backbone. Drive-WM [28] syn-
thesizes maneuver-conditioned multi-view driving videos for
trajectory selection. GenCeption [25] studies transferable
visual representations and vision–language alignment from
video-generative pretraining.
Representation-space futures in driving.
LAW [11] pre-
dicts perspective and BEV latents conditioned on ego tra-
jectories. WoTE [13] and World4Drive [40] use candidate-
dependent futures for online trajectory selection. UniDrive-
WM [30], DriveWorld-VLA [4], VLA-World [24], and
Latent-CoT-Drive [23] integrate future generation or latent
imagination with VLM/VLA planning. DriveVLA-W0 [12]
makes future-image prediction optional at inference, while
HyWorldVLA [33] combines latent prediction with pixel
reconstruction.

Planner-native semantic futures.
DeepSight [36] pre-
dicts DINOv3-aligned BEV futures alongside VLM reason-
ing and planning. Latent-WAM [26] predicts latent transi-
tions from scene and ego-state history, including commands,
velocity, and acceleration, using EMA-encoder targets and
omitting its dynamics module at inference. Building on
DeepSight-style future supervision, TrajWAM introduces an
external future-waypoint query into the VLM’s future states
and jointly trains factual reconstruction and perturbed-query
separation. Its training interface combines fixed DINOv3
targets for the executed query with a separation objective for
unlabeled queries.
Training-time supervision and candidate evaluation.
EgoWAM [10] compares pixel-, DINO-, and motion-
targeted supervision. Fast-WAM [34] and SimWAM [38]
bypass future prediction at inference, whereas Faster-
WAM [37] retains sparse future conditioning.
DA-
WAM [41] addresses single-outcome driving logs by su-
pervising the expert-matched candidate with future latents
and all candidates with factor, utility, and ranking targets for
online evaluation. TrajWAM uses perturbed-query separa-
tion to train shared policy representations and deploys direct
waypoint generation.
3. Method
3.1. Problem Setup and Base Planner
TrajWAM trains an action-generating VLM with a trajectory-
conditioned world readout. The readout and waypoint gen-
erator share planner states. Factual future supervision and
trajectory-query separation update the trainable planner lay-
ers through this shared representation.

3.5. Objective, Gradient Routing, and Native Infer-
ence
The auxiliary “Loss” in Figure 2(a) combines factual recon-
struction and separation; the full objective includes planning
supervision:
L = Lrec + 2Lfact + λsepLsep,
(11)
where Lrec is token-level cross-entropy for reasoning and
waypoint generation, msem is the DINO-space margin, and
λsep weights separation. Both auxiliary losses update the
ActionEncoder, latent FiLM, and final four trainable plan-
ner layers. Factual reconstruction differentiates through the
logged-query prediction; separation differentiates through
the perturbed-query prediction and uses the logged predic-
tion as a detached reference. The frozen DINO projection
transmits gradients to its input states. The vision tower and
multimodal projector remain frozen, and the frozen DINOv3
teacher supplies targets under stop-gradient. The planning
loss updates the trainable planner layers.
Native inference runs autoregressive reasoning and way-
point generation followed by the inherited PID controller.

4. Results
4.1. Experimental Setup
Phase I trains our in-house Base Planner (Phase I) from
Qwen2.5-VL-3B using autoregressive reasoning/waypoint
supervision and trajectory-agnostic DINOv3 future recon-
struction, following the DeepSight-style recipe. Phase II
adds trajectory conditioning and perturbed-query supervi-
sion to this shared starting point.
Phase II starts from this checkpoint and trains TrajWAM
for 24 epochs on 226,656 Bench2Drive examples. We use
eight A100 GPUs, BF16, an effective batch size of 64,
AdamW with cosine decay and 10% warm-up, and train-
ing seed 42 for the 220-route evaluation. Learning rates are
2 × 10−5 for the last four planner layers and 2 × 10−4 for
TrajWAM modules; no validation-based checkpoint selec-
tion is used. Optimization details appear in Appendix A;
checkpoint parameter counts appear in Appendix D.
Model variants.
Our in-house Base Planner (Phase I) ini-
tializes all four Phase-II variants in Tables 11 and 3; each
is independently fine-tuned (FT) for 24 epochs. Base Plan-
ner (planning-only FT) optimizes reasoning and waypoint
generation. Base Planner (unconditioned future FT) adds
trajectory-agnostic factual DINOv3 reconstruction.
Tra-
jWAM w/o separation conditions factual reconstruction on
the trajectory query, and TrajWAM adds the separation ob-
jective. All four variants share the Phase-I DINOv3 training
history. Table 1 compares them with published DeepSight
results and other driving methods.
Closed-loop evaluation covers all 220 routes in the
Bench2Drive Base Set and reports the official Driving Score
(DS) and strict Success Rate (SR). DS averages route comple-
tion weighted by infraction penalties, and SR counts strictly
successful routes. For the 220-route results in Tables 1, 2, 3,
and 11, each Phase-II variant uses seed 42 and one complete
simulator sweep. Table 4 uses seeds 42 and 43, with one
sweep of the same fixed 120 routes per checkpoint. Prior-
method scores come from their publications. Appendix E.1
specifies the checkpoints, new episodes, and protocol for
prediction diagnostics.

Overall closed-loop performance.
TrajWAM achieves
the highest DS (91.53) and SR (75.45%) among the meth-

SparseDriveV2 by 2.38 points and the next-best SR of
72.73% from DynVLA by 2.72 percentage points. TrajWAM

for DeepSight, and 83.10% for GeRo-Qwen2.5VL.
TrajWAM improves over the schedule-matched Base

4.09 percentage points in SR, adding nine successful routes

The trajectory-agnostic Base Plan-

71.36% SR (157/220) for Base Planner (planning-only FT).

Multi-seed subset evaluation.
The planning gains hold
across both training seeds on a fixed 120-route subset cov-

43. Mean gains are 5.55 DS over planning-only FT, 2.05 DS
over TrajWAM w/o separation, and 2.50 DS over matched
additive conditioning (Appendix F). Across the four paired
component comparisons, conditional 95% intervals lie above
zero for DS and include zero for SR (Appendix Table 13).

A. Additional Implementation Details
DINO readout and query construction.
Equations 4–6
specify the DINO readout. The ActionEncoder uses σ = 10;
FiLM is zero-initialized around the identity and broadcasts
one trajectory code over all valid query tokens and five hori-
zons. A frozen DINOv3 teacher supplies BEV supervision,
using the same valid CLS-and-patch-token mask as the pre-
diction. For A−
t , the forward coordinate is independently
scaled in [0.7, 1.3] and the lateral coordinate receives Gaus-
sian noise with standard deviation 2 m, as stated in Equa-
tion 3. The configuration uses msem = 0.05 and λsep = 0.5.
Gradient routing.
Let ϕadapt denote the final four train-
able planner layers and θ the ActionEncoder and latent-FiLM
parameters. The training gradients are
∇ϕadaptL = ∇ϕadapt(Lrec + 2Lfact + 0.5Lsep) ,
(12)
∇θL = ∇θ(2Lfact + 0.5Lsep) ,
∇θLrec = 0.
(13)
The external trajectory conditions the DINO readout after
planner-logit computation. Both factual reconstruction and
perturbed-query separation backpropagate through the DINO
projection and FiLM to ϕadapt. Equations 7 and 10 identify
the detached teacher targets and factual separation reference.
Optimization.
Training uses AdamW with cosine decay,
10% warm-up, BF16, and an effective batch size of 64 across
eight A100 GPUs. The 220-route evaluation uses training
seed 42; the matched 120-route comparisons use seeds 42
and 43. The final four planner layers use learning rate 2 ×
10−5 and the ActionEncoder and latent FiLM use 2 × 10−4.
These are the trainable components during adaptation.

C. Closed-Loop Failure Analysis
We evaluate all 220 official route records, each with a
unique route identifier.
A strict success requires status
Completed or Perfect and no infraction outside the
ignored minimum-speed category. Table 5 summarizes the
route outcomes.
Of 190 destination-reaching routes, 24 fail strict success
because of a non-minimum-speed infraction: nine route
deviations, eight vehicle collisions, five outside-route-lane
violations, one red-light violation, and one layout collision.
The remaining 30 routes terminate as shown in Table 5.
Across all 54 non-success routes, the most frequent route-
level signals are route deviation (21), vehicle collision (13),
and timeout (12). These signal counts overlap because a
route may contain several infractions. The Wilson 95% in-
terval for 166/220 is 69.36–80.67%, describing finite-route
uncertainty for this sweep.

E.2. Fixed-Query Responses and Readout Precision
Using the single-checkpoint TrajWAM and external additive
reference from Appendix E.1, we apply the same trajec-
tory pair across scenes to measure how the query response
depends on scene content. One pair is selected from devel-
opment routes using trajectory metadata before inspecting
predictions. Both queries must be within 5 m/s in speed and
20◦in endpoint direction of an evaluation window’s executed
query. This gives 252 eligible windows from 15 evaluation
routes. Response energy and cross-scene variance use spa-
tial DINO patches, with equal weight per route and equal
weight per window within each route. Future values average
the four horizons from 0.5 to 2.0 seconds; the current-time
output at t = 0 is measured separately.
For fixed queries a0, a1, the FiLM-conditioned linear
readout has the analytic difference
∆(x) = W [(γ(a1) −γ(a0)) ⊙h(x) + β(a1) −β(a0)] ,
(16)
where h(x) is the scene-dependent query state, W is the
DINO projection, and γ = 1 + ∆γ follows Equation 5.
FiLM couples trajectory modulation to h(x); bias-only con-
ditioning gives a scene-independent analytic difference. We
evaluate native BF16 predictions and an FP32 readout re-
play. The replay casts the native hidden states, conditioning
outputs, and loaded weights to FP32 for modulation and
projection, retaining the same backbone outputs. Response
energy is the mean squared difference, and variance is the
mean squared deviation from its route-balanced cross-scene
mean.
TrajWAM produces scene-dependent query responses
with FP32 readout arithmetic: its future cross-scene variance
is 4.171457×10−6, while the additive reference has zero
analytic variance. This behavior follows the scene–trajectory
interaction term in Equation 16. The current-time and future
columns in Table 9 show that this interaction operates across
the supervised sequence.

G. Limitations
Evaluation covers CARLA/Bench2Drive, with one training
seed for the full set and two for all component and shuffled-
pairing controls on the 120-route subset. Query diagnostics
measure factual reconstruction and responses to alternative
trajectories whose outcomes are unobserved; separation also
acts on the current-time anchor. The public initializer’s pre-
training route coverage is unknown. Further evaluation can
measure alternative-action outcomes, training-seed variation,
and real-world transfer.

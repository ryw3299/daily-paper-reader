Abstract
End-to-end driving models have made substantial progress,
yet reliable closed-loop driving in complex traffic remains
challenging. Beyond improving individual models, system-
level orchestration offers a complementary approach by coor-
dinating driving capabilities and computation. We introduce
ROSE-Drive (Runtime Orchestration and System Evolution),
a model-agnostic framework that jointly orchestrates driving
execution and computation. It coordinates frozen driving
models with driving skills, perception tools, and feedback
controllers. A distilled vision–language model (VLM) pro-
poses driving tasks and scene relations. Shared physical
state and persistent tasks support capability composition,
task continuity, and adaptive scheduling. The orchestra-
tor combines model and skill outputs across control chan-

1. Introduction
End-to-end driving policies have made substantial progress
across diverse traffic scenarios [6, 14, 17, 22, 31]. Beyond
improving individual policies, a complementary direction is
to coordinate their use with driving skills, perception tools,
and feedback controllers at the system level. A runtime har-
ness exposes these capabilities through common interfaces.
System-level orchestration determines how their contribu-
tions are combined and when new computation is needed.
The central difficulty is coordinating these choices
throughout an ongoing maneuver. A skill can regulate speed
while retaining a compatible model path. When the model
supplies both motion channels, persistent tracking error can
trigger a controller change. A navigation change can prompt
a new model call. The maneuver may also need to continue
after the latest scene assessment no longer requests it. These
decisions require task state that persists across changing
observations and capability outputs.
We introduce ROSE-Drive, a model-agnostic framework
that jointly orchestrates driving execution and computation
(Fig. 1). A distilled vision–language model (VLM) proposes
driving tasks and scene relations. The orchestrator binds
these proposals to observed objects and road regions to form
task requests. Shared physical state and persistent tasks
guide capability composition, task continuity, and adaptive
scheduling. These mechanisms determine which outputs to
combine, how to maintain responsibility through a maneuver,
and when computation is needed.
A shared task interface also supports cumulative improve-
ment. Skills address physical requirements that recur across
benchmarks, including traffic-interaction timing and obsta-
cle clearance. Current geometry and motion determine each
task’s constraints, allowing the same implementation to han-
dle different objects and layouts. The VLM’s relation vocab-
ulary and skill families stay fixed as execution records guide
offline improvements to skill implementations and orchestra-
tion rules. Successive system versions, called stages, inherit
these improvements.
We study four frozen models across Bench2Drive v0.0.3,
v0.0.4, and Fail2Drive, including transfer before target-
specific revision. Longest6 is reserved for evaluating frozen
systems throughout this process. AutoMoT’s driving score
(DS) progresses from 25 alone to 36, 44, and 52 across sys-
tem stages, while SimLingo progresses from 22 to 36, 39,
and 47. Both systems skip model inference on over 71% of
Longest6 control steps. Our contributions are:
• Model-Agnostic Runtime Framework. We introduce a
unified architecture that connects frozen driving models,
skills, perception tools, and feedback controllers through
shared physical state and a common task interface. Model
adapters preserve each model’s inputs and outputs while
making it available to the same runtime.
• Coordinated Execution and Scheduling. The orchestra-
tor combines model and skill outputs across control chan-
nels while persistent tasks maintain responsibility through
handover. The same task state and execution feedback
guide controller selection, motion-reference reuse, and
model inference, coupling capability composition, task
continuity, and adaptive scheduling.
• System Evolution and Transfer. A stable task interface
supports offline refinement of shared skills and orches-
tration rules with model, perception, and VLM weights
fixed. Successive versions inherit these changes and trans-
fer across benchmarks and driving models.
2. Related Work
Driving Models and Hybrid Planning.
End-to-end poli-
cies integrate perception and planning [14, 20, 40]. Gen-
erative and vision–language models expand their driving
behavior [16, 17, 22, 24, 31, 33], while temporal and closed-
loop learning further improve these policies [4, 34]. Hybrid
systems combine learned and structured components. PDM-
Hybrid combines rule-based planning with learned long-term
waypoint corrections [8], and DriveVLM-Dual couples se-
mantic reasoning with planning [35]. AsyncDriver and Au-
toMoT decouple reasoning from motion prediction [5, 17].
ROSE-Drive builds on frozen driving models and coordi-
nates their use with skills and controllers during execution.
Driving Agents.
Driving agents use language reasoning
and external capabilities to guide driving decisions. Agent-
Driver, DiLu, and LeapAD incorporate tools and experience
into this process [26, 28, 39]. DrivingAgent designs and
validates modules and trains an online scheduling agent [41].
The concurrent AutoAgent0 runtime verifies nominal trajec-
tories against refined scene context and generates recovery
proposals when none is admitted [23]. Argus supplies tem-
porary mitigation trajectories [36]. These two runtimes in-
tervene through motion proposals. ROSE-Drive coordinates
model and skill contributions within a maneuver. The model
can supply steering while a skill supplies speed, and the
task retains responsibility through handover. Our execution
comparisons examine this cooperation, task continuity, and
adaptive scheduling (Sec. 4.4).
Embodied Agent Harnesses.
Robot execution has long
used behavior trees to organize task control flow [7]
and runtime-assurance architectures to supervise controller
switching [27]. Agent harnesses connect semantic reason-
ing to physical execution through shared context, callable
capabilities, and execution contracts [25, 37]. Harness VLA
exposes a frozen VLA as a contact-rich primitive alongside
analytic primitives [42], while RoboHarness uses execution
memory to route subtasks among heterogeneous policies and
prepare compatible handoffs [15]. DynaHarness, Harness-

3. ROSE-Drive
ROSE-Drive coordinates driving capabilities through shared
physical state and persistent tasks (Fig. 2). Shared physical
state Et records tracked objects, geometry, motion, observa-
tion times, and validity. Perception, decision, and control
harnesses expose capabilities to the orchestrator, while skill
contracts define when and how each skill participates in a
task.
3.1. Task State and Model Interface
Capability composition uses navigation, lateral, and longitu-
dinal channels W = {nav, lat, lon}. The navigation channel
adjusts model inputs. Lateral and longitudinal outputs are na-
tive commands or motion references (target paths or speeds
for tracking). Adapters preserve each model’s observations
and output conventions.
A task maintains a driving requirement as the scene
changes, while a skill supplies its execution logic. Task
τi = (bi, ϕi, Wi) stores scene bindings bi, status ϕi, and
required channels Wi ⊆W. A binding links the task to
observed entities or road regions.
Channel ownership αt(c) identifies the task responsible
for output selection and recovery, with 0 denoting no owner.
Output source st(c) identifies whose output is applied, with
0 denoting the driving model and i the skill instance serving
τi. Each channel uses one source, and unowned channels
default to the model. A task can retain ownership while
using compatible model output. Controller kt executes the
selected output, and at requests model inference. Validity

3.2. VLM Proposals and Shared Physical State
The perception harness maintains Et by associating detec-
tions, tracks, and occupancy. It checks whether observations
are current and sufficient for the task. Shared scene bindings
keep task selection, motion generation, and execution checks
tied to the same interaction.
Geometric rules generate candidate scene relations Ct. A
distilled Qwen3-VL-2B evaluates them to propose driving
tasks and scene relations Rt. The orchestrator binds these
proposals to observed objects and road regions, forms task
requests, and checks skill applicability and channel availabil-
ity before admission. Ongoing tasks continue according to
execution feedback even without new VLM proposals.
The VLM is trained open-loop using PDM-Lite expert
contexts and teacher-reviewed relation labels, then frozen
throughout system evolution and evaluation. VLM infer-
ence is scheduled asynchronously every five control steps,
while the driving model is queried on demand. Appendix A

3.3. Skill Library
Four skill families address recurring physical requirements
(Table 1). Observed objects and road regions determine each
task’s geometry and motion constraints. Five VLM relation
families identify these requirements, with emergency yield-
ing and traffic rules handled by the same rule-compliance
family.
Traffic interaction plans departure timing and speed under
predicted traffic occupancy along an available path, includ-
ing a model path. Crossings and merges share this space–
time planner, with geometry, traffic motion, and conflict
type determining its constraints. Obstacle passage plans
a path around obstructions and back to the lane, with tim-
ing constrained by tracked traffic. Obstacle extent and free
road boundaries determine clearance. Different obstructions
change these constraints while reusing the same skill imple-
mentation. Rule compliance maintains traffic obligations
and task progress across observations (Appendix C).

3.5. Task Continuity and Handover
Task completion di and handover readiness hi serve different
purposes. Completion checks whether the task has fulfilled
its requirement. Readiness checks whether the model can
continue from the resulting physical state using valid outputs.
A task is Active during waiting, execution, and handover
checks, Monitor during a model trial, and Idle after termi-
nation. A task can return channels before terminating. A
pending request has not acquired channels.
For traffic interaction, handover readiness starts a moni-
tored model trial. The output source switches to the model

3.7. System Evolution
The relation vocabulary, skill contracts, and control chan-
nels form a stable task interface. Across stages, the VLM
proposes the same types of driving tasks and scene relations,
while execution records guide offline revisions to skill imple-
mentations and orchestration rules. Crossings and merges,
for example, share a traffic-interaction planner whose con-
straints are instantiated from each task’s current geometry
and motion. Improvements to this shared implementation
can therefore be reused by both, and later stages inherit them
through the same interface.
Revisions also refine the construction of shared physi-
cal state, controller selection, reference reuse, and model
inference. Coding agents analyze records, implement revi-
sions, and check execution. Model, perception, and VLM
weights remain fixed across stages. Frozen-stage evaluations
assess their transfer across benchmarks and driving models
(Appendix E).

4.3. System Evolution and Transfer
Before any v0.0.4 revision, Stage 1 transfers the v0.0.3 sys-
tem to the new route set. Across all 220 routes, AutoMoT
DS rises from 84.3 to 95.1 and SimLingo from 86.6 to 92.7
(Table 6). This tests reuse across new layouts within the
same 44 interaction types. Stage 2 transfers the expanded
system to Fail2Drive, raising AutoMoT from 74.8 to 81.3
DS and from 64.0% to 82.0% SR. SimLingo’s SR rises from
67.2% to 80.0%. With SimLingo, the system constructed
using AutoMoT also transfers across models through an
adapter.
On Longest6, AutoMoT progresses from 25 DS alone to
36, 44, and 52 across Stages 1–3, while SimLingo progresses
from 22 to 36, 39, and 47 (Fig. 4 and Appendix E). Each
system retains its sensor configuration and driving model,
perception, and VLM checkpoints across these stages. Sim-
Lingo’s progression from 36 to 39 to 47 demonstrates accu-
mulated skill and orchestration capability with the driving
model and shared perception fixed. Final RC reaches 91%
and 84%, respectively, from 70% for each model alone.

5. Conclusion
ROSE-Drive coordinates frozen driving models, skills, and
tools through shared physical state and persistent tasks. Joint
orchestration of execution and inference improves closed-
loop driving. A stable task interface supports capability
accumulation, demonstrated by frozen-stage transfer across
benchmarks and driving models.

Supplementary Material
This supplement provides implementation details (Ap-
pendix A), shared physical state and VLM outputs (B), skill
contracts (C), handover conditions (D), system evolution
(E), execution analysis and measurement protocols (F), and
benchmark results and baseline sources (G).
A. Implementation and VLM Inputs
Model adapters preserve each driving model’s sensor, coor-
dinate, navigation, and output conventions.
A.1. Perception Reuse and Model Inputs
AutoMoT, TransFuser++, and the evaluated BridgeDrive all
use TransFuser-family perception with object detection and
BEV-semantic outputs. AutoMoT’s native model uses frozen
TransFuser++ camera–LiDAR features. The perception har-
ness applies detection and BEV-semantic heads from the
same pretrained checkpoint to these features. This check-
point also belongs to the TransFuser++ model’s pretrained
ensemble, and its adapter uses the same camera and LiDAR
configuration. Tracking and occupancy processing turn pre-
dictions and point clouds into shared physical state.
BridgeDrive uses the LEAD adaptation with a TFv6
TransFuser backbone, a CenterNet detection head, and a
BEV-semantic decoder [24, 29]. Its native inputs include
cameras, LiDAR, and radar. The adapter preserves these
inputs and the frozen TFv6 model. The common PDM-Lite-
trained TransFuser++ module supplies shared physical state
to the harness through detection and BEV-semantic outputs.
All detectors retain their pretrained weights.
SimLingo retains its visual encoder and camera-only
model inputs, while its complete system additionally uses the
common camera–LiDAR module. For each model, sensor
configuration and learned checkpoints remain fixed across
Stages 1–3, so comparisons between these stages evaluate
system revisions with the same learned perception.
A.2. VLM Inputs and Update Schedule
The runtime uses three update schedules. A local VLM
query is launched in the background every five control steps.
Reference validity, control demand, and handover checks
determine when to call the driving model. The orchestrator
updates tasks and channel allocation at every control step,
using new VLM proposals when available. Proposals retain
their observation timestamps, allowing the interface to reject
stale proposals while continuing admitted tasks from current
shared physical state. Each control step advances 0.05 s
of simulation time. The distilled VLM supplies the online
proposals.
The VLM is Qwen3-VL-2B [30] adapted with LoRA [12].
Its inputs combine front-view images at simulation-time off-
sets of −1.5, −1.0, −0.5, 0 seconds, a compact description
of shared physical state, and perception-derived relation
candidates. The orchestrator maintains active-task state sep-
arately. An empty proposal requests no new task, while an
active task continues according to its execution conditions.
The LoRA adapter uses rank 64, scaling factor 128, and
dropout 0.05 on the language model’s attention and feed-
forward projections. The four front-view frames are resized
to 512 × 256 pixels.
Appendix F describes the wall-clock latency measure-
ment protocol.
A.3. VLM Training
The VLM is trained with cross-entropy averaged over answer
tokens, with prompt tokens masked from the loss. Relation
and auxiliary visual-question-answering examples receive
weights of 1.0 and 0.5, respectively.
We first adapt Qwen3-VL-2B to driving using PDM-Lite
contexts [43] and SimLingo training question-answer pairs,
then train its LoRA adapter on relation labels. Relation train-
ing samples from a dataset of 116,903 examples, including
4,000 auxiliary question-answer pairs from the SimLingo
training split. Training runs for 1,200 optimizer updates with
an effective batch size of 16. AdamW uses learning rate
5 × 10−5, zero weight decay, a 60-step linear warmup, and
cosine decay. Open-loop training is completed during initial
system construction, and the VLM remains fixed across all
reported stages.
A.4. Teacher-Reviewed Relations
VLM training labels are constructed from expert driving
clips and yielding episodes. Rules use annotated future
scene evolution to propose candidate relations, and GPT-6
reviews every candidate. During initial system construction,
development records of scenes requiring skill intervention
inform the teacher prompts. The teacher receives scene im-
ages, a description of shared physical state, and numbered
candidates. It can confirm or reject a candidate, correct its
scene bindings, or mark the available observations as insuffi-
cient. Confirmed candidates and candidates with corrected
bindings become training labels. Future scene annotations
are used only to construct offline labels.
Each target relation identifies its family, subject, counter-
part, region, and task.
A.5. Control Between Model Calls
At each control step, the runtime tracks the selected refer-
ences or forwards native commands on their assigned chan-
nels. Cached references expire after ten control steps and can
be invalidated earlier by scene, task, or navigation changes.

C. Physical Execution Architecture and Skill
Contracts
C.1. Tasks and Skill Instances
Skill families address recurring geometric, temporal, and
traffic-rule requirements. Each skill implementation com-
putes the task’s constraints from measured geometry and
motion. Persistent tasks link these requirements to scene en-
tities and retain execution status and channel responsibility.
Admission grants the skill instance access to the required
channels. Pending requests wait until applicability and chan-
nel conditions are met. Once admitted, a task continues
across VLM updates according to its completion and han-
dover conditions.
Binding first resolves the counterpart and region named
by a relation, then identifies the observations needed by the
skill. For a merge, these include the front and rear vehicles
bracketing the gap. Task identity can remain stable while
these observations change. Before the vehicle commits to
a maneuver, new valid observations can update the binding.
After commitment, the task evaluates changes as part of the
continuing maneuver or recovery.
C.2. Skill Contract
The skill contract defined in the main paper specifies ap-
plicability Ωi, required channels Wi, output generation κi,
completion di, and handover readiness hi. Each applied
channel has a single source.
Rule-compliance tasks retain obligations and progress
across observations, including traffic-signal and STOP han-

skill contributions, while the safety filter checks the com-

C.3. Model Adapter and Controller Selection
The adapter constructs navigation inputs and converts model
outputs to the shared execution interface.
Trajectory-
producing models use a tracker, while control-producing
models retain native commands. Route guidance adjusts
navigation before a model query. Lateral references are se-
lected before tracking, and longitudinal contributions adjust
the command on their allocated channel. The safety filter
checks the composed command. Execution monitoring can
request a new prediction when continuation requires a fresh
reference.
The orchestrator selects between feedback and learned
controllers. The feedback controller uses PID tracking. The
learned controller is a frozen reference-tracking multilayer
perceptron (MLP) with three 512-unit hidden layers, Lay-
erNorm, and SiLU activations. It receives a 20-point path,
target speed, ego-motion feedback, the previous command,
and disturbance estimates.
Its nine predicted longitudi-
nal/steering command pairs use a tanh output, with the first
pair applied. Training minimizes closed-loop tracking error
through a calibrated vehicle-dynamics model. Feedback con-
trol tracks model or skill references, while learned control is
selected for eligible model-reference tracking.

C.4. Traffic-Interaction Planning and Execution
The traffic-interaction skill plans motion along an avail-
able path while the task maintains its interaction goal. The
path can come from the model or a task-specific reference.
Tracked vehicles are propagated along their observed courses
using estimated speed. Oriented body overlap with ego path
samples produces a space–time occupancy grid over an 8 s
horizon at 0.1 s intervals. Crossing and same-direction traf-
fic contribute conflict and headway constraints, respectively.
Crossings and merges share the planner while current ge-
ometry and motion specify its constraints. Static occupancy
limits the space available for stopping and passage.
Dynamic programming searches longitudinal position,
speed, actuator-response state, and the preceding accelera-
tion command. Each transition integrates a candidate acceler-
ation and checks occupancy, speed bounds, and task-specific
motion constraints. Terminal states either clear the conflict
or retain sufficient stopping space before it. The objective
accounts for completion time, clearance margin, acceleration
effort, and changes in acceleration. State discretization and
dominance pruning retain competing feasible trajectories.
An unsuccessful coarse search is retried at finer position and
speed resolution.
Execution uses the first command and retains the remain-
ing plan with its original time reference. Before reuse, the
remaining commands are rolled forward from the current
measured state against refreshed occupancy and the same mo-
tion constraints. A valid retained plan can therefore continue
between searches. When no feasible departure exists, an un-
committed task waits or decelerates. An already committed
task instead compares recovery trajectories using collision
and motion-constraint violations before comfort costs. The
task remains active during recovery, and the common safety
filter checks the applied command.

C.6. Safety Filtering
The common safety filter checks the composed command
against applicable traffic-rule obligations and observed con-
tact constraints. Signal and STOP tasks maintain their obli-
gations across steps. Contact checks use tracked bodies and
LiDAR occupancy along the proposed motion. Braking or
speed restrictions can override longitudinal contributions
from the model or any skill. The task remains responsible
during these adjustments, with release governed by comple-
tion and handover checks.

D.2. Completion and Handover Readiness
Completion evaluates local task fulfillment. Handover readi-
ness evaluates whether the model can continue from the pose,
velocity, route context, and observed conflicts resulting from
the intervention. For example, after the vehicle passes an
obstruction, the task can remain active while it guides the
vehicle back into its lane.
When handover includes a model trial, the model supplies
control while the task retains channel ownership. The run-

can continue. If the trial fails, the task can resume control.
Channels can also be returned while a task is still active. For

D.4. Channel Allocation
Channel allocation is non-preemptive. Existing holders re-
tain their channels. Requests acquire free channels only
when their bindings and applicability conditions are satisfied
(Appendix C). Channel-set expansion is atomic, preserving

contraction releases the removed channels. A task’s outputs

E. System Evolution
System evolution uses execution records to improve skill
execution and orchestration offline. Revisions change ap-
plicability conditions, output generation, and computation
scheduling through the same task interface. All stages retain
the five relation families, four skill families, and three con-
trol channels. The driving model, perception networks, and
VLM keep the same weights throughout these stages.
E.1. Offline Revision and Inheritance
Crossings and merges share a traffic-interaction planner.
Each task supplies its own geometry and traffic motion, so a
change to the planner can serve both maneuvers. Later stages
inherit the revised planner through the same skill contract.
For either maneuver, the orchestrator can pair the planner’s
speed output with a compatible model path.
Execution records link observations and task bindings
to the outputs applied on each channel and the resulting
motion. Coding agents use these records to locate an unmet
requirement, revise the skill or its coordination rules, and
evaluate the revised behavior.
E.2. Physical Mechanisms Behind Revisions
Yielding along the intended path.
During a turn, a pedes-
trian near the exit can conflict with the intended path while
lying outside the vehicle’s current heading direction. Pro-
jecting the interaction along the intended path lets yielding
account for the space the vehicle is about to traverse. The
projection is recomputed as road orientation and participant
positions change. Stage 2 inherits this mechanism from
Stage 1.
Obstacle passage before crossing.
A vehicle partially ob-
structing the ego lane before a straight junction creates a
dependency between passage and crossing. Admitting the
crossing task first can reserve channels needed to pass the ob-
struction. The revised applicability condition defers crossing
when measured clearance permits passage to finish before
the junction, allowing the passage task to acquire the chan-
nels first.
The condition requires an active same-direction maneuver,
a fresh measured obstruction endpoint, and straight-ahead
junction commands. It projects the obstruction’s far endpoint
toward the junction and checks that the remaining separation
accommodates the ego half-length and a junction clearance.
Applicability follows this measured spatial relationship, al-
lowing the same rule to handle different obstacle positions
and junction layouts.

Fixed-schedule updates.
This variant retains the model,
perception and VLM weights, skills, and controller imple-
mentations. It queries the model on three of every ten control
steps and updates perception and orchestration on a fixed
67% of steps. Skill applicability, planning, channel alloca-
tion, and controller-selection rules operate at these updates.
Between updates, selected controllers track retained refer-
ences using current vehicle state. The VLM keeps its origi-
nal cadence. Sensor ingestion, feedback control, and safety
checks continue at every control step.
Paired route analysis.
We pair outcomes by route and
bootstrap the 62 route pairs with 20,000 resamples. The 95%
percentile intervals for the full system’s DS advantage are
[1.35, 8.52] over direct handover, [1.13, 7.74] over model-
controlled cruising/following, and [8.06, 17.86] over per-
ception and safety alone. These intervals describe variation
across the evaluated routes.
The full system incurs zero vehicle collisions, compared
with four across three routes for direct handover and five
across five routes for model-controlled cruising/following.
Perception and safety alone incur 17 vehicle collisions across
16 routes.

I. INTRODUCTION
Reasoning-based vision-language-action (VLA) models are
increasingly used as end-to-end planners for autonomous driv-
ing [1–3]. Given a scene and a driving prompt, these models
can generate multiple plausible trajectory candidates rather
than committing to a single future. This creates a trajectory se-
lection problem, because only one candidate can be executed.
Proposal-based driving systems therefore learn trajectory scor-
ers or selectors to rank the generated candidates [4]. Best-
of-K metrics such as minADE@K, however, select the best
candidate in hindsight and thus mainly measure whether the
generated set contains a good trajectory. At deployment, the
system must instead determine which candidate is most likely
to produce the best closed-loop outcome before any candidate
is executed.
Existing online trajectory evaluators commonly address
this problem through explicit imagination. WoTE [5], for
example, conditions a BEV world model on each candidate,
predicts candidate-specific future BEV states, and evaluates
the trajectory from those imagined outcomes. This makes
future consequences explicit, but it requires a purpose-trained
world model and additional candidate-conditioned prediction
at inference time. Yet a large reasoning-based VLA has already
integrated the observed scene, driving intent, and interaction
context while generating its candidate trajectories. It remains
unknown whether the hidden representation produced during
this process contains enough scene-specific information to
evaluate the candidates without explicitly predicting their
futures.
To answer this question, we introduce AlpaSafe (Fig. 1),
a risk-aware trajectory selector for reasoning-based driving
VLAs. AlpaSafe captures the final-layer hidden states of the
frozen VLA’s prompt prefill during the same forward pass
that generates the candidates and reads only the last token,
a byproduct of generation that requires no additional VLA
computation. A lightweight head then scores each candidate
against this token and filters the candidates on predicted
collision and offroad risk.
To isolate selection quality, we hold the candidate pool and
its official closed-loop outcome labels from the AlpaSim simu-
lator fixed across methods, and learned-selector predictions are
out of fold at the scene level. Matched ablations that replace
the last token with a zero token or with the full prefill sequence
test whether the reused representation matters, and uncertainty
is reported with paired scene-bootstrap intervals. The selector
has 1.7M parameters and runs its full selection path in 0.75 ms
per eight-candidate decision on one GPU, whereas one closed-
loop candidate rollout in the same simulator takes about 111 s.

II. RELATED WORK
Driving scenes often admit several valid maneuvers, mo-
tivating candidate-based formulations that preserve multiple
plausible trajectories rather than regress a single averaged
future [6, 7]. End-to-end planners encode the current observa-
tion using BEV, vectorized, or object-level scene representa-
tions [8–11], while driving foundation models combine vision-
language features with generative trajectory decoders [1–
3, 12]. Once multiple ego trajectories are generated, however,
planning becomes a selection problem.
One family of methods scores each candidate directly
from its geometry and the current scene, without predict-
ing a candidate-conditioned future at inference time. Early
neural motion planners learn costs over sampled feasible
trajectories [13]; subsequent approaches distill metric-specific
teachers or learn generalized trajectory values and metric sub-
scores [4, 14–17]. TOAD further treats a learned scorer as the
objective for test-time trajectory optimization [18]. A central
distinction among these methods is the scene evidence exposed
to the scorer. DrivoR uses multi-camera register tokens shared
by its generation and scoring decoders, while recent studies
show that intermediate VLA states can support downstream
driving decisions and trajectory refinement [19–21]. AlpaSafe
adopts this representation-reuse setting, combining every can-
didate with a shared representation extracted from a frozen
driving VLA and learning a per-trajectory score.
A second family evaluates a candidate only after construct-
ing evidence about its possible consequences. PDM rolls out
kinematic proposals against simplified agent forecasts and
ranks them using hand-designed rules [22]. DTPP replaces
this fixed evaluation with jointly learned ego-conditioned pre-
diction and context-aware cost evaluation for tree-structured
policy candidates [23]. Several world-model methods construct
richer candidate-conditioned futures: WoTE predicts dense
future BEV states, SafeDrive represents safety-critical future
entities in a sparse world, and World4Drive uses an intention-
aware latent world model to support multimodal trajectory
selection [5, 24, 25]. Foundation-model approaches instead
employ a generative VLM reward model or a VLA critic to
evaluate and refine proposed trajectories [26, 27].
The principal distinction is therefore whether selection
requires constructing a candidate-conditioned future world at
decision time, not whether future prediction is used as a train-
ing signal. WPT removes the inference-time cost by distilling
world-model guidance into a lightweight policy and discarding
the world model at deployment [28]. AlpaSafe uses conse-
quence supervision to train a per-trajectory scorer and event-
risk heads rather than the trajectory-generation policy: each
candidate is evaluated directly against the frozen VLA scene
representation, and no candidate-conditioned future world is
predicted at inference time.
III. METHODOLOGY
Fig. 1 gives an overview of AlpaSafe: one forward pass of
the frozen VLA yields both the K candidate trajectories and
the prefill hidden states, and the selector pairs the last token
of those states with each candidate.
A. Problem Setting
We work in AlpaSim, a high-fidelity closed-loop driv-
ing simulator in the tradition of public closed-loop bench-
2


marks [29–31], whose official scene score y ∈[0, 1] aggre-
gates safety, compliance, and progress into a single scalar
per rollout. Each scene contributes three decision groups at
staggered decision times. For each group, the frozen 10B-
parameter generator Alpamayo-1.5, a reasoning VLM coupled
to a trajectory decoder (Fig. 1a), samples K=8 candidate
trajectories from one prompt prefill. Every candidate is then
executed in closed loop and receives its own official score,
giving 24 scoring rollouts per scene. In our data the official
score equals zero when collision-at-fault or offroad occurs and
otherwise equals the simulator’s progress_score. Each
rollout also carries separate collision-at-fault and offroad event
labels.
Given a group g with candidates τ1, . . . , τK and scores
y(τk), a selector π commits to ˆτ = π(g). We evaluate two
primary quantities: selected regret, regret(g) = maxk y(τk)−
y(ˆτ), the selected candidate’s gap from the oracle outcome,
for which lower is better, and tie-aware top-1 agreement,
1[ y(ˆτ) ≥maxk y(τk) −ϵ ] with ϵ = 10−6, for which higher
is better. Both are averaged over the groups of a scene and
then equally over scenes (scene-macro mean).
B. Same-Prefill Representation Extraction
A representation-reuse claim is confounded if the repre-
sentation could come from a different forward pass than the
candidates, for example a different context window, differ-
ent preprocessing, or a silently re-run prefill. We eliminate
this by construction. A capture hook inside the production
predict() path records the final transformer layer’s post-
normalization hidden states of the prompt prefill, H
∈
R3086×4096, during the same call that samples the K can-
didates (Fig. 2); the prefill length is the same for every
group. An exactly-one-prefill assertion makes a second prefill
in the call fail the capture rather than silently substitute
context. The scene vector used at deployment is the last
row, h = H[−1] ∈R4096. Capture-time SHA-256 hashes of
both tensors establish bit-exact provenance, with h ≡H[−1]
verified for every group, and every downstream artifact, from
training tables to locked predictions and analysis records,
carries these hashes forward. Candidates and representation
are therefore co-produced, and the closed-loop scores remain
linked to the corresponding candidates.

E. Evaluation Protocol
Training and evaluation use five-fold cross-validation with
one scene-level partition shared by all learned selectors;
each fold has 357 training, 63 inner-validation, and 105
test scenes. Every scene is scored by the model that held
it out. The selection rule in (6) was adopted after earlier
outcome analyses on these scenes. It is applied to the stored
out-of-fold relative scores and collision/offroad logits, with
model weights and predictions unchanged. The common-pool
comparison is therefore exploratory rather than an independent
confirmatory test. Uncertainty estimates use a paired scene
percentile bootstrap with 10,000 resamples that keeps all
groups of a sampled scene together. The 95% intervals are
pointwise, without multiple-comparison correction, and we
call a difference supported when its interval excludes zero.

V. CONCLUSION
We presented AlpaSafe, a risk-aware trajectory selector
that reads the last hidden token of a frozen driving VLA’s
prompt prefill, filters candidates on predicted collision and
offroad risk, and adds neither VLA training nor inference-
time rollouts. On a fixed pool of 525 scenes with eight
candidates per group, it obtains lower selected regret, higher
top-1 agreement, and a lower collision-at-fault or offroad rate
than both Alpamayo’s default first-sample rule and uniform
random selection. Matched ablations on the same pool show
that a scene-blind input raises the event rate, whereas reading
the full prefill sequence instead of its last token yields no
supported gain in regret or top-1 agreement.
The selection rule was adopted on previously evaluated
scenes, and the comparisons rely on pointwise intervals with-
out multiple-comparison correction, so the results are ex-
ploratory rather than confirmatory. Risk filtering can sacrifice
official-score performance, and non-dominated predictions do
not guarantee safety. The results also rest on cached closed-
loop outcomes of fixed candidates and do not cover repeated
online replanning. In future work, we will evaluate the selector
under online replanning and explore whether the last-token
representation, paired with a candidate trajectory, can seed a
rollout in the style of a world model.

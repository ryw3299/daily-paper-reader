ABSTRACT
Off-road vision-language-action (VLA) models generate scene-conditioned plans, but frequent trajec-
tory updates can repeatedly invoke expensive multimodal inference. Reusing a prediction reduces
this demand while leaving its commands exposed to changes in vehicle motion. FastRoverVLA
addresses this mismatch by decoupling visual plan generation from motion-conditioned execution.
Its training-free interface maintains decoded plans between visual predictions. A speed–curvature
action buffer is aligned with measured motion at each update, integrated into a local trajectory, and
advanced by consuming the current action. The corrected remainder is written back to the buffer,
preserving motion corrections across successive updates. A bounded refresh rule normalizes speed
and curvature variations within a newly decoded plan and assigns a short or long reuse budget
according to the resulting plan-roughness score. The interface requires neither backbone features
nor an additional learned driving policy. Over a 3 s horizon, independent real-request evaluation
achieves average displacement error (ADE) and final displacement error (FDE) of 0.328/0.596 m
on ORAD-3D and 0.266/0.526 m on RELLIS-3D, reducing FDE by 18.0% and 36.7% at request
rates of 6.0% and 4.8%, respectively. These runs retain lower errors across four backbones and both
datasets and reduce Qwen3-VL request-processing cost by factors of 16.6 and 20.8 under serial replay,
while mechanism and motion-only controls identify persistent state correction as the main source of
the observed accuracy benefit. The source code and evaluation materials are publicly available at
https://github.com/ADEPT-ORG/FastRoverVLA.

Introduction
Off-road driving requires motion decisions in environments without fixed lane structure, where surface conditions,
visibility, and feasible routes can change within a journey [1–3]. Vision-language-action (VLA) models connect
visual observations and language-based reasoning to numerical actions or trajectories [4–7]. Their outputs must also
accommodate the vehicle motion that follows prediction. A scene-conditioned plan can remain relevant across several
trajectory updates, while its speed and curvature commands require continued adjustment to the measured state.
Coupling every trajectory update to a new prediction repeatedly invokes multimodal inference [8–11]. Efficient encoders
and inference pipelines reduce the cost of individual calls [12,13], while slow–fast systems combine expensive reasoning
with a faster planning path [6,14]. Action-chunk methods address continuity between predicted sequences through
temporal aggregation, constrained generation, or learned correction [15–17]. These developments leave a specific
execution problem at the decoded-action interface: retaining the motion corrections accumulated after a plan has been
generated, without another learned planner or access to the generative sampler.
A retained plan must account for two changes during reuse: commands are consumed as time advances, and measured
motion changes the state to which the remaining commands must be matched. Direct replay advances the sequence
without resolving this state mismatch. Realigning the original plan at each update incorporates current motion but

FastRoverVLA decouples visual plan generation from recurrent execution around a frozen vision-language model
(VLM) and a deterministic action adapter. The name combines “Fast” for reduced VLM inference demand, “Rover”
for off-road driving, and “VLA” for vision-language-action planning. Visual inference refreshes the action buffer,
while recurrent execution maintains motion-conditioned trajectories between refreshes. Each update aligns the retained
speed–curvature actions with measured ego motion, generates a local trajectory, consumes the current action, and writes
the corrected remainder back to the buffer. A bounded rule uses variation within the refreshed plan to assign its reuse
budget. The resulting interface separates visual plan generation from motion-conditioned trajectory updates without
modifying the backbone or training an auxiliary driving policy.
Evaluation on ORAD-3D [3] and RELLIS-3D [1] separates three questions: whether decoded plans benefit from
persistent correction, whether visual initialization adds information beyond motion history, and whether sparse refresh
reduces measured inference demand. Shared-prediction controls isolate the maintenance operations; motion-only
and matched-image controls examine initialization. Backbone transfer, disjoint-sequence evaluation, and a past-only
heading check test the stability of the execution effect. Independent real-request runs measure its computational
consequence under serial replay.
The main contributions are:
• FastRoverVLA introduces an action-level execution interface for off-road VLA planning. Maintaining a
decoded plan as a recurrent state separates visual prediction from motion adaptation, allowing local trajectory
updates between model requests without an additional learned driving policy.
• The interface combines state alignment, action consumption, and correction write-back to preserve measured-
motion updates in the retained plan. A bounded refresh rule assigns reuse budgets from normalized action
variation, controlling inference demand through the same decoded-action representation.
• Controlled replay and independent real-request evaluation cover two datasets and four backbones. In real-
request evaluation, Qwen3-VL reduces 3 s FDE by 18.0% and 36.7% relative to every-update execution, while
serial request-processing cost falls by factors of 16.6 and 20.8, respectively.

Related Work
2.1
Vision-Language-Action Planning
Robotic VLA models such as RT-2 and OpenVLA [18,19] connect vision-language representations to actions. Driving
planners UniAD and VAD [20,21] emphasize planning-oriented scene representations. LMDrive, DriveLM, DriveVLM,
and EMMA [4–7] incorporate language instructions, structured understanding, or hierarchical reasoning. AutoVLA,

ORION, ReCogDrive, and SimLingo [8, 9, 11, 22] connect language or reasoning with numerical driving actions;
DriveMoE [10] specializes vision and action experts. ColaVLA, WAM-Flow, and Latent-CoT-Drive [23–25] explore
latent reasoning, parallel decoding, or world-model-based planning. Complementary work improves the information and
constraints used for planning: FocalAD [26] models decision-critical local interactions, and GraphBEV++ [27] aligns
camera–LiDAR features. GraphWorld and DriveFuture [28,29] use latent world-state or future-aware conditioning.
DriveWorld-VLA [30] integrates world modeling with VLA planning, while MindDrive [31] evaluates world-model-
generated candidates with a VLM. GuideFlow [32] constrains flow matching, and DIVER [33] applies reward-guided
diffusion to multimodal planning. These advances improve scene-conditioned plan generation, whereas decoded actions
must also remain consistent with the changing execution state. FastRoverVLA addresses this execution stage through
deterministic measured-motion updates to speed–curvature actions. Its frozen planner supplies new visual plans, while a
retained action buffer preserves corrections between predictions, separating plan maintenance from encoder, reasoning,
and decoding design.
2.2
Terrain-Aware Off-Road Planning
Off-road planning couples geometric clearance with physical traversability. BADGR and STEP [34,35] learn navi-
gation affordances or combine stochastic traversability with risk-aware planning. How Does It Feel?, V-STRONG,
and RoadRunner [36–38] learn terrain costs or traversability from visual, proprioceptive, or LiDAR observations.
TRAIL [39] supports joint path and speed optimization through continuous terrain representations; terrain-adaptive
MPC [40] incorporates vehicle–terrain interactions and online parameter estimates. OFF-EMMA [41] uses visual
prompts and self-consistent reasoning for off-road trajectory generation. RELLIS-3D, ORFD, and ORAD-3D [1–3]
provide evaluation settings with rugged terrain and varied visibility. These approaches determine where and how to
move, but measured vehicle motion can require adjustment without changing the selected route or terrain assessment.
FastRoverVLA complements terrain interpretation by updating decoded actions between visual predictions, maintaining
motion consistency without requiring new terrain labels or traversability estimates at every update.
2.3
Efficient and State-Consistent Execution
Temporal planners exploit cross-frame information: MomAD [42] carries trajectory and perception momentum,
MomADv2 [43] retains command-consistent memory and uses flow refinement, and MomWorld [44] propagates latent
future scene states. Efficiency methods improve visual encoding or model size [12,45], reduce reasoning [8,46,47], or
accelerate action generation and inference [13,48]. Slow–fast systems combine infrequent VLM planning with trajectory
refinement [6], learn adaptive activation and fusion [14], or schedule query, cache, and drop decisions [49]. These
mechanisms determine when guidance arrives, while action maintenance determines how decoded sequences evolve
during execution. ACT [15] ensembles overlapping predictions, and Diffusion Policy [50] generates receding-horizon
action sequences. RTC [16] asynchronously generates chunks while preserving committed actions and constraining
overlap through inpainting; REMAC [17] learns within-chunk corrections and preserves prefixes across chunks.
FastRoverVLA instead deterministically aligns a retained speed–curvature sequence with measured motion and writes
corrections back for subsequent updates. Its bounded refresh rule admits new visual plans, while intermediate updates
require neither sampler access nor an auxiliary learned planner. The focus is persistent state correction after decoding,
distinct from learned temporal planning, query scheduling, or new-chunk generation.
3
Methodology
FastRoverVLA maintains a speed–curvature action buffer between visual predictions (Fig. 2). A refresh initializes the
buffer from the current image, ego motion, and instruction. Subsequent updates align its actions with measured motion
and retain the corrected remainder until the reuse budget expires. A plan denotes the action sequence, and a trajectory
denotes the positions obtained by integrating that sequence.

The output horizon and refresh interval are separate parameters. Each update integrates H commands from the current
measured state to produce an H∆t-second trajectory. Recurrent correction and tail completion sustain these updates
throughout reuse, including intervals longer than the original visual prediction horizon.
Each sequence starts with an empty buffer and a plan refresh. A subsequent refresh replaces the buffer when it is
unavailable or its reuse budget is exhausted. Between refreshes, execution uses Bt−1 and current measured motion
without reading a new image. Parameters are fixed after validation. Candidate budgets are ranked by validation FDE,
then ADE and refresh rate, subject to the constraints relative to fixed-interval execution.

4
Experiments
The experiments test whether motion-conditioned plan maintenance can reduce visual inference while preserving
trajectory accuracy in off-road driving. Comparisons across planning systems and frozen backbones establish overall
performance, while controlled ablations distinguish the effects of state correction, visual initialization, and refresh
scheduling. Cross-sequence tests and independent model requests then examine whether the accuracy and inference
savings persist beyond the shared-prediction replay.
4.1
Experimental Setup
Data and Protocol. Experiments use ORAD-3D [3] and RELLIS-3D [1], with 636 evaluation frames from 11 sequences
and 650 frames from three sequences, respectively. Each frame anchors a motion history and its future ground-truth (GT)
trajectory. ORAD-3D separates validation and test sequences; the RELLIS-3D temporal splits have disjoint evaluation
timestamps and supporting history/future indices. Evaluation follows logged-state open-loop replay: measured motion
is read at every update, independently of the preceding prediction, and the buffer resets at sequence boundaries. Within
each backbone, all policies share the same evaluation frames and per-update processing. Every-update execution
admits a fresh plan at each timestamp. Fixed-interval execution retains the recurrent interface with a constant refresh
budget, while FastRoverVLA selects the budget from plan variation. Both reuse policies update the trajectory at every
timestamp, including updates without a visual prediction. The matched comparisons use only motion history available
at the current timestamp. Controlled replay reads the same cached predictions without invoking the model, and U/R
counts updates per admitted plan. Independent real-request runs generate their own plans on the same logged states.
Evaluation Metrics. Each trajectory is evaluated at six ego-relative positions, spaced 0.5 s apart over 3 s. Average
displacement error (ADE) averages the six position errors, and final displacement error (FDE) measures the 3 s endpoint
error. Intermediate L2 errors are reported at 1 and 2 s. Root mean squared error (RMSE) is computed from coordinate
errors within each sample and averaged across frames; P95FDE is the 95th percentile of FDE. All errors are in meters.
Sequence-level intervals use 10,000 paired whole-sequence bootstrap resamples, weighting sequence means equally
to account for temporal dependence within recordings. ORAD-3D uses the preceding displacement as its reference
heading. RELLIS-3D retains the archived centered-heading convention, with a past-only check in Section 4.4.
Implementation. All backbone comparisons use the same evaluation frames, GT trajectories, and execution operators.
Model weights are frozen, and execution parameters are fixed after validation. The default Qwen3-VL configuration
uses β = 0.65 and δv = 0.25 m/s, with (Ks, Kl, τ, α) = (12, 32, 0.1463, 0.40) on ORAD-3D and (8, 24, 1.2074, 0.65)
on RELLIS-3D; thresholds are rounded. Fixed reuse budgets are 12 and 8, respectively. Runtime measurements use
one RTX 4090, BF16 weights, 448 × 448 images, and temperature zero. Request cost is cumulative model-request
processing time per logged update under serial replay, excluding initialization, image preparation, and warm-up.

4.5
Inference Efficiency
FastRoverVLA reduces model requests by 91.8–95.2% across the eight backbone–dataset pairs while also lowering
ADE and FDE relative to every-update execution (Table 8). Qwen3-VL requests a fresh plan on 6.0% of ORAD-3D
updates and 4.8% of RELLIS-3D updates, reducing cumulative request-processing time by factors of 16.6 and 20.8. Its
3 s FDE simultaneously decreases by 18.0% and 36.7%. The saving comes from avoiding repeated visual inference
while motion-conditioned trajectory updates continue at every logged state.

Conclusion
FastRoverVLA maintains decoded driving plans through recurrent motion correction, allowing visual planning and
trajectory updates to run at different frequencies. State alignment, action consumption, and correction write-back
preserve a usable speed–curvature buffer between bounded refreshes. Across four frozen backbones and two off-road
datasets, the interface reduces trajectory error and model-request demand without retraining. Independent Qwen3-VL
runs reduce requests by 94.0% on ORAD-3D and 95.2% on RELLIS-3D, with 3 s FDE reductions of 18.0% and 36.7%
and request-processing savings of 16.6 and 20.8 times. The ablations show that accuracy depends on carrying motion
corrections into later updates, while bounded refresh governs inference demand. The corrected action remainder enables
later trajectory updates to use motion feedback accumulated since the last visual prediction. Closed-loop evaluation
remains necessary to assess performance under state-estimation error and changing scene conditions.

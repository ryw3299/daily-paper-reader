Abstract
Frontier models have demonstrated remarkable general-
purpose reasoning capabilities, opening new possibilities
for intelligent decision making in the physical world. In au-
tonomous driving, many recent approaches seek to leverage
these capabilities through additional adaptation for action
output, particularly in scenarios where existing models un-
derperform. We introduce Generalist Action Critic (GAC), a
zero-shot framework that combines frontier models’ general-
purpose reasoning with the planning competence of spe-
cialized driving models through an inference-time harness.
In GAC, specialized planners generate multiple candidate
trajectories and make an initial selection, while a frozen
frontier model serves as an action critic that assesses the
selected trajectory against scene evidence and either retains

performance by correcting up to 52.13% of eligible unsafe
trajectory selections while introducing new failures in only
0.67% of originally high-quality selections. These results
demonstrate the potential of harnessing frontier models to
improve driving safety through inference-time cooperation
with specialized planners.

1. Introduction
Recent advances in artificial intelligence have significantly
expanded the capabilities of general-purpose models. From
language understanding and generation to multimodal rea-
soning, complex problem solving, and tool use, frontier mod-
els are breaking traditional boundaries in the range of tasks
that general-purpose AI can perform [2, 3]. This progress
has now reached the physical world, with recent studies
demonstrating GPT-6 Astra’s strong spatial reasoning and
decision-making capabilities in robotic manipulation, navi-
gation, and other embodied AI tasks [4, 5].
Autonomous driving is a representative domain of embod-
ied AI, requiring systems to connect scene understanding
with driving decisions and vehicle control [1, 6–8]. Ex-
isting approaches organize these capabilities through dif-
ferent architectures, including planning-oriented end-to-end
(E2E) [1, 9–11], vision-language-action (VLA) [12–15], and
world–action models (WAM) [16, 17], as illustrated in Fig-
ure 1. Despite their different architectures, these paradigms
have largely advanced through task-specific training and
adaptation. As frontier models continue to advance, a key
question is how to directly leverage their general-purpose
reasoning capabilities to improve driving decisions.
Multi-candidate planning provides a concrete setting for
exploring this question. The inherently multimodal nature of
driving behavior has motivated a growing shift from predict-
ing a single trajectory toward generating multiple candidate
trajectories [18, 19]. Effective candidate selection is there-
fore essential to realizing the full potential of multimodal
planning. Yet existing planners do not always select the best
available trajectories, leaving a gap between candidate gen-
eration and selection [20]. Frontier models’ strong spatial
reasoning offers a promising way to address this gap.
To this end, we introduce Generalist Action Critic
(GAC), a new paradigm for autonomous driving that com-
bines general-purpose frontier models with specialized driv-
ing planners through inference-time cooperation. Rather
than adapting frontier models for driving action output, GAC
directly employs them as action critics to assess and improve
the decisions of specialized driving systems. In this work,
we instantiate GAC for multi-candidate trajectory planning,
where the critic evaluates the planner’s initial selection and
reselects from the available candidates when needed. Ef-
fective trajectory assessment requires understanding how
candidate trajectories interact with the surrounding environ-
ment and determining whether they are safe and appropriate
for the driving situation. We therefore develop an evidence-
grounded inference harness that integrates layered visual
representations with a structured reasoning context to sup-
port selective decision revision. We envision GAC as a step
toward a new generation of autonomous driving paradigms,
where advances in general-purpose intelligence and special-
ized planning can jointly shape future driving systems.
Our main contributions are summarized as follows:
• We introduce Generalist Action Critic (GAC), a new
paradigm for autonomous driving that enables zero-shot,
inference-time cooperation between general-purpose fron-
tier models and specialized driving planners.
• We develop an evidence-grounded inference harness that
integrates layered visual representations, a structured rea-
soning context, and selective decision revision to support
trajectory evaluation based on scene evidence.
• We demonstrate GAC’s effectiveness across three driving
planners on HUGSIM and NAVSIM v1. GAC improves
HD-Score by up to 32.1%, increases DrivoR’s HD-Score
to 43.2 on the full HUGSIM benchmark, and recovers up
to 52.13% of eligible zero-score failures.
2. Related Work
General-purpose models for embodied reasoning.
Re-
search on embodied intelligence has explored how general-
purpose models can support reasoning and decision making
in the physical world. Early work connected language mod-
els with executable robot skills, environmental feedback,
spatial representations, and visual annotations for action
selection [21–24]. In autonomous driving, language and
vision-language models have been used for driving deci-
sions, reasoning-assisted planning, and cooperation with
conventional planning systems [25–27]. However, earlier
models faced limitations in spatial reasoning and reliable
physical execution, constraining their roles in complex em-
bodied tasks [28, 29]. Recent advances in frontier models,
particularly GPT-6 Astra, have demonstrated stronger capa-
bilities in robotic manipulation and expanded their roles in
direct action generation and cooperation with learned poli-
cies [4, 30]. Frontier models have also been evaluated in
closed-loop driving [31]. These advances motivate our ex-
ploration of frontier models as action critics for evaluating
and improving driving decisions.
Data-driven autonomous driving.
Learning-based ap-
proaches have explored diverse architectures for driving per-
ception, planning, and control [6–9, 32]. UniAD [1] exem-
plifies a unified, planning-oriented end-to-end architecture
that integrates perception, prediction, and planning. Subse-
quent studies have advanced scene representations, planning
efficiency, and the interaction between prediction and plan-
ning [10, 33, 34]. Vision-language approaches incorporate
pretrained multimodal knowledge and reasoning into driving,
supporting scene understanding, language-guided control,
and trajectory planning [12, 13, 15, 35, 36]. World-model-
based approaches connect predictive world representations
with action planning, allowing predicted scene dynamics
to inform driving decisions [16, 17, 37–39]. GAC builds
on these architectures by introducing a new paradigm for
inference-time cooperation between frontier models and spe-

Multimodal planning and trajectory selection.
Earlier
planning approaches primarily predicted a single ego tra-
jectory, while recent methods increasingly model multiple
possible driving behaviors to capture the multimodal nature
of driving [18, 40–42]. Candidate sets are constructed using
predefined trajectory vocabularies [40], scene-conditioned
proposals [42, 43], and generative trajectory models [11, 44].
Selecting the trajectory to execute is therefore a criti-
cal component of multimodal planning. Hydra-MDP and
DrivoR learn to predict simulation-derived planning scores
for candidate evaluation [18, 42]. GTRS improves the gener-
alization of learned trajectory scoring by training on a dense
trajectory vocabulary with trajectory dropout [19]. More re-
cently, iDriveVLA identifies the gap between candidate qual-
ity and selection performance, introducing a safety-aware
scorer and a trained modulator that adjusts criterion weights
using frozen VLM features [20]. GAC instead leverages
frontier models’ general-purpose reasoning for trajectory
assessment and reselection without additional training.
3. Generalist Action Critic
3.1. Framework
The Generalist Action Critic (GAC) framework consists of
three components: a specialized driving planner, an evidence-
grounded inference harness, and a general-purpose frontier
model serving as the action critic, as illustrated in Figure 2.
Given a driving scene, the planner generates and scores K
candidate trajectories C = {τ0, . . . , τK−1} and selects τb,
where b denotes the original selection index. These outputs
are passed to the harness through a common trajectory in-
terface that supports different planning architectures. The
harness integrates layered visual representations with a struc-
tured reasoning context, providing shared visual input I and
stage-specific textual inputs T1 and T2 to the action critic F.
The critic performs selective decision revision by assessing
the original selection and, upon rejection, reselecting from
the existing candidates. Both the planner and the frontier
model remain frozen.
Each candidate trajectory is represented by eight time-
indexed poses over a 4-second horizon:

Decision guidelines (G1, G2).
The guidelines establish
shared conventions for coordinate frames, timestamps, ego
state, route information, and image annotations. They also
specify the evaluation metrics and their aggregation into
an overall driving score. Building on these definitions and
conventions, we specify three shared reasoning principles:
(1) Spatiotemporal reasoning. The guidelines require rea-
soning about the ego vehicle’s position relative to actors
and road boundaries over time, considering its full foot-
print. For potential conflicts with other actors, the critic
combines time-indexed candidate poses with historical
and current observations to assess whether the ego vehi-
cle and other actors may occupy overlapping regions at
the same time.
(2) Evidence and uncertainty. The guidelines require ex-
plicit evidence for every decision, including acceptance.
Justifications must explain the expected consequences of
candidate motion based on scene observations and spec-
ify their location and approximate timing where relevant.
These explanations must distinguish observed evidence
from assumptions about future behavior and explicitly
acknowledge uncertainty when it affects the judgment.
(3) Performance tradeoffs. The guidelines require the critic
to weigh safety, progress, and comfort when evaluating
candidate motion, while accounting for traffic rules. Im-
provements in one aspect must be considered alongside
potential costs in others. Lower speed or shorter travel
distance alone does not establish better performance.
Both stages share these reasoning principles but require dif-
ferent justifications. Assessment (G1) requires evidence that
supports acceptance or substantiates a specific concern war-
ranting rejection. Reselection (G2) requires comparative
evidence explaining why the final candidate is preferred.
3.4. Selective Decision Revision
GAC performs selective decision revision using layered vi-
sual representations and a structured reasoning context. The
critic first assesses the planner’s original selection and com-
pares alternative candidates only upon rejection. Each stage
uses its own model context with complete instructions.
In the first stage, the critic evaluates the planner’s selected
trajectory τb based on the driving context and decision guide-
lines. Given the shared visual input I and assessment input
T1, the critic returns an acceptance decision a ∈{0, 1} and
a supporting reason r1:
(a, r1) = F(I, T1).
(5)
Here, a = 1 indicates acceptance, while a = 0 triggers
reselection. If accepted, the planner’s original trajectory is
retained without further candidate comparison.

4. Experiments
4.1. Experimental Setup
Planners and frontier model.
We evaluate GAC with
iDriveVLA, DrivoR, and DiffusionDrive [11, 20, 42], which
generate 64, 64, and 20 candidate trajectories, respectively.
Each planner is paired with GPT-6 Astra [2] at maximum
reasoning effort. Original denotes the planner’s native se-
lection, while GAC assesses and reselects from the same
candidate set. Candidate evaluation scores and privileged
evaluator information are withheld from the critic.
Closed-loop
evaluation.
We
evaluate
GAC
on
HUGSIM [48], using its 345-scenario evaluation set
for comparison with existing methods. To enable cross-
planner evaluation while keeping frontier-model usage
costs manageable, we use HUGSIM-160, a balanced set
of 160 HUGSIM scenarios with ten scenarios from each
combination of four data sources (KITTI-360, nuScenes,
PandaSet, and Waymo) and four difficulty levels (Easy,
Medium, Hard, and Extreme).
For each planner, Original and GAC share the same check-
points, sensor adaptations, controllers, and simulation set-
tings. Planning runs at 4 Hz, with GAC assessment at 2 Hz
unless otherwise specified. The simulator waits for critic
decisions before advancing.
Open-loop evaluation.
We export candidate trajectories
for all 12,146 scenes in the NAVSIM v1 navtest split [49].
Based on the original selection’s PDMS, we divide scenes
into Failed (PDMS = 0), Intermediate (0 < PDMS <
90), and High-quality (PDMS ≥90). The Failed group
includes zero-score selections with at least one positive-score
alternative, comprising 188, 219, and 541 eligible scenes
for iDriveVLA, DrivoR, and DiffusionDrive, respectively.
For the Intermediate and High-quality groups, we sample
100 distinct scenes three times per planner and average the
results across the three samples.

Evaluation metrics.
We follow the official HUGSIM
and NAVSIM v1 evaluation protocols. Both benchmarks
use the PDM Score (PDMS), which combines a collision-
related metric (NC), drivable area compliance (DAC), time-
to-collision within bound (TTC), and comfort (C), with NC
and DAC serving as multiplicative factors. NAVSIM ad-
ditionally includes ego progress (EP). On HUGSIM, route
completion (RC) measures progress along the prescribed
route, and HD-Score is the product of RC and the mean
PDMS over an episode. For the NAVSIM ablations, we addi-
tionally report the per-instance NC × DAC score to quantify
collision avoidance and drivable-area compliance jointly. All
component scores are normalized to [0, 1], and reported re-
sults are scaled to 0–100, with higher values indicating better
performance. Additional evaluation details are provided in
Appendix B.
4.2. Closed-Loop Evaluation
Overall performance.
We evaluate DrivoR with GAC on
the full HUGSIM benchmark. As shown in Table 1, GAC im-
proves DrivoR’s HD-Score from 35.7 to 43.2 (+21.0%) and
RC from 49.8 to 55.0, achieving state-of-the-art performance
among the compared methods trained without additional sim-
ulation data.
Cross-planner performance.
We further evaluate GAC
across three planners on HUGSIM-160, as shown in Table 2.
GAC consistently improves NC and TTC across all three
planners, demonstrating improved driving safety alongside
gains in PDMS and HD-Score. DiffusionDrive achieves the
largest relative HD-Score gain of 32.1%. These improve-
ments are accompanied by stable or higher RC. Notably,
GAC further improves iDriveVLA despite its existing safety-
aware scoring mechanism.
Performance across difficulty levels.
Figure 4 shows that
GAC improves HD-Score and NC across all four difficulty
levels for all three planners. The largest HD-Score gains
occur in Medium scenarios for each planner. The smaller
gains in Easy and Extreme scenarios may reflect the strong
baseline performance in Easy scenarios and the persistent
difficulty of completing routes in Extreme scenarios. We
provide detailed breakdowns in Appendix C.
Assessment frequency.
We evaluate GAC at 1 Hz and
2 Hz on HUGSIM-64, a balanced subset of HUGSIM-160
containing four scenarios per source–difficulty combination.
Planning remains fixed at 4 Hz. As shown in Table 3, both
assessment frequencies improve PDMS and HD-Score over
native selection across all three planners, with 2 Hz yielding
larger gains in both metrics.
4.3. Open-Loop Selection Analysis
Closed-loop evaluation measures driving performance over
complete episodes, while open-loop evaluation examines
individual trajectory selections. We analyze failure recovery
and decision preservation on NAVSIM v1.
Failure recovery.
Table 4 presents the results across the
three groups. In the Failed groups, GAC improves NC, DAC,
and TTC across all three planners, recovering 42.51–52.13%
of eligible zero-score selections. Mean PDMS increases
from zero to 36.26–47.41, demonstrating that improved tra-
jectory selection can recover decisions that originally failed
safety-related evaluation.
Improvement and preservation.
Beyond recovering
failed selections, GAC also improves mean PDMS across
all three planners in the Intermediate groups, with consistent
gains in TTC. For High-quality selections, NC, DAC, and
TTC remain at or above 99.00, while only six new zero-score
failures occur among 900 evaluated instances, yielding an
overall new failure rate of 0.67%. Mean PDMS decreases
by 1.46–2.84 points, largely due to reduced ego progress,
reflecting more conservative trajectory selection. Further
analysis of the safety-degradation cases shows that violations
first occur after 1 s of the four-second evaluation horizon.
In closed-loop evaluation, GAC reassesses selections every
0.5 s using updated observations, allowing decisions to be
revised before such risks materialize. Further analyses are
provided in Appendix D.

4.4. Ablation Studies
We examine the contributions of selective decision revi-
sion and its visual and contextual inputs using matched
iDriveVLA candidate sets on NAVSIM.
Workflow ablations.
Table 5(a) compares GAC with three
alternative workflows. Uniform retains GAC’s assessment
decisions and samples uniformly from the candidate set upon
rejection. Direct selects from all candidates without prior
assessment, while Select then review evaluates a newly se-
lected candidate against the original choice. GAC substan-
tially outperforms Uniform on Failed-group PDMS, demon-
strating the importance of evidence-based candidate compar-
ison. Although Direct achieves higher Failed-group PDMS,
GAC better preserves the safety of High-quality selections,
achieving an NC×DAC score of 99.00 versus 91.00. When
both methods are evaluated on the same 54 failed cases
identified by GAC’s assessment stage, GAC recovers 50
compared with 44 under Direct, demonstrating the benefit
of assessment-guided reselection. Select then review also
yields lower PDMS than GAC in both groups.
Input ablations.
Table 5(b) examines the contributions of
reasoning principles and geometric overlays. Failed-group
PDMS drops from 49.26 to 29.22 without reasoning prin-
ciples and to 27.42 without geometric overlays. Geometric
overlays also help preserve the safety of High-quality selec-
tions, improving NC×DAC from 97.00 to 99.00. Ablation
details are provided in Appendix E.

5. Conclusion
We introduce Generalist Action Critic (GAC), a new
paradigm for zero-shot, inference-time cooperation between
frontier models and specialized driving planners. Through
an evidence-grounded inference harness, GAC integrates
layered visual representations and a structured reasoning
context to support selective decision revision. Closed-loop
experiments on HUGSIM demonstrate consistent improve-
ments in driving safety and overall performance across three
planners. GAC also enables DrivoR to achieve the high-
est HD-Score among the compared methods on the full
HUGSIM benchmark. On NAVSIM v1, GAC recovers up
to 52.13% of eligible zero-score failures. Ablation studies
further validate the effectiveness of GAC’s key components.
Together, these findings demonstrate that frontier-model rea-
soning can complement specialized driving planners through
inference-time cooperation to improve driving safety.

and the y-axis pointing left. Candidate trajectories, spatial
anchors, and future occupancy are all expressed in this fixed

Camera projection.
Let v ∈{F, L, R} denote the front,
front-left, and front-right cameras. We project the spatial

Rendering and compositing.
Anchor segments and ve-
hicle footprints are first clipped against a near plane. The
resulting geometry is then projected into the ideal image
and rendered alongside the theoretical horizon on annota-
tion layers. To match the lens distortion of the raw camera
images, we use a calibrated five-parameter Brown–Conrady
model [50] where required. The annotation canvases are
enlarged beyond the image boundaries to accommodate the
displacement of projected geometry caused by distortion.
The shifted canvas origins are accounted for in the princi-
pal points and inverse-sampling coordinates. We then use
inverse mapping to resample the annotations into the raw
image coordinates and composite them onto the original cam-
era images without undistorting or geometrically resampling
those images.

Shared reasoning principles
(1) Spatiotemporal reasoning. Consider the full vehicle footprint, including its front, rear, and corners. Use timed candidate poses and
current and historical observations to assess road-boundary violations and potential conflicts with other actors at compatible times.
(2) Evidence and uncertainty. Ground every decision, including acceptance, in scene evidence. Distinguish observations from
assumptions about future behavior, and qualify uncertainty when it affects the assessment or candidate comparison.
(3) Performance tradeoffs. Evaluate safety, progress, and comfort jointly while accounting for applicable road rules. Consider
insufficient progress and abnormal or aggressive motion alongside potential collision and drivable-area risks.

Each valid response

requires a Boolean acceptance decision, while reselection

stages require a nonempty reason and a self-reported confir-

how the earlier concern is addressed and, when the original
candidate b is retained, justifies that choice. A validated
acceptance returns b immediately, while a validated rejection
triggers reselection, whose selected candidate determines
the final choice.
B. Experimental Details
B.1. Checkpoints and Benchmark Adaptations
We use an unreleased iDriveVLA checkpoint [20], the
publicly released DrivoR NAVSIM v1 checkpoint without
SimScale [42], and the publicly released DiffusionDrive
NAVSIM checkpoint [11]. All three planners use front,
front-left, and front-right images together with ego state.
iDriveVLA and DrivoR additionally use rear-view images,
while DiffusionDrive uses LiDAR. For candidate selection,
iDriveVLA and DrivoR use learned planning-quality scores,
and DiffusionDrive uses classification scores. The critic
uses a consistent five-image configuration across planners,
together with stage-specific candidate information.
For
NAVSIM, candidate trajectories are exported before critic
evaluation and reused across selection methods. Diffusion-
Drive uses fixed per-scene randomness during candidate
generation.
Sensor and geometry adaptation.
For NAVSIM, vehicle
footprints use the nuPlan Pacifica dimensions in the rear-axle
frame, with ℓf = 4.049 m, ℓr = 1.127 m, and w = 2.297 m.
For HUGSIM, we use the ground projection of the vehicle
center as the reference point for candidate poses, spatial an-
chors, and vehicle footprints, with camera calibration defined
consistently in the same frame. The footprint dimensions
are obtained from scene metadata, with ℓf = ℓr = L/2,
where L and w denote vehicle length and width. HUGSIM
uses a pinhole camera model for image rendering, allowing
geometric annotations to be projected directly without lens-
distortion matching. For DiffusionDrive on HUGSIM, we
generate a synthetic point cloud from rendered depth, using
the same adaptation for Original and GAC.
B.2. Scenario Selection and Evaluation Protocols
HUGSIM scenarios and execution.
For the full bench-
mark comparison, we evaluate DrivoR + GAC with
assessment at 2 Hz on the 345-scenario pre-challenge
HUGSIM [48] evaluation set used by DrivoR [42]. The
set contains 48 Easy, 127 Medium, 86 Hard, and 84 Extreme
scenarios. We compare GAC with previously published re-
sults from DriveZero [47] and DrivoR [42] under the same
evaluation protocol, with overall scores averaged equally
across all 345 scenarios.
For cross-planner evaluation, we construct HUGSIM-160
by randomly sampling 10 scenarios from each combination
of four data sources (KITTI-360, nuScenes, PandaSet, and
Waymo) and four difficulty levels (Easy, Medium, Hard,
and Extreme). All three planners are evaluated on the same
scenarios, with GAC and Original compared under identical
settings. GAC assessment runs at 2 Hz on HUGSIM-160. We
further construct HUGSIM-64 by randomly sampling four
scenarios from each data source and difficulty combination
within HUGSIM-160. This subset is evaluated at both 1 Hz
and 2 Hz to compare critic assessment frequencies.
The simulator and planner operate at 4 Hz, with GAC
reviewing the planner’s selection every two planning steps
at 2 Hz or every four steps at 1 Hz. At each planning up-
date, the planner generates new candidates from the current
observations, and only the initial segment of the selected tra-
jectory is executed. The planner’s original selection is used
at steps without critic assessment, and the simulator waits
for the critic response when assessment is triggered. All fre-
quencies refer to simulation time, with simulator and planner
random seeds fixed at zero. Missing historical observations
are explicitly indicated in the image attachments.
NAVSIM scenarios.
The Failed group includes original
selections with zero PDMS and at least one positive-score al-
ternative. All 188, 219, and 541 eligible scenes are included
for iDriveVLA, DrivoR, and DiffusionDrive, respectively.
The Intermediate and High-quality groups are defined by
original PDMS scores in (0, 90) and [90, 100], respectively.
14


For each planner, we independently sample three sets of
100 distinct scenes from each of the Intermediate and High-
quality groups. Scenes may appear in multiple sets, and each
occurrence is included in the reported statistics.

E. Ablation Details
E.1. Workflow Ablations
Original uses the planner’s original selection without critic
intervention. Direct bypasses assessment and selects from
all 64 candidates in a single call. It retains the reasoning
principles and five-image configuration, including spatial
anchors but excluding the original candidate’s footprint over-
lays, explicit original-selection information, and assessment
feedback. Select then review first selects a candidate inde-
pendently and then compares it with the planner’s original
selection to determine the final choice between the two. This
variant preserves the reasoning principles, spatial anchors,
and original candidate’s footprint overlays.
Uniform retains GAC’s recorded assessment decisions
and accepted selections, replacing only the reselection strat-
egy after rejection. It selects from all 64 candidates with
equal probability, including the original candidate, without
additional reselection model calls.
We compute the expected PDMS of Uniform analytically.
For each scene s, let a(s) = 1 indicate acceptance and
a(s) = 0 rejection, with qb(s) denoting the original selec-
tion’s PDMS and qi(s) the score of candidate i among Ks
candidates. The expected final score is

E.2. Input Ablations
The ablation without reasoning principles retains the field
definitions, coordinate and image conventions, evaluation
definitions, and response schemas. The ablation without ge-
ometric overlays removes spatial anchors and projected vehi-
cle footprints from the images while retaining the reasoning
principles. Both configurations preserve GAC’s assessment
and conditional reselection procedure.

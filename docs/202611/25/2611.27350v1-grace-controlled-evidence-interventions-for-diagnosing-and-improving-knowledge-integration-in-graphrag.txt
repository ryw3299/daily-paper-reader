Abstract. GraphRAG systems answer knowledge-intensive questions
by combining a language model’s parametric knowledge with relational
evidence retrieved from a graph. Aggregate accuracy, however, obscures
whether failures arise from missing paths, distractors, or preference for
memorised knowledge. Diagnosing these behaviours requires changing
the evidence while holding the question fixed. We introduce GRACE,
constructed by executing LC-QuAD 2.0 SPARQL queries over Wikidata
to recover answers and supporting paths, extracting an intact evidence
subgraph for each question, and deriving four controlled interventions
from it: severing a supporting path, injecting type-compatible distrac-
tors, attaching high-degree hubs, and replacing the supported answer
with a counterfactual entity. All five states share the same question and
option positions, with targets derived from their evidence; retaining only
validated five-state groups yields 4,028 questions and 20,140 question–
graph pairs. These comparisons expose two limitations: question-only
routing cannot distinguish different evidence states for the same ques-
tion, and relevance guidance improves answer-supporting states but re-
duces accuracy when the support path is severed. The latter shows that
individually useful facts do not necessarily provide sufficient collective
support. This motivates QCFSE-SA, which jointly models question–fact
relevance and graph-level sufficiency. Typed local attention follows con-
nections between facts, global attention integrates the complete sup-
plied graph, and question-conditioned readouts provide organisational
guidance to a frozen generator. A separately calibrated graph-support
gate controls abstention, addressing insufficiency that relevance guid-
ance alone does not resolve. With identical factual context and a frozen
generator, QCFSE-SA raises controlled-state accuracy from 57.17% to
62.78% (+5.61 points; question-clustered 95% CI [+5.04,+6.17]) and
broken-state accuracy from 55.41% to 67.85%, although over-abstention
remains a challenge on semantic distractors.

Introduction
Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) combines parametric knowledge with
inference-time evidence [27, 9], while graph-based variants organise that evidence
as entities, relations, or hierarchical summaries [7, 12, 41]. This combination cre-
ates a basic attribution problem: a correct answer does not reveal whether the
generator actually used the retrieved graph or simply relied on knowledge al-
ready encoded in its parameters. Consider the query “What currency is used by
the country whose capital is Paris?”. A language model may already associate
France with the euro, while a retrieved graph may independently support the
same answer through the path Paris →France →Euro. If the system outputs
Euro, correctness alone cannot tell which source determined the prediction.
This ambiguity becomes consequential when the retrieved evidence is less
straightforward. The graph may contain facts that are individually relevant
but collectively insufficient to support an answer, plausible alternatives such as
French franc connected through a different relation, or highly connected entities
that are structurally salient without supporting the query. In other cases, the
supplied graph may support an answer that conflicts with the model’s paramet-
ric knowledge. The central question is therefore not only whether GraphRAG
answers correctly, but whether it can distinguish supporting evidence from dis-
traction, recognise when the available evidence is insufficient, and follow the sup-
plied context when it conflicts with memory. A single end-to-end score cannot
distinguish these failure modes. Text perturbation studies have exposed related
robustness failures [3, 5, 52], but do not isolate the structural failure modes of
graph evidence.
GRACE addresses this gap through controlled, matched evidence conditions.
For each question, it fixes the question and answer interface while evaluating
the system under five graph states that separate complete support, missing sup-
port, semantic distraction, topological distraction, and context–memory conflict.
Holding the question fixed removes variation in question difficulty from these
comparisons and also exposes a limitation of pre-retrieval, question-only rout-
ing: the same question can be paired with evidence that is sufficient, insufficient,
distracting, or conflicting, none of which can be observed before the retrieved
graph is inspected.
More importantly, these controlled comparisons reveal that relevance is not
sufficiency. In our experiments, relevance-based evidence organisation improves
every state in which the graph still supports an answer, yet reduces accuracy
when the critical support path is severed: residual facts can remain relevant
to the question without jointly forming a complete basis for answering. This
observation motivates two complementary components. QCFSE organises the
complete supplied graph conditional on the question and its fact connections,
while QCFSE-SA additionally estimates whether the graph as a whole provides
sufficient support to answer. The factual context and frozen generator remain
identical in the paired comparison, isolating evidence integration from changes
in retrieval volume or model scale.


Research Questions.
We ask which failure modes the controlled interventions expose (RQ1),
whether question-only routing can distinguish evidence conditions that arise
after retrieval (RQ2), and whether question-conditioned evidence organisation
and explicit sufficiency control improve a frozen generator under fixed factual
context (RQ3).
Contributions.
– GRACE: 4,028 questions and 20,140 matched observations across five graph
states, with a validated construction and evaluation protocol.
– A five-state audit of four graph-retrieval frameworks and two question-only
routing policies.
– QCFSE, which produces question-conditioned organisational guidance
over the complete supplied graph while preserving its factual context, and
QCFSE-SA, which adds calibrated graph-level sufficiency control.
– The empirical finding that relevance and sufficiency are distinct require-
ments.

Related Work
2.1
GraphRAG and KGQA
Retrieval-augmented generation combined learned retrieval with generation and
multi-passage reading [24, 14, 27, 17, 2, 18, 9]. Later systems organised evidence
through corpus graphs, hierarchical summaries, or associative retrieval [7, 12, 41,
13], while KG-oriented methods exposed structured interfaces, retrieved rela-
tion paths, traversed graphs, or selected and scored question-specific subgraphs
[20, 32, 43, 16, 34, 4, 36, 37, 53]. KG-completion work likewise addressed missing
structure [28, 30]. Existing KGQA and knowledge-intensive benchmarks covered
semantic parsing, compositional questions, verification, and shared-corpus re-
trieval [6, 46, 51, 44, 26, 38, 50, 8], but ordinarily associated each question with
only one evidence condition.
2.2
Robustness and Knowledge Conflict
Prior robustness studies separated noise rejection, integration, and counterfac-
tual behaviour, and showed that irrelevant content, its position, or even a small
adversarial injection could change a RAG answer [3, 5, 52, 29, 42, 54]. A com-
plementary literature examined disagreement between parametric memory and
context, including entity substitution, explicit separation of contextual and mem-
orised answers, conflict taxonomies, and unreliable self-assessment of knowledge
boundaries [31, 48, 35, 49, 39]. These studies primarily perturbed passages or in-
ferred conflict behaviour from separate examples.


2.3
Adaptive Retrieval and Controlled Evaluation
Adaptive RAG methods triggered, skipped, or iterated retrieval using generation
confidence, reflection tokens, predicted complexity, self-reported knowledge, rea-
soning steps, or entity popularity [21, 1, 19, 47, 45, 33]; these decisions inherited
known difficulties in confidence calibration, self-evaluation, and selective predic-
tion [11, 22, 23]. Controlled evaluation offered a complementary methodology:
behavioural tests, contrast sets, and counterfactually augmented data isolated
targeted decision factors rather than relying only on aggregate accuracy [40, 10,
25].
2.4
Research Gap and Positioning of GRACE
The preceding lines of work leave three evaluation factors entangled. Standard
KGQA benchmarks vary the question together with its evidence, passage-level
robustness tests do not capture failures caused by graph structure, and adap-
tive retrieval policies often decide from the question or model confidence before
observing the evidence that must support the answer. Consequently, they can-
not cleanly determine whether an error arises from retrieval routing, evidence
organisation, or failure to recognise that the available evidence is insufficient.
GRACE addresses this gap by holding the question, answer options, and
option positions fixed while intervening directly on the supplied graph. For every
question, it evaluates an intact graph alongside states that sever the reasoning
path, add type-compatible or topologically salient distractors, or replace the
supported entity. These matched comparisons isolate missing support, graph-
specific distraction, and context–memory conflict from question difficulty. They
also allow a question-only gate to be evaluated against the graph conditions
realised after routing, revealing when an evidence-dependent decision cannot be
made from the question alone and motivating separate mechanisms for evidence
organisation and sufficiency recognition.
3
The GRACE Benchmark
3.1
Matched Construction and Source Data
GRACE treats the five states of one question as a matched group. For question
q, the natural-language text, the ordered option tuple Oq, and the option letters
remain fixed; only the supplied graph Sq,v and its state-dependent target yq,v
change. This design reduces confounding by question difficulty and makes within-
question prediction changes attributable to the evidence intervention.
Questions and executable SPARQL programs come from LC-QuAD 2.0 [6].1
We execute SELECT queries against Wikidata [46], resolve the answer and sup-
porting path, map identifiers to English labels, and extract a clean subgraph
containing that path and its neighbourhood. As Fig. 1 shows, every retained
source item must yield all five states, one fixed option tuple, and a fully vali-
dated mapping.

Proposed Method
4.1
Overview and Decision Rule
A retrieved graph creates two distinct decisions. Evidence organisation asks
which facts and connections matter for the question; evidence sufficiency asks
whether those facts form a complete basis for answering. A remaining fact can
be relevant after the decisive path has been severed, so relevance alone cannot
justify an answer. QCFSE addresses the first decision by encoding the complete
question–graph pair, and QCFSE-SA adds an explicit sufficiency controller.

plete graph supplied in evidence state v, where ei = (si, ri, oi) is a KG triple.
QCFSE produces organisational guidance gθ(q, Sv) and a graph-support proba-
bility cθ(q, Sv) ∈[0, 1]. With frozen generator G, calibration-selected threshold
τ, and insufficient-information option yins, the complete decision is

4.5
Sufficiency Calibration and Context Preservation
After training and checkpoint selection, τ is selected on a disjoint 300-question
calibration set by maximising controlled-state accuracy over observed support
scores and is frozen before testing. Calibration labels therefore determine only
the decision threshold and do not train the evidence representation. The paired
comparison also preserves factual context: both arms pass the identical serialised
Sv to G, only gθ is appended, and ordered-context hashes are checked for every
pair.
5
Experimental Evaluation
5.1
Metrics and Protocol
We report per-state exact-match accuracy and their equal-weight mean
(controlled-state accuracy); failed or unparsable outputs count as incorrect.
All 95% confidence intervals use 10,000 paired question-clustered bootstrap
resamples, keeping the five states of each question in one cluster. QCFSE
uses 4,250 questions for training, 750 for checkpoint selection, and a disjoint
300-question calibration set for sufficiency-threshold selection. The common
generator is Qwen3.5-35B-A3B (4-bit AWQ, temperature 0, seed 42), and
the frozen encoder is cross-encoder/ms-marco-MiniLM-L6-v2. All paired
Full-KG/QCFSE runs fix the question, options, ordered graph, generator,
prompt, and decoding settings; factual-context hashes match for all 20,140 test
instances.

5.3
Question-Only Answerability Routing
Two DeBERTa-v3-base encoders [15] predict no-context answerability from the
question, using either open-ended answer matching or an option-aligned probe.
Table 4 compares them with always retrieving.
The open-ended gate gains 1.40 points (95% CI [+0.94, +1.89]) while saving
11.59% of calls. Aggressive option-aligned routing saves 68.42% but loses 2.32
points, including 9.66 on swapping. A question-only gate applies the same route
to every evidence state and can therefore suppress context precisely when the
graph must override a strong parametric answer.

5.5
Threats to Validity
About 99% of broken serialisations contain an Unknown entity cue, which the
method may exploit despite not seeing the state name. GRACE is derived from
one KGQA source (LC-QuAD 2.0 over Wikidata), one query form (SELECT),
and one paired generator; incomplete LightRAG and GraphRAG runs preclude a
final framework ranking. Swapping measures consistency with supplied evidence,
not external-world truth. These caveats do not affect the byte-identical-context
QCFSE comparison.
6
Conclusion
GRACE uses matched graph interventions to expose evidence-integration fail-
ures that clean accuracy and question-only routing conceal. QCFSE shows that
question-conditioned organisation improves how a frozen generator uses a fixed
complete graph, while its broken-state regression establishes that relevance is


not sufficiency. QCFSE-SA makes sufficiency explicit, improving the aggregate
result while revealing over-abstention on difficult but answerable graphs as the
next calibration problem.

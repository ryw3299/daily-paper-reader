Autonomous driving is turning to vision-language-action models (VLAs). Most answer and plan by decoding, one step at a time. Each step is another forward pass, so a plan can take over a second of GPU time. Embodied AI often pairs such a slow, reasoning System 2 with a fast System 1 that acts. Human drivers plan at a glance, with System 1. Faster designs usually put System 1 outside the vision-language model (VLM), in a planner without its abilities. Yet before a VLM writes its first token, it has already read the scene into one hidden state. We show that, once trained, this hidden state is enough to drive and to answer questions about what the model sees. SuperGlance reads it to turn a decoder-only VLM into an encoder-style driving policy. Its linear head scores a fixed set of 5,273 candidate trajectories. With the set fixed, rewards are computed offline, so supervised targets can be reward-weighted and reinforcement learning maximizes the exact expected reward. Before and during supervised policy training, multiple-choice training keeps the VLM’s visual abilities in one pass, so the policy still answers questions about driving scenes. Training questions are built against shortcuts. Each must be answered right in two option orders. Against drift, a KL term keeps each stage near its starting model. One pass offers VLAs a more plausible path to System 1. SuperGlance answers visual multiple-choice questions as well as its base VLM does by decoding. It plans in 84 ms of GPU time, six times faster than Qwen-Drive-1.0, and at least as well on NAVSIM, PAI-AV and WOD-E2E. In AlpaSim closed loop, km per at-fault incident holds for both planners up to a 300-ms delay and drops beyond. With GPU time charged as delay, SuperGlance’s lead over Qwen-Drive-1.0 grows from 2.8 to 3.9 times. Delayed by 1.2 s, our model falls below Qwen-Drive-1.0 at its own 518 ms.

1arXiv:2611.19138v2 [cs.RO] 18 Nov 2026 1 Introduction 

Autonomous driving is turning to vision-language-action models (VLAs), vision-language models (VLMs) trained end to end as driving policies [1–5]. They bring broad visual knowledge. Such a policy reads camera frames and the ego state and outputs a trajectory for the next few seconds. A VLM answers by decoding, and most driving VLAs produce this trajectory step by step as well (Fig. 2a,b): they write waypoints as text or action tokens, one per forward pass [2, 6– 8], or denoise it over several steps with a planning expert that reads the VLM’s features [1, 5]. Every step is another forward pass, and the passes add up. On one H200 GPU at batch size one, Qwen-Drive-1.0 [1] spends 518 ms of GPU time on a plan over 11 passes, and 1,213 ms when also writing short reasoning (Fig. 1, left). The car keeps moving while the policy thinks, so the plan arrives late (Sec. 4.3). A human driver plans at a glance, with the fast, automatic thinking known as System 1 [9]. Faster designs mostly move planning out of the VLM into a separate planner or smaller student (Sec. 2), leaving the VLM’s abilities behind. So far, a fast driving VLA that answers questions either plans outside the VLM or regresses one trajectory from query tokens [10]. Yet before a VLM writes its first token, it has already read the scene into one hidden state. We show that, once trained, this hidden state is enough to drive and to answer questions about what the model sees. VLMs became decoder-only to write free text; a plan is not free text. BERT [11] and GPT [12] classify from one hidden state with a small head, as did early vision-language transformers. We bring this classification approach to driving VLAs, with candidate tra-jectories as the classes, so a plan takes one forward pass. We present SuperGlance, which turns a decoder-only VLM into an encoder-style driving policy that plans in one forward pass (Fig. 1, left; Fig. 2c). A linear Policy Head on the last hidden state at the answer position, [ANS] , scores a fixed set of 5,273 candidate trajectories, most of them clus-ter centers of human trajectories (Sec. 3.1). As the set is fixed, every candidate’s reward in every training scene is computed once, offline. Training has three stages (Fig. 3). Stages 1 and 2 train on multiple-choice questions to keep the VLM’s visual abilities in one forward pass and carry them to questions about driving scenes. Stage 3 trains the policy, first toward a supervised target that can weight candi-dates by reward. The supervised policy can still leave much probability on unsafe candidates (Fig. 4b), so reinforcement learning (RL) raises the expected reward, computed exactly over all candidates without sampling, and lifts NAVSIM’s no-collision and time-to-collision scores. The language-model head is kept and question data stays in training, so the final model still answers questions at almost no cost to planning. With no decoding, denoising or separate planner, the VLM itself plans at a glance, as a human driver does.

With each plan’s GPU time charged as delay, it drives 3.9 times as far per at-fault incident as Qwen-Drive-1.0 (Fig. 1). Without delay, it still leads, as does every one-pass variant in our ablations (Sec. 4.3). • SuperGlance plans from one hidden state of a VLM by scoring fixed candidates, so targets can weight them by reward and RL maximizes their exact expected reward. • Training in two option orders keeps the VLM’s visual abilities in one pass and carries them to questions about driving scenes, at almost no cost to planning. • One checkpoint plans in 84 ms, 6.2 times faster than Qwen-Drive-1.0, and at least as well on NAVSIM, WOD-E2E, PAI-AV, AlpaSim and Language2Drive; its visual multiple-choice answers rival its base VLM’s decoded ones. 

## 2 Related Work 

2.1 Vision-language models as driving policies 

Recent driving VLAs differ mainly in how they emit a tra-jectory (Tab. 1). AdaThinkDrive [6], Curious-VLA [7] and DriveTeach-VLA [8] write waypoints as text, AutoVLA [2] decodes action tokens, DriveFine [16] decodes bin tokens by masked diffusion, and WAM-Flow [17] refines numeric to-kens by discrete flow matching. ReCogDrive [5] and Qwen-Drive-1.0 [1] attach a denoising planner or action expert, and world-model policies [18–21] add future-frame predic-tion. Most plan over sequential decoding or denoising steps; SuperGlance reads its plan from one hidden state in a single forward pass (Fig. 2). Fast designs usually move planning out of the VLM: dual systems pair it with a conventional or smaller plan-ner [4, 22, 23] or lightweight action expert [24], and dis-tillation trains a student that skips reasoning [25]. Car-LLaVA [26], Reasoning-VLA [27] and, for robot actions, OpenVLA-OFT [28] regress from query tokens in one pass; SimLingo [10] does so in a language-capable VLM. An-chorVLA [14] refines a decoded trajectory-anchor token with a flow model, ORION [29] a planning token with a generative planner, and FiS-VLA [30] uses a robot VLA’s last blocks as a fast policy that still denoises. Unlike regression and anchor-token policies, it plans by scor-ing candidates from one hidden state, so its target weights each by reward (Sec. 4.4) and RL uses all of their rewards. Many driving VLAs add GRPO-style RL [31] over sampled trajectories [1, 2, 5]. Perception-based planners score a fixed trajectory vocabulary [32–35], per-scene candi-dates [36, 37] or both [38]. ZTRS [15] takes an exact policy gradient over such a vocabulary, and DriveDPO [39] distills imitation and safety scores into a policy aligned by prefer-ence optimization. SuperGlance brings the vocabulary and exact, sampling-free RL into a VLM. 

2.2 Decision making in vision-language models 

Early VLMs such as ViLBERT [40], LXMERT [41] and UNITER [42] followed BERT [11], casting visual ques-tion answering (VQA) as classification over frequent an-swers with a small head per task on a pre-trained encoder. Flamingo [43], BLIP-2 [44] and LLaVA [45] instead write their answers token by token, as do Qwen3-VL [46] and Qwen3.5 [47]. Scoring a fixed answer set takes one pass for any answer length; so does reading option-letter logits for multiple choice [48], though with letter and position bi-ases [49]. SuperGlance brings the classification head back into a generative VLM: its Policy Head scores a fixed set of trajectories as a VQA head scores answers. 

## 3 Method 

SuperGlance plans and answers questions from one hidden state. It reads that state through two heads, each in one forward pass: the language-model head answers multiple-choice questions and a linear Policy Head scores a fixed set of candidate trajectories (Fig. 2). Two training stages on multiple-choice questions keep the VLM’s visual abilities in one forward pass, and a third trains the policy. 

3.1 Architecture 

Inputs. Our VLM, Qwen3.5-9B [47], receives the current front-camera frame at 1024 ×576 and, if available, the three frames taken 1.5, 1.0 and 0.5 s earlier at 544 ×320: 576 visual tokens for the current frame and 170 per past frame. The prompt gives the ego speed, acceleration, past 1.5 s of positions, navigation command and any instruction, and ends at the answer position, [ANS] . No detector or map output enters the prompt; the scene comes from the frames alone. 

Candidates. Each candidate is a 4-s trajectory of 8 poses (position and heading) at 2 Hz in the ego frame. Of the 

K = 5,273 candidates, 4,096 are k-means centers of human trajectories in the 92,339 NAVSIM training scenes, and one is stationary. NAVSIM rarely reaches 13 m/s, so 1,024 more are k-means centers of PAI-AV driving at that speed or faster. Clustering misses stops and curves that are rare in human driving, so 152 more are built by rule for RL (appendix). The best clustered candidate per scene reaches a mean PDM score (PDMS) of 99.84 on 10,549 held-out NAVSIM training scenes and a mean rater feedback score (RFS) of 9.55 on the rated WOD-E2E validation frames.

3.2 Data 

QA data. Stage 1 uses 1.16M questions from text and vi-sual [52–57] datasets and stage 2 uses 124,378 driving ques-tions from DriveLM-nuScenes [3], all recast as multiple choice with one gold option letter (Fig. 3; appendix). 

Rewards. Because the candidates are fixed, every candi-date’s reward r(x, k ) in every training scene x is computed once before training. On NAVSIM [50], it is the PDMS times the driving-direction compliance (DDC), both in [0 , 1] .A candidate that collides, leaves the drivable area or vio-lates the time-to-collision margin counts as unsafe, so it loses a further 10 and ranks below every safe candidate. On PAI-AV [58], it is −0.2 times the average displacement er-ror (ADE, in meters) to the logged future, and on the rated WOD-E2E frames [59] it is the RFS divided by 10. 

Rollout rows. In closed loop, a policy drifts into states that logged driving never shows. To learn to recover, stage 3 also trains on 46,583 frames, 2.1% of its rows (examples), rendered along AlpaSim rollouts of earlier models on 492 scenes outside the evaluation scenarios. Their target trajec-tory rejoins the human path in about 3 s, never ahead of the human, and slows behind a lead vehicle. 

3.3 Training 

Each stage trains rank-32 LoRA adapters [60] on the lan-guage model, merged before the next, and a KL term keeps each stage near its starting model, so that later stages keep what earlier ones learned. 

Stage 1: general QA. Read in one pass, a VLM’s option letter, like an LLM’s [49], is biased toward some letters and positions (Fig. 5c), so stage 1 trains it to follow the content. Each question with gold letter y has a twin with shifted options and gold y′. One letter is drawn from each order’s distribution, p and p′, and the pair hits only if both are gold, so an order-dependent answer earns no credit (Fig. 5). The reward is one minus the Brier score [61] of the pair distribution p ⊗ p′ against the gold pair, and 32 drawn pairs estimate its gradient without bias (appendix).

On language, Qwen3.5-9B’s written replies lead our letter by up to 17.08 points when reasoning first; when answering first, they stay within 1.4 points of its letter (appendix). 

Planning and answering. Question data keeps the VLM’s world knowledge and costs planning almost nothing (ap-pendix Tab. 19): without it, the model is level on navtest and AlpaSim and 0.01 m lower in PAI-AV’s 3-s ADE, but falls below Qwen3.5-9B’s letter on all eleven benchmarks. Skipping only stages 1 and 2 leaves planning level and costs DriveLM and 3DSRBench 3.20 and 4.36 points, a tenth and a third of the loss without question data.

## 5 Conclusion 

We find that before a VLM writes its first token, it has already read the scene into one hidden state, which, once trained, is enough to drive and to answer questions about what the model sees. SuperGlance reads it to turn a decoder-only VLM into an encoder-style driving policy whose fixed can-didates let targets weight plans by reward, RL maximize the exact expected reward and instructions pick among them. Questions built against shortcuts, each answered in both op-tion orders, keep the VLM’s visual abilities in one pass. One pass also reacts faster, and time is distance: with each plan’s GPU time as delay, our lead in km per at-fault incident over Qwen-Drive-1.0 grows from 2.8 to 3.9 times. One model answers questions on eleven benchmarks and plans in 84 ms of GPU time: a path to System 1 in the VLM.

## A Training data 

This section lists the training data of the three stages: the multiple-choice questions of stages 1 and 2 (Tab. 9) and the rows of stage 3 (Tabs. 10 to 12), with how each source is built and what is kept out of training. 

Answer readout. Every question of the first two stages is a multiple-choice question with options labelled A, B and so on. The prompt lists the options and ends with ‘Answer with only the letter of the correct option.’ The answer distribution is the softmax over the logits of the option letters predicted at [ANS] , the last prompt position, so one forward pass gives the whole distribution; yes/no questions are two-option questions. Figure 8 shows questions of both stages as the model reads them, and Fig. 3 a stage-3 row. 

Question pools. Table 9 lists the sources of stages 1 and 2; stage 2 converts only DriveLM’s closed-form questions. The sources carry answer cues: SAT lists the gold first in all 139,632 of its questions, and 16,548 of its 19,101 counting questions (87%) are answered 1; the option shuffling and the tempered answer sampling below counter both. Count options are five consecutive integers that start zero to four below the gold, never below zero, so the gold is not always the middle value. Chart values are spaced by the larger of the gold’s precision and 15% of the gold, and a jittered box overlaps the true one at IoU 0.3–0.8. The extra option, an answer that rejects every listed option, goes to text questions with three or more options, except WANLI’s, whose three la-bels cover every case. Only single-image questions are kept. Options are shuffled once with a fixed seed, so questions whose options name other options, such as ‘all of the above’, are dropped. Within a source, a draw picks a question type with probability proportional to n1/2 and then an answer with probability proportional to n3/4, where n is the number of questions of that type or with that answer. Images with identical bytes or the same Visual Genome or COCO image never cross the training and development splits. The devel-opment splits hold 45,826 questions of SAT, GQA, TallyQA, AI2D, ChartQA and TextVQA and 150 DriveLM questions of each kind; DriveLM is split by scene into 558 training, 69 development and 69 test scenes. 

Decontamination. We drop 1,688 duplicate questions, 88 questions with more than one correct option, and 8,171 questions on an image of SAT’s evaluation splits [53], BLINK [65], CV-Bench [64] or 3DSRBench [66], matched by identical bytes or the same Visual Genome or COCO image. Images added for OpenImages, SpatialSense and DriveLM questions are also dropped within Hamming dis-tance 10 of an evaluation image under a 63-bit DCT hash, among them 175 DriveLM images of the question kinds and 150 of the box and relation kinds. Text questions that share at least 50% of their 10-word shingles (runs of 10 consecutive words) with the questions of the language benchmarks of Tab. 2 or ARC-Easy [69] are dropped as well. 

Driving pools. Table 10 lists the rows of each phase of stage 3, Tab. 11 their prompt and Tab. 12 how each source becomes a row. No training pool holds a frame or clip of any evaluation set; the last row of Tab. 12 lists what each source leaves out. Rollout rows teach recovery: their frames follow AlpaSim rollouts of earlier models in 492 NuRec scenes, 327 from PAI-AV’s training split and 165 from its validation split, none among the 916 AlpaSim scenarios. The recovery trajectory of a rollout row returns to the human path in about 3 s, never ahead of the human, and slows behind a lead vehicle. Each instruction scene gives three rows on the same frame: one without an instruction, one with an instruction that the logged future follows and one with an instruction that it does not follow. Each row’s target is q ∝ exp( −d/t ),with t = 1 m on NAVSIM and WOD-E2E and 0.5 m on PAI-AV. Without an instruction, it spreads over all candidates with d the ADE; with an instruction that the logged future follows, over the candidates that follow it; with one that it does not follow, over the feasible candidates that follow it (on NAVSIM, no collision, inside the drivable area and lane changes only into a lane of the same direction; on PAI-AV, no overlap with an obstacle box from 0.5 to 4 s; on every source, acceleration at most 3.5 m/s 2, also from the current speed, and yaw rate at most 0.8 rad/s), with d the distance to the logged future in the direction the instruction leaves free: along the path for lane and turn instructions, across it for all other kinds. Instructions come from 8 templates for each of 12 kinds, and the last two of each kind are used only in development. To keep Language2Drive’s wordings out of training, we wrote the templates ourselves: none comes within a string similarity of 0.65 of any of Language2Drive’s 904 instruction strings, and no instruction row comes from a Language2Drive log. A development set of 1,000 rows, 200 each from held-out NAVSIM logs, rollout rows, WOD-E2E, held-out PAI-AV training clips and instruction rows, tracks the loss every 1,000 steps; we keep the last checkpoint. Of its rows, 65 come from 40 of the 916 AlpaSim scenarios.

Delay. The simulator calls the policy every 100 ms of sim-ulated time and stops its clock while the policy computes; the policy returns its full plan. Following streaming per-ception [103], we charge each model’s computation time as delay. ## G Limitations 

SuperGlance drives only weighted means of its 5,273 candi-dates, so a manoeuvre rare in human driving must be built by rule and trained again: no clustered candidate brakes to a stop from 9 m/s or faster (Sec. C). In AlpaSim without delay, 10 of the final model’s 53 at-fault collisions are with a car stopped in or partly in its lane, though a candidate that stops or goes around exists in each. Decoded, the fi-nal model trails Qwen3.5-9B’s replies with thinking off on five of the seven language benchmarks in Tab. 18, all but HellaSwag and BoolQ. 22

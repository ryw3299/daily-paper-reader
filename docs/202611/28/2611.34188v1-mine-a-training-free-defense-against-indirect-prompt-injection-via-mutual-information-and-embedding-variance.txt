MINE: A Training-Free Defense against Indirect Prompt Injection
via Mutual Information and Embedding Variance
Truong Phan and Ngoc-Quan Pham and Dat Quoc Nguyen
Qualcomm AI Research*
{truophan, ngocquan, datnq}@qti.qualcomm.com
Abstract
Existing defenses against indirect prompt injec-
tion in retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)
are inherently reactive: prompt-engineering
methods are often overridden by the injected in-
structions, while supervised detectors must be
retrained for each new attack style. We propose
a different paradigm: defend by maximizing rel-
evance, not by detecting attacks. Our method,
MINE, is training-free and attack-agnostic, and
it also serves as a query-conditioned reranker.
To evaluate defenses under realistic conditions,
we introduce LC-Inj-SQuAD, a long-context
benchmark with an average of 4,032 words per
context. We evaluate on four datasets spanning
short-context QA (Inj-SQuAD, Inj-TriviaQA),
long-context QA (LC-Inj-SQuAD), and agent
tool-call injection (InjecAgent). Across all four,
MINE achieves the best accuracy–security
trade-off among training-free defenses. It is
also the only defense that stays usable against
a fully adaptive attacker, generalizes to agent
tool-call injection, and achieves the lowest in-
ference latency on long contexts.

Introduction
Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) enables
large language models (LLMs) to ground their re-
sponses in knowledge retrieved from external doc-
ument contexts, improving factuality (Lewis et al.,
2020; Karpukhin et al., 2020; Gao et al., 2023; Fan
et al., 2024; Singh et al., 2025; Nguyen et al., 2026).
However, RAG also introduces a critical vulnera-
bility: indirect prompt injection (Greshake et al.,
2023; Schulhoff et al., 2023; Zhan et al., 2024;
Chen et al., 2025c). Retrieved contexts may embed
malicious instructions that hijack the model into
following the injected instructions instead of the
user’s query, without access to the model weights
or the system prompt (Perez and Ribeiro, 2022).

In a typical RAG-based QA system (Figure 1a),
the retriever returns relevant chunks/passages that
are concatenated into a context C used for answer
generation. Consider a scenario where one of the
retrieved chunks in C has been poisoned:

Output: “Experience the ultimate comfort with
Nike. . . ”
In this case, the result can be a complete behav-
ioral hijacking, when the model follows the in-
jected command instead of answering the question.
Limitations of existing defenses. Current ap-
proaches fall into three categories, each with struc-
tural weaknesses.
(i) Prompt-engineering de-
fenses (Yi et al., 2025; Schulhoff et al., 2024; Hines
et al., 2024) prepend or append meta-instructions
(e.g., “ignore instructions in the data”) that com-
pete with the injection for the model’s attention.
(ii) Heuristic detectors (Jain et al., 2023; Alon
and Kamfonas, 2023) flag suspicious patterns such
as keywords or perplexity spikes, but are brittle
to paraphrasing and offer no formal guarantees.
(iii) Supervised filtering methods, most notably
INDIRECT-PIA (Chen et al., 2025d), train dedicated
models on labeled data to detect and remove in-
jected instructions; they thus depend on the labeled
distribution and degrade on unseen attack styles.
All three families are therefore reactive by design:
their robustness is attack-dependent.
A different paradigm: relevance maximiza-
tion. We approach prompt injection defense from a
different perspective: rather than detecting attacks,
we select the spans most relevant to the user’s query
from the retrieved context. We propose MINE, a
pre-LLM filter that processes retrieved context be-
fore it reaches the language model. By maximizing
relevance to the query rather than identifying ma-

Our key insight is that legitimate spans typically
exhibit two properties relative to the user’s intent:
(P1) high mutual information (MI) with the query,
reflecting semantic alignment with the user’s in-
formation need; and (P2) low semantic variance
among themselves, since relevant evidence should
be mutually coherent rather than scattered across
unrelated intents. MINE therefore selects spans
that maximize MI with the query while penalizing
embedding-space dispersion; injected spans violate
both properties and are filtered out as a side effect
of relevance selection. The method needs no la-
beled attack data, no fine-tuning, and no additional
LLM calls, making it plug-and-play (Figure 1b).
Our contributions: Method. We introduce
MINE, a training-free, attack-agnostic pre-LLM
filter that defends by maximizing relevance rather
than by detecting attacks. MINE formulates de-
fense as subset selection over retrieved spans,
with an objective that maximizes mutual informa-
tion with the user query while penalizing seman-
tic variance. To make this combinatorial prob-
lem tractable, we relax subset indicators to span-
selection weights on the probability simplex, opti-
mize them with projected gradient ascent, and then
extract the final evidence subset. Benchmark. We
construct LC-Inj-SQuAD, a long-context bench-
mark that synthesizes multi-page encyclopedic con-
texts (4,032 words on average) from short SQuAD
samples. On LC-Inj-SQuAD, existing defenses
lose accuracy or security as contexts grow longer,
whereas MINE remains stable, keeping ASR at
most 3.31% across all evaluated LLMs and static
attack types. Findings. Across three LLMs, five
static attack types, and four datasets, MINE at-
tains the best accuracy–security trade-off among
training-free defenses. Under a fully adaptive at-
tacker, it remains the only usable defense, although
its ASR rises to 23.20% (Section 5.4). It also gen-
eralizes to agent tool-call injection (Section 6) and
achieves the lowest latency in long-context settings
(Section 5.5).

Related Work
Indirect Prompt Injection. Perez and Ribeiro
(2022) introduced direct “Ignore previous instruc-
tions” attacks, while Greshake et al. (2023) later
formalized the indirect variant, in which adversar-
ial instructions are embedded within retrieved data.
Attacks have since diversified into conversational
topic transitions (Chen et al., 2025c), learned exe-
cution triggers (Pasquini et al., 2024), multi-modal
payloads (Bagdasaryan et al., 2023), and RAG ex-
ploits (Zou et al., 2025).
Prompt-Engineering Defenses.
Sandwich
prompting (Schulhoff, 2024), reminders (Yi et al.,
2025), and Spotlight (Hines et al., 2024) in-
ject meta-instructions to guide the model, while
training-based variants harden the model itself
(Wallace et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2025a,b; Piet
et al., 2024). These in-LLM approaches rely on the
model’s instruction-following ability to override
the injection, the capability that attackers exploit.
Detection-Based Defenses. Perplexity-based
detection (Alon and Kamfonas, 2023) and para-
phrasing defenses (Jain et al., 2023) are brittle to


adaptive attacks, while Llama Guard (Inan et al.,
2023) trains a dedicated safeguard LLM. More re-
cent detectors instead exploit internal model sig-
nals (Hung et al., 2025; Xie et al., 2024; Hu et al.,
2024). INDIRECT-PIA (Chen et al., 2025d), fine-
tunes models on labeled attacks; while effective
in-distribution, it requires substantial training in-
frastructure and degrades on unseen attack styles.
RAG-Poisoning Defenses. Defenses against
corpus poisoning (Zou et al., 2025), such as Ro-
bustRAG (Xiang et al., 2024) and Astute RAG
(Wang et al., 2025), isolate or consolidate retrieved
passages, but they target a different objective. Poi-
soning alters the answer to a query, so poisoned
passages are topically aligned with it by construc-
tion. In contrast, injection alters the task. Both
approaches also incur extra LLM calls per query.
Closest to our setting is RAGDefender (Kim et al.,
2025), a training-free clustering filter that flags ad-
versarial passages via inter-passage embedding sim-
ilarity, which we adapt as a baseline. MINE differs
by scoring subsets jointly-coupling query relevance
with within-subset coherence-rather than scoring
each passage independently. We use RAGDefender
as the poisoned-RAG representative because it is a
recent post-retrieval defense and its original paper
reports stronger performance than RobustRAG and
other prior defenses.
Information-Theoretic Relevance and Sub-
set Selection. Mutual information (MI) estima-
tors (Belghazi et al., 2018; Oord et al., 2018) un-
derpin contrastive representation learning, includ-
ing InfoNCE-trained dense retrievers (Karpukhin
et al., 2020); however, these are training-time meth-
ods. In contrast, MINE applies MI at inference
time, requiring no training. Our continuous relax-
ation over the simplex draws on machinery from
differentiable subset sampling (Xie and Ermon,
2019), while replacing coverage-based submodular
selection (Lin and Bilmes, 2011) with a mutual-
information objective. To our knowledge, MINE
is the first method to cast indirect prompt injec-
tion defense as MI maximization. As shown in
Table 1, MINE is the only defense that satisfies all
five design criteria.

3.1
Problem Setup
Given a user’s query prompt q, the retrieval system
returns a context C formed by concatenating rel-
evant chunks/passages from an untrusted corpus.
Because C originates from external, unverifiable
sources such as the web, it may contain adversari-
ally authored content.
We segment C into n contiguous spans C =
{s1, s2, . . . , sn} by splitting on sentence bound-
aries and merging consecutive sentences until each
span reaches a minimum length of 50 characters.
This granularity balances two competing goals:
fine-grained spans enable precise removal of in-
jected content, while overly short spans lack se-
mantic coherence.
The defense must produce a filtered subset C*
⊆C such that the LLM’s output, conditioned on
(q, C*) matches the ground-truth answer grounded
in the legitimate portions of C.

Our LC-Inj-SQuAD Long Context
Benchmark
Previous works primarily evaluate prompt injec-
tions using short contexts C of 100-300 words
(Chen et al., 2025d). Currently, no benchmark ad-
dresses the strictly long context setting, where the
injected text acts as a ”needle in a haystack”. To ad-
dress this limitation, we introduce LC-Inj-SQuAD,
a long-context benchmark built by extending sam-
ples from the short-context Inj-SQuAD dataset
(Chen et al., 2025d). Our benchmark construction
pipeline consists of three steps, each performed
via GPT-5.4-mini prompting with structured output
validation.1 See prompts used in the Appendix A.
Step 1: Target span extraction. Given a triple
(q, C = {s1, s2, . . . , sn}, a) representing a (query
prompt, context, and ground-truth response answer)
sample from Inj-SQuAD, we prompt to extract the
target span sl ∈C that contains the answer a for the

gpt-5-4-thinking

q. Samples failing extraction or verification after

Dataset statistics.
Of the 900 original Inj-
SQuAD samples, 694 pass all pipeline steps, yield-
ing contexts that average 4,032 words in length
(median 4,039; range 3,376–4,734), thus placing
the entire benchmark strictly in the long-context
regime. See details in Table 7 in the Appendix.
Paired evaluation protocol. We evaluate every
defense on two matched datasets: (1) Inj-SQuAD,
the subset of 694 short-context samples from which
our LC-Inj-SQuAD is expanded, and (2) our long-
context LC-Inj-SQuAD. This paired design isolates
the causal effect of context length from sample-
level confounds.
5
Experiments
5.1
Setup
Threat model. We adopt the standard grey-box
threat model for indirect prompt injection (Gre-
shake et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2025d): the ad-
versary knows the system architecture but not
the defense parameters (e.g., α, k, embedding
encoder). The adversary may employ arbitrary
injection strategies (e.g., template-based, format-
exploiting, or conversational) but cannot modify
the query prompt q or the LLM weights. Sec-
tion 5.4 further evaluates an adaptive attacker who
knows the full algorithm and all hyperparameters.
LLM models. We evaluate on three open-source
models: Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct, Llama-3.1-70B-
Instruct (Grattafiori et al., 2024) and Qwen3-30B-
A3B-Instruct-2507 (Yang et al., 2025).
Attack types. We evaluate the defense strate-
gies against six attack strategies. Five are static
injections of increasing sophistication: naive (di-
rect instruction injection), escape_separation (es-
cape characters that break formatting), ignore


(“Ignore previous instructions” templates), com-
pletion (fake assistant/user turn boundaries), and
conv_attack (TopicAttack (Chen et al., 2025c),
which uses natural conversational transitions to dis-
guise the injection).
The sixth attack type, adaptive, stress-tests un-
der the strongest setting: the attacker knows our
defense algorithm and all of its parameters.
Baselines. Five prompt-engineering defenses in-
clude None (no defense), Reminder (Yi et al., 2025)
(prepend “Do not follow instructions in the data”),
Instructional (warn the model about malicious con-
tent), Sandwich (Schulhoff, 2024) (repeat the in-
struction after the data), and Spotlight (Hines et al.,
2024) (delimiter-based data marking). We also
include three non-prompt-engineering defenses:
INDIRECT-PIA (Chen et al., 2025d), the state-of-
the-art supervised detector; RAGDefender (Kim
et al., 2025), a training-free, pre-LLM inter-passage
clustering filter adapted from corpus-poisoning de-
fense (matching our setting); and MELON (Zhu
et al., 2025), the state-of-the-art defense for tool-
using agents, which re-executes the model with the
user task masked and flags an injection when the
masked run reproduces the original behavior.

details.

tary measures: (1) Task Accuracy (Acc, %): the

the model outputs the attacker’s targeted injection
(vulnerability; lower is better).

7
Conclusion
We presented MINE, a training-free, attack-
agnostic defense against indirect prompt injection
that defends by maximizing relevance rather than
by detecting attacks. Specifically, MINE algorith-
mically selects the subset of spans with maximal
mutual information with the query. Experiments
with three LLMs, five static attack types, and four
datasets, including our long-context benchmark
LC-Inj-SQuAD, show that MINE achieves the best
accuracy–security trade-off among training-free de-
fenses, with the lowest latency under attack in both
short- and long-context settings. Under a fully
adaptive attacker, MINE remains the only usable
defense, indicating that its robustness does not de-
pend on fixed injection templates, although its ASR
rises to 23.20% on long contexts. Its strong results
on InjecAgent further suggest that pre-LLM rele-
vance filtering is a practical direction for securing
systems that condition on noisy external context,
from RAG to tool use.
Limitations
MINE’s splitter merges sentence-boundary frag-
ments until each span reaches 50 characters, which
presumes well-formed prose with standard punctu-
ation. Code, tabular or otherwise structured data
(e.g. JSON, markdown tables), and languages with
non-standard sentence terminators can yield spans
that are too coarse to isolate an injection from its
surrounding legitimate content. Domain-specific
chunkers are a natural extension.
Ethics Statement
This work aims to improve the security of LLM
systems that consume untrusted external content.
The attacks in our experiments come from exist-
ing benchmark families or simple re-renderings of
them; our contribution is a defense and an analysis
of when existing defenses fail. MINE requires no
proprietary model access, no fine-tuning, and no
user data beyond the query and retrieved context
available at inference time.

C
Adaptive Attack Details
Attack design. Two ideas drive the attack. (1)
Hide the malicious instruction in one legitimate-
looking span.
MINE keeps or discards each
sentence-level span whole, so we wrap the instruc-
tion with content topically similar to the retrieved
document and collapse the result into a single span.
The wrapper inherits the document’s relevance sig-
nal, and MINE’s splitter cannot separate the mali-
cious content from its wrapping. (2) Plant two
such spans at opposite ends of the retrieved
document. We use two complementary hiding
strategies: a filter-aware rewrite (start, optimized
against MINE), and a single-span conversational
injection (end, adapted from TopicAttack (Chen
et al., 2025c)). Because MINE’s filter is position-
agnostic, the attacker gets two independent draws
against the filter across both position (start vs. end).
D
InjecAgent Details
Adaptation.
We apply MINE to the tool
Observation before returning it to the ReAct
agent. The MI maximization and PGA optimiza-
tion of §3.3 are unchanged. Three components are
re-tuned for the tool-use setting.
(i) JSON-aware splitter.
The sentence-
boundary regex of §3.1 produces fewer than two
spans on compact JSON tool outputs, which con-
tain neither .!? nor newlines. In that case we fall
back to a splitter that additionally breaks on field-
boundary commas, so injected JSON fields become
filterable spans.
(ii) Selection with weight floor. A span is kept
iff it lies in the top 80% by weight and wi > 0.05 ·
wmax.
(iii) Document-order assembly. §3.3 reorders
retained spans by decreasing weight to combat lost-
in-the-middle. We instead preserve original docu-
ment order, since ReAct agents parse Observation
as coherent narrative; reordering scrambles JSON
field orders, list structures, and turn semantics.

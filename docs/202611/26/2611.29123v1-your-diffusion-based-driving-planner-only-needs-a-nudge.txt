## Abstract 

Diffusion-based planners capture the multimodality of hu-man driving behavior well, yet covariate shift in closed-loop driving can lead them to generate trajectories that result in collisions or road departures. Reinforcement learning (RL) finetuning offers a promising way to tackle this issue, but ap-plying it to diffusion-based planners is complicated by their iterative denoising process and receding-horizon execution. We find that small adjustments to the planned trajectories

## 1. Introduction 

Trajectory planning in autonomous driving requires antici-pating how other vehicles will move and deciding when to proceed or yield [39]. Human demonstrations of these inter-actions are often multimodal [60, 61]. A planner trained to predict a single trajectory with a mean squared error loss can average those maneuvers into a plan that follows neither [11]. Diffusion-based planners can represent these distinct maneu-vers by learning a distribution over trajectories conditioned on the traffic scene [28, 60]. They perform well in simula-tion [49, 60] and in real-world driving [61]. Despite their success, diffusion-based planners can still collide with other vehicles or leave the road in closed-loop driving [42, 60]. One contributing factor is covariate shift: a planner trained on logged demonstrations can encounter unfamiliar states as its own errors accumulate [40]. Rein-forcement learning (RL) post-training can help address this mismatch by allowing the planner to learn from the states it visits in closed-loop simulation, using rewards that reflect the consequences of its driven trajectories [12, 50, 55, 57]. However, applying RL to diffusion-based planners raises two difficulties. The first difficulty comes from iterative denoising: a plan comes from several denoising steps, so its likelihood is intractable. Policy-based methods therefore treat each denoising step as an action [32, 38], which mul-tiplies the horizon of the decision process by the number of denoising steps [5, 37, 38]. Value-based methods update policies by backpropagating the critic’s gradient through all denoising steps, which is costly and can destabilize training [21, 36, 54]. The second difficulty arises from receding-horizon execution. To remain responsive to changing traffic, a driving planner may generate a trajectory spanning several seconds but execute only a single control interval before replanning [22]. When the full trajectory is treated as the RL action, the environment provides feedback on the exe-cuted portion while the rest of the plan is superseded. This mismatch complicates value learning [5, 38]. The first difficulty can be addressed by casting iterative denoising as a Markov decision process, which enables pol-icy gradient training at the cost of a longer RL horizon [4, 38]. ReCogDrive [27] and DiffusionDriveV2 [62] use this for-mulation to finetune their planners on open-loop scores of plans sampled in logged states (Fig. 1a). PlannerRFT [26] addresses the resulting state distribution gap by training on states visited during closed-loop rollouts. Its exploration policy learns from rollout rewards, while its diffusion-based planner learns from open-loop plan scores (Fig. 1b). Scoring whole plans in open loop avoids the second difficulty, at the cost of a gap to the closed-loop score of the driven trajec-tory, since the tracker follows only a prefix of each plan [13]. It remains open whether RL can improve diffusion-based planners from closed-loop scores efficiently. All these methods finetune the diffusion-based planner itself, which is where both difficulties arise. This raises a natural question: can we post-train diffusion-based planners without finetuning them? As a first test, we keep the planner fixed and add small random adjustments to the speed and curvature of its plans (Sec. A). On nuPlan [22], at least one of 20 closed-loop trials avoids the failure in more than half of the scenarios that a pretrained Diffusion Planner [60] fails, while sampling the planner again avoids almost none. Based on this observation, we propose PlanNudge (Fig. 1c), which keeps the diffusion-based planner frozen and learns the adjustment with a nudge policy. At each planning step, the nudge policy outputs a two-dimensional nudge, a speed offset and a curvature offset, to adjust the plan. This design addresses both difficulties. For iterative de-noising, the diffusion-based planner is frozen and the nudge is generated in one forward pass, so the RL agent makes one decision per plan, whatever the number of denoising steps. For receding-horizon execution, the action space is small and both action dimensions change the executed portion of the plan. The critic assigns credit to two numbers that matter instead of to a whole plan of which only the beginning is driven. Freezing the planner also preserves its pretrained multimodal distribution over maneuvers, and a zero nudge recovers the sampled plan exactly. We train the nudge policy on closed-loop returns with off-policy RL. We evaluate PlanNudge on nuPlan [22], a closed-loop planning benchmark built on large-scale real driving logs. Compared with the state-of-the-art RL finetuning method PlannerRFT [26], starting from the same pretrained Dif-fusion Planner [60], PlanNudge matches or exceeds its re-ported scores in all six settings with 6.2 M environment steps, about one sixth of PlannerRFT’s reported 40 M, and with less than one fifth of its model compute, the floating-point operations of all network passes during RL training (Sec. I). We also demonstrate the effectiveness of PlanNudge on Hy-per Diffusion Planner (HDP) [61] with both reactive and non-reactive training traffic modes. Our results are means and standard deviations over six training runs. Our main contributions are: • We propose PlanNudge, which freezes the diffusion-based 2planner and trains a nudge policy with off-policy RL in closed loop. The nudge policy generates its action in a single forward pass, without iterative denoising, so the RL horizon stays short. The action has two dimensions, and both change the executed portion of the plan, which sim-plifies value learning under receding-horizon execution. • We demonstrate on nuPlan that PlanNudge matches or exceeds the previous state-of-the-art RL finetuning method in all six settings, with about one sixth of its environment steps and less than one fifth of its model compute. • We provide several guidelines for post-training au-tonomous driving planners with off-policy RL, covering reward shaping, value learning, and exploration. 

## 2. Related work 

Diffusion-based driving planners. Diffusion models [18] and flow matching [30] are widely used to generate robot actions [9] and vehicle trajectories [60]. Diffusion Plan-ner [60] generates the ego plan together with the future trajectories of the surrounding traffic participants, and Flow Planner [49] uses flow matching to model interactive be-havior. Both achieve strong closed-loop results on nuPlan. DiffusionDrive [28] denoises from anchor trajectories with a truncated schedule for end-to-end driving, and Hyper Dif-fusion Planner [61] scales diffusion-based planning to large real-world datasets and real-vehicle tests. These planners learn from logged demonstrations by imitation, which brings the known limitations of behavior cloning [10]. The imita-tion loss carries no notion of driving quality, so a plan that nearly hits another vehicle costs no more than one that keeps clear. The planner is trained only in logged states, so its own errors lead it into states that the logs never cover, where the errors compound (covariate shift) [40]. It can also rely on spurious cues in the scene instead of the causes of the demonstrated behavior (causal confusion) [14, 47]. Plans that look right in open loop therefore still end in collisions or off the road in closed loop [13, 42, 60]. Data augmenta-tion and auxiliary losses mitigate these limitations [7], but they cannot cover the states that the planner itself produces, which are only visited by closed-loop training. 

RL post-training for diffusion-based driving planners. 

RL post-training supplies the driving objective that imitation lacks: the planner is rewarded for the quality of its plans instead of their closeness to the log [19, 26, 27, 62]. Iterative denoising makes the likelihood of a plan intractable, so RL methods for diffusion models treat the denoising process as a Markov decision process [4, 32, 38], backpropagate a critic’s gradient through the denoising steps [54], or weight the de-noising objective by a reward [61]. Most driving methods apply them in logged states: they sample several plans per state and move the planner toward the plans that a learned reward model [19], human feedback [25], or rule-based tra-jectory scores [27, 31, 61] rank highest. Two gaps remain: the planner still never visits the states that its own driving produces (the state gap), and the score of a plan is not the outcome of executing its beginning and replanning (the re-ward gap). PlannerRFT [26] closes the state gap: an explo-ration policy trained in closed loop with PPO [41] guides the sampling, so the planner is updated in the states of its own rollouts. Its reward remains the open-loop score of a sampled plan, its planner is updated with policy gradient [4] and group-relative advantage [43], and its two policies are trained on-policy, which takes 40 M environment steps in its reported setup. PlanNudge closes both gaps with one policy: the reward is the closed-loop return of the driven trajectory, and a nudge policy learns from it with off-policy RL while the planner stays frozen, so RL makes one decision per planning step, not per denoising step. 

Learning residual policies. Residual policy learning im-proves a fixed controller with a learned correction [20, 44]. Recent work trains such a residual on top of a pretrained robot policy with on-policy [1] or off-policy [2, 34, 53, 56] RL. A small residual policy brings large gains in manipula-tion. Motivated by the effect of small random adjustments (Sec. A), PlanNudge applies it to closed-loop trajectory plan-ning for the first time, to our knowledge. This application raises two challenges. First, a plan is a long sequence of waypoints, which is a large action space for a residual. Plan-Nudge therefore nudges only the speed and curvature of the plan, a two-dimensional action that changes the executed por-tion of the plan. Second, the downstream tracker filters out small per-step changes of the plan, so exploration uses tem-porally correlated noise and scripted speed events to reach the maneuvers that avoid failures. As the score of a drive arrives only at the end of an episode, PlanNudge also learns values with multi-step returns. Without multi-step returns or correlated noise, much of the gain is lost (Sec. 5.4). In addition, a dense safety penalty shapes the reward.

We shape the reward by adding a small dense safety penalty to the terminal score of the nuPlan metrics. For explo-ration, temporally correlated Ornstein–Uhlenbeck noise [29] is added to the nudge during data collection, so that the noise persists long enough to change the vehicle’s motion through the tracker. Additionally, scripted maneuvers, a brake or a push held for seconds, can be applied during exploration to supply the maneuvers that avoid driving failures. We call them speed events. For example, a push can be applied when the vehicle stays stopped although it should proceed, also known as the inertia problem [10].

## 6. Conclusion 

We show that learning to nudge the plans of a pretrained diffusion-based planner is an effective alternative to fine-tuning it. Our proposed PlanNudge freezes the planner and trains a nudge policy on closed-loop scores. The policy generates a two-dimensional action in a single forward pass without iterative denoising, so the RL horizon stays short. Both dimensions change the executed portion of the plan, which simplifies value learning under receding-horizon ex-ecution. In all six nuPlan settings, PlanNudge matches or exceeds the final scores of the previous state-of-the-art RL finetuning method, with about one sixth of its environment steps and less than one fifth of its model compute. ## A. Failure analysis 

This section presents the failure analysis that motivates Plan-Nudge (Sec. 1). Unless otherwise stated, results are obtained using the frozen Diffusion Planner [60] on the 1,118 Val14 NR scenarios in our simulator. We classify a scenario as a failure if its closed-loop score is below 50. 

Few scenarios lie near the failure threshold. We exam-ine the pretrained planner’s score distribution across all six evaluation settings. Figure 9 shows that few scenarios have scores near the threshold of 50. Most failures receive a score of 0 because at least one of the score’s four gating terms is zero. At this threshold, the planner fails in 72 Val14 NR scenarios in our simulator: 38 due to at-fault collisions, 16 due to drivable-area violations, and the remaining 18 due to low scores for other reasons. 

Random nudges reveal opportunities to avoid failures. 

We perturb each plan using the action space of PlanNudge (Sec. 4.1). The speed offset is bounded by 1 m/s, and the curvature offset at the beginning of the plan is bounded by 0.05 m −1, subject to the constraints of the plan transform (Sec. D.1). The nudges are random and temporally corre-lated across planning steps (Sec. 4.2). For each noise scale, we rerun each of the 72 failure scenarios 50 times, using new random nudges in each rollout. At a noise scale of 0.1 rela-tive to the action bounds, 53% of the initially failed scenarios achieve a score of at least 50 in at least one of 20 rollouts. This fraction increases with the noise scale (Fig. 10). By comparison, resampling the planner with new diffusion noise and no nudge avoids failure in only 2 of the 72 scenarios, even after 50 rollouts per scenario. Under the sampling configuration used here, resampling alone therefore rarely resolves these failures.

## C. Reward 

The episode reward combines a terminal score with a safety penalty at each step. At the end of the episode, the envi-ronment computes the closed-loop score S ∈ [0 , 100] of the driven trajectory using the nuPlan devkit’s metric definitions. The terminal score contribution is S/ 100 . An at-fault colli-sion or a departure more than 0.3 m beyond the drivable area terminates the episode with a terminal score contribution of zero. D.3. Speed events 

Speed events are enabled in each data-collection episode with probability 0.5. In these episodes, while a push or a brake is active, its value replaces the exploration noise added to the actor’s speed component. The curvature component always retains its exploration noise. A push starts when the vehicle has stood for 1 s, its base plan covers less than 0.2 m in the next second, and the IDM would accelerate the vehicle. Specifically, we apply the IDM to the ego vehicle with a standstill gap of 1 m and require an acceleration above 0.3 m/s 2. The push adds up to half of the speed bound and lasts while the IDM still gives this acceleration. A brake is held for a duration drawn uniformly from 2 to 5 s and lowers the planned speed by a quarter of the current speed, at most by the speed bound. The first hold starts with the episode. When a hold ends, the next hold starts at once with probability 0.5 and after a pause drawn from the same range otherwise. No brake acts during a push. The replay buffer stores the executed action of every step.

## I. Computational cost of RL training 

PlannerRFT reports its hardware but not its training time, so we compare estimated model FLOPs per training run up to the reported checkpoint (Tab. 8). We calculate training totals from measured forward-pass costs, counting each backward pass as two forward passes. Under the accounting assump-tions in Tab. 8, PlannerRFT’s reported configuration requires approximately 5.6 times as many model FLOPs as Plan-Nudge. For PlanNudge, actor and critic updates, including critic refits, account for approximately 85% of model FLOPs, and calls to the frozen planner account for approximately 15%.

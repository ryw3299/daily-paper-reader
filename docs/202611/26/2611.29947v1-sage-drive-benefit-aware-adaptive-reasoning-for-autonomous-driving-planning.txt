1SAGE-Drive: Benefit-Aware Adaptive Reasoning for Autonomous Driving Planning Donglei Rong, Wenbin Yao, Congcong Bai, Xi Gao, Qiaoqiao Ren, Sheng Jin, Cheng Xu 



Abstract —Explicit reasoning enables autonomous vehicles (AVs) to interpret complex traffic interactions and support trajectory planning, but introduces additional computational demands. Determining when explicit reasoning can improve planning accuracy remains a key challenge, as its benefits vary across driving scenarios. To address this challenge, this study proposes Scene-Adaptive Gating of Explicit Reasoning for Driving (SAGE-Drive), a benefit-aware adaptive reasoning framework for autonomous driving planning. First, paired planning outcomes with and without explicit reasoning are analyzed to measure the reasoning benefit, i.e., the reduction in planning error achieved by explicit reasoning, which serves as the supervision label. SAGE-Drive then learns to predict these benefits using geometric features of the initial trajectory planned without reasoning and multimodal scene information, and invokes explicit reasoning only for the scenarios with the highest predicted benefits under a predefined reasoning budget. Experiments on 1,497 paired driving scenarios reveal that explicit reasoning improves planning accuracy in 53.5% of cases while degrading it in 46.5%, yielding a near-zero average benefit. On the test set, with a 20% reasoning budget, SAGE-Drive achieves an average displacement error (ADE) of 1.5191 m, compared with 1.5252 m for random selection. At a 35% budget, it achieves an ADE of 1.5148 m, close to the 1.5141 m obtained when reasoning is applied to every scenario. These findings establish the predicted reasoning benefit as an effective criterion for reasoning selection, allowing explicit reasoning to be invoked where it improves planning accuracy at a fraction of its computational cost. 

Index Terms —Autonomous driving, adaptive reasoning, benefit prediction, computational efficiency, trajectory planning.

INTRODUCTION UTONOMOUS vehicles (AVs) operate in diverse traffic environments. Rule-based and learning-based planning methods perform reliably in routine situations, where road layouts, traffic rules, and interactions with other road users are predictable. In complex scenarios, however, such as those involving unexpected behavior of other agents, limited visibility, or ambiguous right-of-way, these methods may not capture how traffic participants interact, leading to unsafe maneuvers or unnecessary delays. Handling such scenarios requires an AV to understand the scene and its interactions before an action. Vision-language models (VLMs) offer a natural way to meet this need: they can generate intermediate reasoning steps that interpret visual observations, infer interactions among road users, and evaluate candidate driving actions. This explicit reasoning enables an AV to identify potential conflicts, anticipate the consequences of alternative maneuvers, and select trajectories that lower collision risk while avoiding unnecessary disruptions to traffic flow. Existing applications of explicit reasoning in autonomous driving (AD) fall into three categories: scene understanding, reasoning-guided planning, and adaptive reasoning. For scene understanding, DriveLM [1] uses graph-structured question answering to link perception, prediction, and planning. Reason2Drive [2] introduces annotated reasoning chains to support multi-step interpretation of driving situations. For reasoning-guided planning, DriveVLM [3] integrates scene description, scene analysis, and hierarchical planning. VLR-Driver [4] uses spatiotemporal reasoning to analyze traffic interactions and generate driving decisions. More recently, adaptive reasoning methods adjust whether and how much to reason according to driving conditions. FactorDrive [5] adapts reasoning steps based on planning-critical factors, and STEER [6] learns to choose between direct prediction and explicit reasoning using planning-related rewards. These studies show that explicit reasoning supports scene understanding and driving decisions, and that its computational cost motivates more efficient reasoning strategies. However, none of these methods explicitly estimates how much reasoning will improve or degrade trajectory planning accuracy before invoking it. As a result, reasoning can be invoked in routine scenes, where it adds latency or even degrades the trajectory, and skipped in challenging interactions, where it would improve the driving decision. To address this gap, this study proposes Scene-Adaptive Gating of Explicit Reasoning for Driving (SAGE-Drive), a benefit-aware adaptive reasoning framework for AD planning. First, the reasoning benefit is defined as the reduction in A2trajectory planning error achieved by explicit reasoning. It is measured from paired planning results with and without reasoning and serves as the supervision for benefit prediction. SAGE-Drive then combines multimodal scene representations from camera observations, LiDAR data, and ego-vehicle motion history with geometric features of an initial trajectory generated without explicit reasoning. Based on these features, a benefit predictor estimates the reasoning benefit before explicit reasoning is invoked. Under a predefined reasoning budget, SAGE-Drive selects the scenarios with the highest predicted benefits for explicit reasoning and retains the initial trajectories for the rest. The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section II reviews related work on scene understanding, reasoning-guided planning, and adaptive reasoning for AD. Section III presents the proposed SAGE-Drive framework, including reasoning benefit supervision, multimodal scene representation, and budget-constrained reasoning selection. Section IV describes the experimental setup and evaluates planning accuracy, computational efficiency, and the effects of different reasoning budgets. Finally, Section V concludes the paper. II. RELATED WORK Related work on explicit reasoning for AD falls into three categories: scene understanding, reasoning-guided planning, and adaptive reasoning. These categories build on one another, progressing from interpreting traffic scenes and their interactions to using such understanding for driving decisions and trajectory planning, and finally to deciding whether and how much to reason during planning (Fig. 1). 

Fig. 1. Framework of related work. 

A. Scene Understanding 

VLMs have been applied to AD to interpret visual observations and describe road users, road layouts, and traffic signals in natural language. Talk2Car [7] investigates visual grounding by identifying objects in traffic scenes from natural-language driving commands. nuScenes-QA [8] combines camera images and LiDAR data to support question answering about surrounding objects and their spatial relationships. LingoQA [9] extends visual question answering to driving videos, enabling the interpretation of traffic scenes across consecutive frames. CODA-LM [10] further examines the ability of VLMs to describe traffic scenes and identify unusual objects in corner-case driving scenarios. These studies establish the use of visual and language information to represent the key elements of traffic environments. Beyond identifying scene elements, recent studies have explored structured reasoning to understand interactions among road users. DriveLM [1] uses graph-structured visual question answering to link object identification, traffic interactions, and behavior prediction. Reason2Drive [2] organizes driving scene interpretation into sequential perception, prediction, and reasoning steps to examine road-user behaviors and their implications for driving decisions. NuScenes-SpatialQA [11] uses 3D scene graphs to support reasoning about relative positions and distances among road users. OmniDrive [12] further introduces counterfactual reasoning to assess how alternative driving actions would affect future traffic situations. These advances in scene understanding provide essential information about road users, traffic interactions, and potential conflicts. Reasoning-guided planning uses this information to evaluate candidate maneuvers and generate trajectories that account for surrounding traffic conditions. 

B. Reasoning-Guided Planning 

Reasoning-guided planning methods incorporate explicit reasoning directly into driving decision-making. DriveMLM [13] aligns multimodal language model outputs with behavioral planning states, enabling driving decisions based on sensor observations, traffic rules, and user instructions. DriveVLM [3] integrates scene description, scene analysis, and hierarchical planning to link traffic scene interpretation with driving decisions. VLR-Driver [4] uses spatiotemporal chain-of-thought reasoning to assess potential risks and infer the intentions of surrounding road users before driving actions. ReAL-AD [14] introduces hierarchical reasoning that organizes decision-making into strategic, tactical, and operational levels. These studies show that explicit reasoning translates traffic scene understanding into driving decisions that guide motion planning. Explicit reasoning has also been integrated into motion planning. GPT-Driver [15] reformulates motion planning as a language modeling task and uses a fine-tuning strategy to generate trajectory. EMMA [16] uses a multimodal language model to process camera observations and navigation commands, generating future trajectories through a unified language representation. ORION [17] links scene reasoning with trajectory generation by introducing a planning token that conditions a generative planner on the interpreted driving context. More recently, Drive-R1 [18] incorporates reinforcement learning to align reasoning processes with motion planning outcomes, using rewards derived from predicted trajectories and driving actions. These studies integrate explicit reasoning into trajectory generation, linking traffic scene interpretation with the prediction of future vehicle motion. However, applying explicit reasoning uniformly across driving scenarios adds computational cost without consistently improving planning accuracy, motivating adaptive reasoning strategies that determine when reasoning is beneficial. 3

C. Adaptive Reasoning for Planning 

Adaptive reasoning strategies adjust whether and how much to reason according to each driving scenario. AdaDrive [19] introduces an adaptive slow-fast framework that determines when to activate language-model reasoning based on scene complexity and prediction confidence. AutoVLA [20] combines fast trajectory prediction with slow chain-of-thought reasoning, using reinforcement fine-tuning to reduce unnecessary reasoning in routine scenarios. AdaThinkDrive [21] develops an adaptive thinking mechanism that learns to select between direct prediction and explicit reasoning with a reward that compares trajectory quality across the two modes. ThinkDrive [22] uses scene complexity estimation to trigger asynchronous causal reasoning while maintaining real-time planning. More recently, CAR-VLA [23] jointly considers scene complexity and dynamic risk to adjust reasoning depth, urgency, and focus, distinguishing between direct prediction, deliberate reasoning, and hazard-focused responses. These approaches decide when to reason by scene complexity, prediction confidence, and risk, or learn this decision from trajectory-based rewards. Another line of work allocates reasoning computation to balance planning accuracy and inference latency. GovernorVLA [24] introduces a learned routing mechanism that identifies driving situations with high predicted trajectory errors and selectively activates a more computationally expensive reasoning policy. ASSCG [25] formulates reasoning invocation as a resource-aware sequential decision problem, making frame-level query, cache, and drop decisions to refresh, reuse, or suppress slow-system guidance. SlackDrive [26] addresses runtime computational constraints by selecting the visual-token input budget before each inference based on available processing time and estimated planning performance. These approaches advance reasoning allocation by incorporating prediction errors, computational constraints, and planning feedback into inference decisions. Existing adaptive reasoning methods allocate computation based on scene complexity, model uncertainty, computational constraints, or planning-related feedback. However, these factors provide no estimate, before reasoning is invoked, of how much explicit reasoning will improve or degrade trajectory planning accuracy relative to direct planning. This motivates benefit-aware reasoning allocation that estimates the expected reasoning benefit in advance and uses this estimate to guide reasoning selection under a predefined reasoning budget. III. METHODOLOGY This section presents SAGE-Drive, a benefit-aware framework that selectively invokes explicit reasoning to improve trajectory planning accuracy. The framework first measures the reasoning benefit, defined as the reduction in trajectory planning error achieved by explicit reasoning, from paired planning trajectories generated with and without reasoning. These measurements serve as supervision for benefit prediction. SAGE-Drive then extracts multimodal scene representations from camera observations, LiDAR data, ego-vehicle motion history, and navigation information, together with geometric features derived from an initial trajectory generated without explicit reasoning. Based on these features, a benefit predictor estimates the reasoning benefit before explicit reasoning is invoked. Under a predefined reasoning budget, SAGE-Drive selects the scenarios with the highest predicted benefits for explicit reasoning and retains the initial trajectories for the rest (Fig. 2). 

Fig. 2. SAGE-Drive framework. 

A. Problem Formulation 

(1) Reasoning benefit . A driving scenario is described by four inputs: camera observations, LiDAR data, ego-vehicle motion history, and navigation information. Given these inputs, fast planning directly generates a future ego-vehicle trajectory, whereas slow planning incorporates explicit reasoning into trajectory generation. Planning error is measured by the average displacement error (ADE), the mean Euclidean distance between planned and ground-truth trajectories over the entire planning horizon. The reasoning benefit is defined as the ADE of fast planning minus that of slow planning, so a positive value indicates that explicit reasoning reduces planning error. C. Multimodal Scene Representation 

(1) Multimodal feature extraction . Multimodal features relevant to benefit prediction are extracted from camera observations, LiDAR point clouds, ego-vehicle motion history, and navigation information. Images from eight surround-view cameras at four time steps are processed by a frozen DINOv2-Small encoder, producing 32 visual feature vectors of 384 dimensions each. LiDAR point clouds are represented by 24-dimensional spatial features, including point distributions across radial and angular regions, point density, elevation statistics, and average distance. Ego-vehicle motion history is encoded as an 80-dimensional vector derived from 16 historical and current states, each describing two-dimensional relative position, heading, speed, and yaw rate. Navigation commands are represented by four-dimensional one-hot vectors indicating left turns, right turns, straight or following movements, and other commands. (2) Multimodal feature fusion . A Transformer-based fusion encoder integrates the extracted multimodal features into aunified representation of the driving scene. Visual features are projected into a 192-dimensional latent space and supplemented with camera-view and temporal embeddings to preserve their spatial and temporal information. LiDAR, ego-motion, and navigation features are projected into the same latent space and assigned modality embeddings. A. Experimental Setup 

(1) Dataset description. The experiments are conducted on a multimodal paired-planning dataset constructed from nuReasoning [28], spanning 340 driving logs and 499 clips, with three frames sampled from each clip. Each driving scenario is represented by multi-view camera observations, LiDAR point clouds, ego-vehicle motion histories, and navigation commands. For each scenario, Fast and Slow Planning generate separate trajectories over a 5 s planning horizon, yielding 1,497 matched pairs. The pairs are split by driving log into training, validation, and test sets of 1,047, 225, and 225 pairs, respectively, so that samples from the same log remain in a single subset. Additional unlabeled frames from training clips are used for self-supervised pretraining of the fusion encoder; the validation and test sets are used for model selection and performance evaluation, respectively. (2) Baseline models . To evaluate the effectiveness of benefit-aware reasoning selection, SAGE-Drive is compared with three baseline planning strategies: Fast-only, Slow-only, and Random Selection. All methods use Qwen-Drive-1.0-4B as the planning backbone so that performance differences arise only from reasoning selection. Fast-only applies Fast Planning to every scenario, whereas Slow-only applies Slow Planning to every scenario. Random Selection invokes Slow Planning for randomly selected scenarios under a predefined reasoning budget and retains Fast Planning trajectories for the remaining scenarios. SAGE-Drive instead selects scenarios according to their predicted reasoning benefits. For a consistent comparison, Random Selection and SAGE-Drive are evaluated under the same reasoning budget, with 20% as the primary setting. Planning performance is evaluated using the same paired trajectories and ground-truth references across all strategies. (3) Evaluation metrics . Trajectory planning performance is evaluated using average displacement error (ADE) and final displacement error (FDE). ADE measures the average Euclidean distance between planned and ground-truth positions over the planning horizon, while FDE measures their displacement at the final planning step. Both metrics are reported in meters, with ADE50 and FDE50 serving as the primary evaluation metrics over a 5 s horizon. To examine temporal differences in planning accuracy, ADE is additionally evaluated over horizons of 10, 20, 30, and 40 planning steps, corresponding to 1 to 4 s. Reasoning efficiency is measured by the Slow Planning call rate, defined as the proportion of scenarios assigned to Slow Planning. The effectiveness of reasoning selection is assessed through ADE reductions relative to Fast-only and Random Selection. Statistical uncertainty is quantified using 95% confidence intervals obtained through driving-log-level cluster bootstrap resampling. (4) Implementation details . B. Reasoning Benefit Analysis 

To investigate the effects of explicit reasoning on trajectory planning accuracy, paired fast and slow planning outcomes are analyzed from three perspectives: overall reasoning benefit, temporal benefit variation, and scene-dependent reasoning benefit. (1) Overall reasoning benefit . As shown in Fig. 3, explicit reasoning has heterogeneous effects across the 1,497 paired driving scenarios. It reduces ADE in 53.5% of scenarios and increases it in the remaining 46.5%, with large variation in the size of these changes. The direction of the change is largely consistent along the trajectory: ADE and FDE benefits share the same sign in 84.3% of scenarios, so gains and losses generally persist to the final position. These findings show that the effect of explicit reasoning is heterogeneous and motivate selective reasoning that accounts for both its gains and its losses. 

Fig. 3. Overall reasoning benefit. (2) Temporal benefit variation . The benefit of explicit reasoning is concentrated at the beginning of the planning horizon (Fig. 4). Fast and Slow Planning accumulate position error at almost the same rate over the 50 steps (Fig. 4(a)), so their pointwise difference stays small: it is positive at early steps but fluctuates around zero with widening uncertainty as the horizon extends (Fig. 4(b)). The cumulative benefit accordingly rises at first and then decays toward zero (Fig. 4(c)). Aggregated by interval, the benefit is clearest within the first 10 steps, whereas later intervals have wider uncertainty ranges that overlap zero (Fig. 4(d)). Explicit reasoning therefore yields a small short-term gain but no sustained improvement in long-horizon trajectory accuracy. 

Fig. 4. Temporal benefit variation. (3) Scene-dependent reasoning benefit . As shown in Fig. 5, neither initial speed nor scene identity reliably indicates whether explicit reasoning will improve planning accuracy. The mean benefit varies nonlinearly with initial speed, being slightly negative at intermediate speeds and positive at low and high speeds, but its uncertainty intervals largely overlap zero. Scene-adjusted estimates show the same pattern. The benefit also varies within individual scenes: across three sampled frames, most scenes contain both gains and losses, and a concordance test with a p-value of 0.957 shows that the within-scene consistency of the benefit is no higher than chance. These results motivate estimating the reasoning benefit from frame-level features rather than from coarse scene attributes. 

Fig. 5. Scene-dependent reasoning benefit.

D. Reasoning Budget Analysis 

Planning error decreases as the reasoning budget increases, and SAGE-Drive separates from random selection mainly at longer planning horizons, as shown in Fig. 7(a)–(e). At short horizons, the two curves nearly coincide. The SAGE-Drive curve is not monotonic at intermediate budgets because the realized benefits of the additionally selected scenarios vary. At a 35% reasoning budget, SAGE-Drive reaches an ADE of 1.5148 m over the full 50-step horizon, close to the 1.5141 m of Slow-only. This recovers about 95% of the ADE improvement of Slow-only over Fast-only with 35% of the Slow Planning calls. The relative gain of SAGE-Drive over random selection depends on both the reasoning budget and the planning horizons. At short horizons, the gain stays close to zero at most budgets. At longer horizons, positive gains emerge at several low and intermediate budgets, but the uncertainty bands also widen because the benefits of the selected scenarios vary more over longer horizons. Under the 20% reasoning budget, benefit-guided selection shifts the selected scenarios toward positive reasoning benefits, mainly at longer horizons. At short horizons, the distributions of selected and unselected scenarios nearly coincide. At longer horizons, the selected scenarios shift modestly toward positive benefits. Both groups still contain gains and losses, and the magnitude of both grows with the planning horizon.

E. Ablation Analysis 

As shown in Fig. 8, the gain from reasoning selection depends on both the selection strategy and the computational constraints. ADE improvements vary across strategies and reasoning budgets, and speed-based selection reaches its largest gain at a 20% budget. At this budget, speed-based selection and the configuration without the safety arbiter yield positive average improvements, whereas the remaining strategies stay close to zero. Inference deadlines further restrict which scenarios are eligible for Slow Planning: the eligible proportion rises sharply as the deadline approaches 22 s, but the budget still caps Slow Planning calls at 20%.

F. Qualitative Analysis 

The three representative scenarios in Fig. 9 show that explicit reasoning can improve, degrade, or leave unchanged the planning accuracy. In Case 01, a car-following scenario, Slow Planning produces a trajectory closer to the ground truth, reducing ADE from 1.496 m to 1.412 m and FDE from 4.348 m to 4.199 m. CONCLUSION This study investigated the effects of explicit reasoning on autonomous driving trajectory planning and proposed SAGE-Drive, a benefit-aware adaptive reasoning framework for autonomous driving planning. Analysis of 1,497 paired planning outcomes revealed that explicit reasoning reduced trajectory error in 53.5% of scenarios and increased it in 46.5%, resulting in a near-zero average benefit. To account for these variations, SAGE-Drive predicts the reasoning benefit from the Fast Planning trajectory and multimodal scene information and invokes Slow Planning only for the scenarios with the highest predicted benefits. Experimental results showed that SAGE-Drive achieved an ADE50 of 1.5191 m with a 20% reasoning budget, compared with 1.5252 m for Random Selection. At a 35% budget, SAGE-Drive achieved an ADE50 of 1.5148 m, close to the 1.5141 m of Slow-only, which invokes Slow Planning for every scenario. These findings show that benefit-aware reasoning selection retains most of the accuracy gain of explicit reasoning with a fraction of the Slow Planning calls. Future research will focus on real-time reasoning selection and closed-loop planning evaluation in dynamic traffic environments. APPENDIX

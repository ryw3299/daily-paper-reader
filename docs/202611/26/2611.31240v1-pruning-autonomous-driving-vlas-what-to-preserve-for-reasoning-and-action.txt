ABSTRACT 

Reasoning vision–language–action (VLA) models for autonomous driving use chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning to guide action generation, but incur substantial memory and computational costs. We study structured pruning for these models, focusing on preserving both scene understanding and trajectory prediction. Our analysis of Taylor importance in Alpamayo-1.5-10B shows that reasoning and tra-jectory objectives assign different importance to the same vision–language model (VLM) structures. In the action expert, MLP channels exhibit more stable im-portance rankings across denoising steps than query heads, and a small fraction of channels accounts for most of the measured importance. Based on these find-ings, we propose MoOP, which combines importance ranks from both objectives for VLM pruning and aggregates stepwise importance to select a fixed subset of expert MLP channels. At 24.0% total parameter reduction, VLM-only pruning with MoOP performs comparably to or slightly better than the strongest baselines in driving, while retaining 94.0% of the dense model’s LingoQA score, far above all other methods. At a higher reduction of 30.0%, MoOP degrades much less than every other method, as it is the only one whose reasoning does not collapse. Removing 93.75% of expert MLP channels increases total parameter reduction to 39.4%, with nearly unchanged open-loop errors relative to VLM-only pruning and competitive closed-loop performance. The resulting model achieves a 1.37 × end-to-end inference speedup and 36.6% lower peak memory than the dense model, without post-pruning fine-tuning. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Reasoning vision–language–action (VLA) models for autonomous driving (NVIDIA et al., 2025c; Zhou et al., 2025; Fu et al., 2025; Yuan et al., 2026) extend action prediction with explicit chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning (Wei et al., 2022) to understand complex driving situations and explain driving decisions. Alpamayo (NVIDIA et al., 2025c), for example, conditions a flow-based (Lip-man et al., 2023) action expert on context from its backbone, which includes Chain-of-Causation (CoC) reasoning generated by a vision–language model (VLM). Incorporating CoC in action de-noising process improves trajectory prediction and closed-loop driving over a trajectory-only base-line, particularly in challenging scenarios (NVIDIA et al., 2025c). However, this procedure involves substantial computation: a large VLM generates CoC tokens autoregressively, and an action expert runs repeatedly to predict trajectories through iterative denoising. As autonomous driving requires continuous action updates under rapidly changing scene conditions, this computational bottleneck presents a critical challenge for real-time deployment (Li et al., 2026). Structured model pruning offers a promising direction to reduce this cost by removing unnecessary parameters based on objective-driven importance scores (Ma et al., 2023; Li et al., 2025; Muralid-haran et al., 2024). However, applying structured pruning to reasoning VLA is particularly chal-lenging. Unlike standard VLM, reasoning VLA leverages a shared VLM backbone for two different

Deciding what to prune thus requires assessing structural importance across both training objectives and architectural components. To derive a tailored pruning strategy for reasoning VLA, we examine Alpamayo (NVIDIA et al., 2025c), a representative VLA framework for autonomous driving that predicts trajectories along with CoC reasoning. Specifically, we assess the importance of structural units for both reasoning and action predictions using Taylor importance (Molchanov et al., 2017; 2019; LeCun et al., 1989; Hassibi et al., 1993), which estimates the loss change induced by removing each unit. We further analyze the distribution of importance scores within the action expert across iterative denoising steps, accounting for its distinct operating mechanism from the autoregressive VLM backbone. Our analysis of the VLM backbone reveals that the parameters important for reasoning and those important for action generation are only partially aligned. Thus, pruning based solely on a single ob-jective leads to either CoC collapse or a sharp decline in action prediction, as described in Figure 1. These findings suggest that preserving both scene understanding and action generation requires ac-counting for both objectives during pruning, unlike existing single-objective pruning methods. For the action expert, parameter importance may vary across denoising steps. We find that the impor-tance of attention heads shifts with the step, whereas MLP channel importance remains nearly con-stant. Moreover, while the importance score for Q-heads is broadly distributed, Figure 5c discloses that the top 10% of MLP channels account for approximately 98.2% of the measured importance. Motivated by these findings, we propose Mixture-of-Objective Pruning (MoOP), which jointly con-siders the dual objectives and the distinct architectural characteristics of both VLA modules. For the VLM backbone, where the two objectives are only partially aligned, MoOP scores each unit by the maximum of its importance ranks under the two objectives and removes units critical to neither CoC reasoning nor trajectory generation. In the action expert, MoOP instead prunes dense MLP chan-nels that contribute little to prediction across denoising steps. At a matched 24.0% total parameter reduction, MoOP matches or outperforms the compared methods on open- and closed-loop driv-ing, and achieves the highest driving QA score by a large margin. At a higher reduction of 30.0%, the gap widens: MoOP is significantly better than all other methods in open-loop driving and the best in closed-loop driving. With additional MLP-channel pruning in the action expert, the model reaches 39.4% total parameter reduction, a 1.37 × end-to-end inference speedup and 36.6% lower peak memory without post-pruning fine-tuning, while retaining competitive driving performance. Our main contributions are as follows: • We demonstrate that VLM pruning must simultaneously account for both reasoning and action generation to preserve scene understanding and driving capability in reasoning VLA models. • We discover that a small subset of MLP channels in the action expert is sufficient to main-tain robust action prediction quality. • We propose MoOP, the only compared pruning method that preserves reasoning, which keeps it robust at higher pruning ratios where existing methods degrade sharply. 2Preprint. 

2 RELATED WORK 

Reasoning Vision–Language–Action Models for Autonomous Driving. Driving VLAs connect scene understanding and language reasoning with action prediction. DriveVLM (Tian et al., 2024) integrates scene analysis with hierarchical planning, while ORION (Fu et al., 2025) conditions a generative planner on an LLM-generated planning token. AutoVLA (Zhou et al., 2025) unifies reasoning and discrete actions within an autoregressive model. Alpamayo (NVIDIA et al., 2025c) generates Chain-of-Causation (CoC) reasoning and conditions a separate action expert on the VLM context to predict continuous trajectories. We study how to prune this architecture while preserving its contributions to reasoning and trajectory prediction. 

Pruning Language and Vision-Language Models. Language-model pruning methods estimate importance through gradients or weight–activation statistics, as in LLM-Pruner (Ma et al., 2023) and Wanda (Sun et al., 2024), and optimize layerwise sparsity allocation, as in T´ yr-the-Pruner (Li et al., 2025). For VLMs, SlimVLM (Wen et al., 2026) accounts for visual-token redundancy and module sensitivity. Reasoning-aware approaches further adapt compression to the generation pro-cess: RAC (Lucas et al., 2026) reconstructs activations from model-generated CoT (Wei et al., 2022) traces, while MuCRASP (Dutta & Aditya, 2026) considers reasoning-critical transitions and cross-modal alignment. Our work examines a shared VLM that supports both textual reasoning and continuous action generation, combining importance measured under the two objectives. 

VLA Compression. VLA compression has explored structural pruning, quantization, and inference acceleration. For robotic manipulation, EfficientVLA (Yang et al., 2025) combines layer pruning, token selection, and action-head caching; CLP (Nguyen et al., 2026) removes redundant VLM and action-head layers before fine-tuning; and QVLA (Xu et al., 2026) allocates channelwise precision using action sensitivity. In autonomous driving, Depth-Wise Probing and Pruning (Babu et al., 2026) removes ORION decoder layers based on changes in the planning token, while FlashDrive (Li et al., 2026) accelerates Alpamayo through caching and speculative decoding. We combine CoC- and trajectory-based VLM pruning with action-expert MLP pruning guided by importance stability and concentration across denoising steps, without post-pruning fine-tuning.

4 ANALYSIS 

We analyze Alpamayo-1.5-10B (NVIDIA et al., 2025c) to examine how importance is distributed in the VLM backbone under the two objectives and in the action expert across denoising steps. We use 100 calibration clips selected from the PhysicalAI-AV training dataset (NVIDIA et al., 2025c) for Taylor importance computation. Details on calibration dataset sampling are in Appendix A.1. For VLM units, IFM is obtained by backpropagating the FM loss of the action expert through the KV cache into the VLM. Importance is computed separately for the query heads and MLP channels of each layer, and all comparisons in this section are made within a layer and unit type, matching how units are selected for pruning in Section 5. 4.1 IMPORTANCE SCORE IN VLM B ACKBONE 

Finding 1. CE and FM importance rankings diverge in the late VLM layers. Even for the same generated CoC tokens, the two objectives favor different units for reasoning generation and action conditioning. We first ask whether CE and FM importance identify the same VLM units for pruning. Within each layer, we compare the rankings induced by ICE and IFM using Spearman’s ρ (Figure 3a). The rankings largely agree through layer 21 but diverge in later layers, suggesting that pruning with either objective alone can remove units important to the other. Figure 4a shows this comparison at the level of individual units. The units of each layer are sorted by their IFM rank, so each row of the IFM

5 METHOD 

Our approach uses CE and FM importance jointly to select VLM units and step-averaged FM impor-tance to select action expert MLP channels. In both components, selection is performed within each layer and unit type under a specified pruning budget. Applying the same pruning budget in every layer keeps comparisons focused on the scoring rule rather than on budget allocation. In the action expert, we prune intermediate MLP channels using FM importance. The CoC pre-diction loss depends on the VLM but not on the action expert parameters (Eq. 1), so expert pruning requires only the FM criterion. Because the expert reuses its weights throughout denoising, we score each channel using the step-averaged importance ¯IFM (ℓ, u ) in Eq. 4. This score summarizes the es-timated FM-loss sensitivity to removing a channel across the full denoising process. In practice, the choice of aggregation matters little. Using the score of any single denoising step instead yields nearly the same results (Appendix C.1), consistent with the stable rankings in Section 4.2. The stable MLP importance rankings in Section 4.2 support retaining a fixed set of channels for all steps. The concentration of MLP importance motivates evaluating high channel-removal ratios. Within each expert layer, we first average the step-specific scores and then rank the channels once, applying Eq. 6 to ¯IFM with o = FM . Expert attention is retained because query-head importance is more broadly distributed and its rankings vary across steps. Combined pruning yields a 1.37 × end-to-end speedup and 36 .6% lower peak memory than the dense model (Table 4). The two components contribute complementary savings. VLM pruning provides most of the end-to-end speedup ( 1.30 ×), as it shortens prefill and autoregressive decoding, which dominate latency. Expert pruning accelerates the denoising stage itself by 1.35 × (204 .3 to 150 .9 ms) and is essential for memory: it removes a further 1.71 B parameters and 3.19 GiB of peak memory, raising the peak-memory reduction from 22 .4% to 36 .6% . This saving relies on the stable MLP rankings of Section 4.2. If the rankings changed across denoising steps, each step would need its own set of channels and the expert would have to keep every channel selected at any step, whereas here a single pruned expert serves all steps. 

7 CONCLUSION 

We presented MoOP, a structured pruning method that accounts for the roles of the VLM and action expert in reasoning VLAs. Controlled comparisons show that using both reasoning and trajectory importance better preserves driving QA, trajectory prediction, and closed-loop scene scores than using either objective alone. Among the compared pruning methods, MoOP is the only one that pre-serves driving-scene reasoning, and its advantage in driving grows at a higher pruning ratio. For the action expert, we exploit the stability and concentration of MLP importance across denoising steps to identify channels for removal. Together, the two components compress Alpamayo-1.5-10B with-out any post-pruning fine-tuning: VLM pruning provides most of the inference speedup, and expert pruning further reduces the memory footprint while leaving driving performance largely unchanged. The two findings that motivate MoOP also largely hold on Alpamayo 2 Edge (Appendix C.3), sug-gesting that they are not specific to a single model. 10 Preprint.

All compared models start from Alpamayo-1.5-10B. The dense model, denoted Baseline, is evalu-ated without pruning. For the main VLM comparison, all pruning methods remove 24 .0% of the full model’s parameters and leave the action expert intact. We evaluate every model without post-pruning fine-tuning, so recovery training does not account for differences between methods. 

LLM-Pruner. LLM-Pruner (Ma et al., 2023) identifies groups of dependent structures and esti-mates their importance using gradient information. Removing these groups together preserves the connectivity of the remaining network. We apply this structural pruning baseline to the VLM and omit the optional LoRA recovery stage used in the original method. 

Wanda. Wanda (Sun et al., 2024) scores a weight by the product of its absolute magnitude and the norm of its input activations. The original method removes low-scoring weights to produce unstruc-tured sparsity. In our comparison, we use Wanda’s importance criterion to select structured VLM units, enabling comparison with methods that physically remove query heads and MLP channels. 

T´ yr-the-Pruner. T´ yr-the-Pruner (Li et al., 2025) optimizes the distribution of sparsity across lay-ers under an overall parameter budget. It constructs a supernet of locally pruned candidates at multiple sparsity levels and uses a coarse-to-fine search to select their combination. This provides a comparison with global sparsity allocation, whereas MoOP uses a uniform per-layer budget and combines importance from two objectives. The total parameter reduction is matched in our VLM comparison. 

Single-objective variants. CE-only and FM-only isolate the effect of the pruning objective. They use ICE and IFM , respectively, to rank units within each layer and unit type, then remove the lowest-ranked units. Both variants match MoOP in the Taylor criterion, calibration set, pruning targets, and per-layer removal counts. They therefore test whether either objective alone suffices to preserve the VLM’s reasoning and action-conditioning roles. 

MoOP and additional expert pruning. MoOP combines CE and FM ranks using the maximum-rank rule in Section 5.1. We separately assess additional expert MLP pruning on top of this VLM, using step-averaged FM importance as described in Section 5.2. The expert ablation removes 75% ,

87 .5% , or 93 .75% of MLP channels while retaining expert attention (Table 4). The main configura-tion uses 93 .75% expert MLP removal, reaching 39 .4% total parameter reduction. Results with and without expert pruning are distinguished in the main tables. A.3 EVALUATION PROTOCOLS AND DATA SAMPLING

Evaluation data and inputs. We use the full official LingoQA evaluation split (Marcu et al., 2024): 500 questions from 100 segments, with five questions per segment and two human reference answers per question. No questions are subsampled or filtered. Each segment provides five front-camera frames indexed 0–4. We use the last four (1–4), closest to the question time, to match Alpamayo-1.5’s four-frame input per camera. All four are labelled as the front camera, and the question is passed verbatim through the model’s native VQA template, without additional style instructions or ego-trajectory inputs. 

Evaluation protocol and metrics. Evaluation is zero-shot, with no training on LingoQA. Answers are decoded greedily with a maximum of 128 tokens. We use the official Lingo-Judge checkpoint, take the higher of the two reference-conditioned logits, and count an answer as correct when this logit exceeds zero. The reported score is the percentage of correct answers over all 500 questions. 

CoC likelihood. Alongside LingoQA, we report the teacher-forced negative log-likelihood (NLL) of CoC text, averaged over tokens. It is computed on the CoC generated by the Baseline for the Test500 clips, and measures how well a compressed model retains the CoC generation of the Base-line. Appendix B.1 additionally reports it on the ground-truth CoC of OOD-train. A.4 CHOICE OF PRUNING UNIT TYPES 

Query heads and intermediate MLP channels define the granularity of pruning, that is, the type of unit that can be removed. We did not choose these unit types because they were estimated to be less important than other types, and we did not compare importance across unit types in advance. Importance is measured afterwards, only to decide which individual heads and channels to remove within each type. Likewise, other units, such as layers, hidden dimensions and KV heads, are kept not because they are more important, but because of the architecture. In Alpamayo (NVIDIA et al., 2025c), each expert layer l attends to the KV cache of VLM layer l,which is how the VLM conditions action generation. Removing layers or hidden dimensions would break this layer-to-layer conditioning, and removing KV heads would remove part of the KV cache itself, which is shared by several query heads under grouped-query attention (Ainslie et al., 2023). Query heads and intermediate MLP channels, in contrast, can be removed without changing this interface. They are also the standard units in structured pruning of transformers (Michel et al., 2019; Voita et al., 2019; Kwon et al., 2022; Ma et al., 2023; Muralidharan et al., 2024). Removing them deletes entire rows and columns of the weight matrices, so the matrices simply become smaller. For example, removing an MLP channel deletes one row of the up and gate projections and one column of the down projection. Unlike removing individual weights, which leaves scattered zeros that standard hardware cannot skip efficiently, this yields real speedups without specialized sparse kernels. 17 Preprint.

To test whether the differences between methods grow under stronger compression, we prune every method at a VLM pruning ratio of r = 0 .5, which removes 30 .0% of all parameters. We eval-uate closed-loop driving on Origin150 and Hard100, open-loop prediction and LingoQA with the bootstrap of Appendix B.1, and call a difference significant when its paired 95% interval excludes zero. 

Closed-loop driving. As shown in Tables 12 and 13, MoOP achieves the highest scene score on both sets. On Origin150, it is significantly better than every other compressed method in scene score and at-fault collisions. On Hard100, its scene score is significantly higher than those of all other compressed methods except T´ yr-the-Pruner, whose difference ( +0 .037 [ −0.037 , +0 .111] ) is not significant. At r = 0 .4, MoOP already achieves the highest Origin150 scene score, but its margins are small, as shown in Table 6 (0.042 over T´ yr-the-Pruner and 0.018 over LLM-Pruner). At r = 0 .5,however, these margins grow to 0.124 and 0.232 on Origin150 and are clearly significant. 

Open-loop prediction. As shown in Table 14, MoOP is significantly better than every other method in open-loop prediction. The closest method, T´ yr-the-Pruner, has a minADE@6 higher by 0.813 m on Test500 and 0.749 m on OOD-Val262. Every compressed method is significantly worse than the dense Baseline at this ratio, but the gap of MoOP on Test500 (0.395 m) is about one third of that of T´ yr-the-Pruner (1.208 m). The ranking in open-loop prediction closely follows the ranking in closed-loop driving. 

LingoQA and CoC likelihood. Table 15 reports LingoQA accuracy and CoC NLL. MoOP keeps 47.4% LingoQA accuracy, significantly above all other methods and 13.2 percentage points above LLM-Pruner, the next best, which is not distinguishable from always answering “yes”. CE-only falls significantly below always answering “yes”, by 10.6 percentage points, and T´ yr-the-Pruner, FM-only and Wanda fall to 1.6–7.2%. MoOP also has the lowest CoC NLL among the compressed models (0.376), below CE-only by 0.164. C.1 EXTENDED ANALYSIS 

Non-uniform layer-wise allocation based on rank correlation. In the main experiments, we apply the same pruning ratio to every layer so that the comparison isolates the effect of the pruning criterion. Our analysis, however, also suggests a non-uniform allocation. We base it on the CE–FM rank correlation computed on generated CoC tokens rather than on all tokens (Figure 3a). Although the all-token correlation is high in the early layers, the CoC-token correlation remains low across much of the backbone, so units in the early and middle layers can also serve CoC generation and action conditioning differently. Let ρℓ be the CoC-token rank correlation of layer ℓ, and let ¯ρ be its mean over the layers whose ratio is adjusted. We set the pruning ratio of layer ℓ to 0.4 + a (ρℓ − ¯ρ),so that layers with a lower correlation are pruned less. Two adjustments keep this allocation stable. First, the CoC-token correlation fluctuates considerably between adjacent layers, which would cause abrupt changes in the pruning ratio between neighbor-ing layers. Since such changes degrade performance (Yin et al., 2024), we scale the deviation by 

a = 0 .25 , which limits it to at most 0.12 for query heads and 0.10 for MLP channels. Second, since the early layers are particularly sensitive to pruning (Ma et al., 2023), we keep the first i = 4 layers at the uniform ratio of 0.4. Query heads and MLP channels are allocated separately, units within each layer are still selected by the max-rank rule, and the total parameter reduction remains 39.4%, as in MoOP. Under the same budget, this allocation lowers minADE@6 from 0.951 m to 0.939 m on Test500 and from 1.118 m to 1.086 m on OOD-Val262 (Table 16). A more careful search over a and i may improve performance further. Our analysis can therefore guide not only the selection of units within each layer but also the pruning ratio of each layer. 24 Preprint. C.2 ABLATION OF THE COMBINATION RULE 

The max-rank rule does not weight the two objectives explicitly. This choice is deliberate, because the CE and FM importance scores have different scales: FM scores are on average about 1.6 × larger than CE scores. Since the max-rank rule operates on within-layer ranks, it is unaffected by this difference in scale, and it retains a unit if the unit ranks high under either objective. It also adapts to the rank correlation of each layer without a layer-specific weight. Where the two rankings agree, the combined score closely follows the shared ranking, and where they diverge, the top-ranked units under each objective are retained. To test whether the raw scores can be combined directly, we compare max-rank with max-raw, which takes the maximum of the raw CE and FM importance scores and thus implicitly favors FM in proportion to its larger scale (Table 19). Max-raw gives a minADE@6 of 0.974 m on Test500 and 1.173 m on OOD-Val262 and a LingoQA accuracy of 59.2%, compared with 0.950 m, 1.119 m and 68.8% for max-rank. We also compare max-rank with the mean of the two ranks and with the mean of the raw importance scores. The two rank-based rules perform similarly, and their paired differences are not significant on any metric, whereas both rules that combine raw scores degrade reasoning. Letting the scale of one objective determine its priority is therefore harmful, which supports combining the objectives through their within-layer ranks. D.4 MULTI -A NSWER CASES IN CLOSED -L OOP EVALUATION 

As the examples in §D.3 show, the closed-loop score reflects driving quality in most scenes. The exception we observed is the multi-answer case: a driving situation that admits more than one acceptable behavior, of which the protocol rewards only one. At an intersection, for example, both turning and going straight can be valid. Each closed-loop scene, however, has a single reference: the path the human driver took when the scene was recorded. The model is given no navigation command, so nothing tells it which of the valid options that path follows. AlpaSim (NVIDIA et al., 2025b) measures progress along the recorded path and stops scoring once the ego is more than 4 mfrom it. A model that chooses a valid option other than the recorded one therefore receives a lower score than its driving warrants. Figs. 23–26 show scenes where the unpruned Baseline scores below MoOP for this reason. In three of them, the Baseline states a route choice that the scene supports (a clear side street, a right-turn signal it reads as green, cross-traffic that has cleared) and takes it, while the recorded driver goes the other way. In the fourth, the recorded driver bears right at a fork and the Baseline keeps its lane. Scoring stops where the two paths separate, so the Baseline is credited with nothing it drives afterwards, whereas MoOP follows the recorded route and scores 1.000 in all four. In these scenes, the lower score of the Baseline reflects which route it chose rather than how well it drove. To quantify how often this occurs, we count a rollout as taking an alternative route when scoring stopped under the 4 m rule and the ego then continued for at least 10 m without any off-road event or collision. We also estimate the score lost to the rule for every rollout stopped by it, not only for those on alternative routes. The lost score is the additional progress score that the ego accrued along the reference route after scoring stopped, counted until the end of the rollout. The alternative-route fraction is similar across methods, at 5.0–8.0% on Origin150 (except 12.3% for CE-only) and 9.0–14.0% on Hard100, and its differences between MoOP and T´ yr-the-Pruner or LLM-Pruner are not significant. Even if this progress were credited back to every method, MoOP would still achieve the highest scene score among the methods at the same budget on both sets (0.867 on Origin150 and 0.663 on Hard100). The route dependence therefore lowers absolute scene scores to a similar degree across methods and does not account for the differ-ences between them. The ambiguity could be removed by conditioning the policy on a navigation command or by scoring against all drivable routes consistent with the scene.

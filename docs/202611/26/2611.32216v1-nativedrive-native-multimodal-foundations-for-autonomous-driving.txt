Autonomous driving requires semantic understanding and fine-grained scene perception for precise action prediction. Vision-language-action models (VLAs) inherit semantic priors, but low-dimensional action labels provide limited structural supervision. Some studies extend VLAs with visual generation tasks or adopt video-based world-action models (WAMs), but both approaches introduce appearance-reconstruction overhead, which is less relevant to planning. Native multimodal models offer an alternative to unify understanding and generation within one backbone. On this basis, we propose NativeDrive, a native encoder-free multimodal driving framework adapting the unified backbone for policy learning and introduce a dense supervision task tailored to driving. The Native Action Interface reuses the understanding expert’s token-prediction interface for the discrete policy and adapts the generation expert for continuous policy. Driving Semantic Editing reuses the generation expert’s editing capability for dense scene-structure supervision during training, while inference runs only the selected planning policy. NativeDrive achieves competitive planning performance among unified understanding-and-generation models on NAVSIM v1 and v2, with ablations validating its components’ effectiveness. 1arXiv:2611.32216v1 [cs.CV] 24 Nov 2026 1 Introduction 

Autonomous driving requires models to integrate high-level semantic understanding with fine-grained scene perception for reliable driving actions [26; 11; 25]. Large pretrained models offer transferable priors for these tasks: vision-language models (VLMs) supply world knowledge and semantic understanding [41; 13], while video generation models provide motion and temporal dynamics priors for modeling environmental change [24]. Transferring these capabilities to driving policies requires a suitable action interface. Low-dimensional trajectory labels alone only weakly supervise learning scene structure [12]. The key question is how to design action interfaces that match the pretrained backbone’s modeling mechanisms and dense visual objectives that reflect driving requirements. Existing approaches primarily build driving policies on vision-language or video generation backbones. The VLM-based route, shown in the leftmost column of Fig. 1(a), transfers semantic knowledge and instruction understanding into planning through an action interface [41; 13; 35; 12]. Some methods introduce dense visual supervision by adding an external generation module [12] or extending the vocabulary with visual tokens [12; 35]. The video-based route, shown in the second column of Fig. 1(a), exploits pretrained motion and temporal dynamics priors by learning from future-scene generation and action prediction [28; 24; 17; 16]. Prior work uses attention masking to decouple action prediction from future visual tokens, enabling trajectory-only inference [16]. Both routes achieve competitive planning performance. However, understanding-oriented VLMs typically rely on an extended generation module and lack jointly pretrained understanding and generation foundations [12; 35]. Video generation backbones model appearance and dynamics [24], but future-frame reconstruction also captures visual details less relevant to planning [37]. Unified understanding-and-generation backbones provide another route to transfer both types of priors [39; 12; 23; 42]. In the 3rd column of Fig. 1(a), some approaches use unified backbones that natively model discrete visual and text tokens to predict future visual tokens [39; 12; 23], either reusing token prediction for discrete actions [39; 12] or extending a continuous trajectory prediction head [23]. Recently, native encoder-free architectures enable multimodal understanding and generation via direct image-patch embeddings and pixel prediction [8], reducing reliance on separate visual encoders and intermediate representation alignment. This design supports jointly developing action interfaces and visual supervision for driving. Building on this, we propose NativeDrive , jointly designing a policy interface and an auxiliary visual task around the backbone’s native modeling mechanisms. In the rightmost column of Fig. 1(a), we build discrete and continuous driving policies using the native mechanisms of the understanding and generation experts, respectively. We also use backbone generation for a driving-specific auxiliary visual task. NativeDrive combines these key attributes: a unified backbone, flexible action interfaces, and training-only visual task as illustrated in Fig. 1(b). Our contributions are: • We propose NativeDrive, which adapts a native unified multimodal backbone to driving policy learning by pairing discrete action prediction with either continuous trajectory generation or training-only semantic editing. • We design a Native Action Interface aligning two policy branches with the backbone’s native modeling mechanisms. The discrete branch uses the understanding expert to predict quantized action tokens, while the continuous branch adapts the generation expert into a lightweight action head. • We introduce Driving Semantic Editing, adapting the generation expert’s pretrained editing capability to produce semantic maps. This training-only task provides dense supervision on road layout and traffic participants without additional inference cost. • We investigate key design choices for adapting a unified multimodal model to autonomous driving, including action interface, visual task, and joint training. Comparisons and ablations demonstrate the feasibility and effectiveness of the proposed components. NativeDrive achieves 88.8 PDMS on NAVSIM v1 [6] and 88.5 EPDMS on NAVSIM v2. ## 2 Related Work 

Unified multimodal models support understanding and content generation within one framework, enabling capabilities to transfer across modalities [2; 27; 29; 7]. Unified multimodal models can be grouped by whether they use discrete or continuous representations for visual generation. Chameleon [2] and Emu3 [27] autoregressively model both text and discrete image tokens, whereas Show-o [29] combines autoregressive language modeling with discrete diffusion for image generation. Continuous approaches generate images or videos in latent or pixel space. BAGEL [7] combines autoregressive language modeling with continuous image generation through modality-specific experts that share self-attention. Show-o2 [30] uses flow matching for both image and video generation. SenseNova-U1 [8] unifies visual understanding and generation in an encoder-free architecture with convolutional patch embeddings for input and direct pixel prediction for output. 

Driving action interfaces typically use discrete token prediction or continuous trajectory generation. Discrete interfaces tokenize trajectories through per-dimension scalar quantization [3], motion-primitive clustering [41], or frequency-domain coding [12]. Doe-1 [39] builds on Lumina-mGPT [15] and discretizes displacement and yaw, while DrivingGPT [3] uses component-wise binning with a Llama-style autoregressive backbone. AutoVLA [41] constructs physical action tokens through K-disk clustering. DriveVLA-W0 [12] uses FAST [22] to tokenize trajectories in its Emu3 [27] and Qwen2.5-VL [1] variants. FAST applies a discrete cosine transform followed by quantization and byte-pair encoding. Continuous action interfaces use regression, diffusion, or flow matching. DriveVLA-W0 [12] also connects a lightweight action expert to the backbone through joint attention for query-based regression or flow matching. Vega [42] uses a compact action transformer to generate trajectories through diffusion. DriveWAM [24] and UNIVERSE [16] jointly model continuous trajectories and video latents within a generative backbone. Beyond driving, action representations in embodied policies [22; 9] can inform the design of action interfaces for driving policies. 

Dense visual supervision for driving policies varies in task design, how visual generation is integrated, and whether action inference requires it. For task design, DriveVLA-W0 [12] and Vega [42] predict future images, FSDrive [35] generates future frames annotated with lane dividers and 3D object boxes, and ExploreVLA [23] jointly predicts future images and depth maps. To incorporate visual generation, DriveWAM [24] builds on a pretrained video generation backbone, while ImageWAM [37] reuses pretrained image editing for robot policy learning. DriveVLA-W0 [12] and FSDrive [35] extend understanding-oriented backbones with a generation module and an expanded visual vocabulary, respectively. At inference, FSDrive [35], DriveWAM [24], and ImageWAM [37] rely on visual generation for action prediction. DriveVLA-W0 [12], Vega [42], ExploreVLA [23], UNIVERSE [16], and Fast-WAM [34] can predict actions without visual generation. 3Frozen

## 3 Method 

In this section, we explore native action adaptation and semantic supervision for driving with a unified multimodal backbone (Fig. 2). The Native Action Interface reuses the understanding expert for discrete action token prediction and adapts the generation expert into a lightweight continuous action head. The two training configurations depend on how the generation expert is reused. One jointly trains the discrete and continuous branches to study how discrete supervision supports continuous planning, while the other pairs the discrete branch with training-only Driving Semantic Editing for dense scene-structure supervision. 

3.1 Framework 

Native Multimodal Backbone. We adopt SenseNova-U1 [8] as our native multimodal backbone. Its encoder-free architecture uses lightweight convolutional patch embeddings for image input and direct pixel prediction for visual output. With multimodal understanding competitive with leading understanding-only models and strong visual generation capabilities, it provides a foundation for transferring both capabilities to driving. 

Model Inputs and Outputs. At time t, the input is Ct = ( It, u t, s t), where It comprises multi-view visual observations from current and preceding frames. The navigation instruction ut and ego state st, including velocity and acceleration, are text-serialized and embedded with the system prompt. The primary output is a future ego trajectory: 

Yt = {(xk, y k, ψ k)}Hk=1 , (1) where (xk, y k) and ψk denote the position and heading relative to the current ego pose, and H counts future waypoints. 

Policy Configurations. NativeDrive supports two policies based on how the generation expert is reused. The understanding expert provides discrete action prediction in each. The first uses the generation expert for auxiliary semantic editing, while the second adapts it into a lightweight continuous action head. Section 3.4 describes training and inference for each configuration.

Lightweight Action Expert. We initialize a lightweight action expert from the generation expert to transfer pretrained capabilities for efficient action planning. Appendix A details the layer-wise weight interpolation and expert dimensions for the FM-only configuration. We initialize weights by interpolating the generation weights to match the reduced dimensions, while randomly initializing the action input projection and output head.

Data Construction. Figure 4 illustrates our offline annotation pipeline for front-camera images across the NAVSIM and Waymo datasets. A frozen Mask2Former [4] pretrained on Cityscapes [5] generates pseudo-labels for eight driving-relevant semantic categories. After removing small components and low-confidence pixels using rule-based filtering, rejected regions are rendered black. Retained labels are rendered with a fixed RGB palette to form spatially aligned image-semantic map pairs. Human review covers both the filtering rules and the resulting semantic maps, guiding iterative refinement of the rules and reprocessing of failed samples. To preserve original resolutions, inputs are padded to multiples of 32 before prediction and cropped afterward. They are accompanied by a fixed text prompt for instruction-guided editing, while samples without semantic targets omit the editing loss but retain applicable action losses.

74 Experiments 

In this section, we compare NativeDrive with representative state-of-the-art planners, evaluate its key components through ablations, and present visualization results. 

4.1 Experimental Settings 

Datasets and Benchmarks. We evaluate NativeDrive on NAVSIM [6] using its official splits and protocols. We report PDMS on v1 and single-stage EPDMS on v2 navtest [21]. Each input includes current and preceding frames from the front, front-left, and front-right cameras, sampled 0.5 s apart. The current front-view image is resized to 720p, and all remaining images to 360p. We predict ten future waypoints over a 5 s horizon at 2 Hz. All experiments use these settings, unless otherwise specified. 

Tokenizer and Policy Training. We train the VQ action tokenizer on trajectories sampled with equal probability from NAVSIM [6] and Waymo E2E [31]. For tokenizer training, we use AdamW [19] for 50,000 steps on 8 GPUs with a learning rate of 2 × 10 −4 and a global batch size of 16,384. For policy training, we fine-tune SenseNova-U1-8B-MoT-SFT [8] with AdamW, freezing the observation image embedding module and action tokenizer. We use a global batch size of 32, weight decay of 0.1, and a 50-step warm-up to a peak learning rate of 5 × 10 −5, followed by cosine decay to 10 −6. We train the discrete policy for 5 epochs on 8 H20 GPUs, with or without semantic editing. For joint training, we initialize the shared modules and token prediction head from the discrete-only checkpoint and the continuous action head from the pretrained generation expert, then train both branches for 10 epochs. 

4.2 Main Results 

NAVSIM v1. NativeDrive-C and NativeDrive-D score 88 .4 and 88 .8 PDMS on NAVSIM v1, respectively, rising to 91 .1 and 93 .8 under best-of-six oracle selection (Table 1). Without driving-domain backbone pretraining, NativeDrive-D leads the listed SFT unified models after just five policy-training epochs and achieves the highest listed best-of-six score. 

NAVSIM v2. On NAVSIM v2 navtest , NativeDrive-D delivers strong performance among unified models under the post-fix protocol, achieving 88 .5 EPDMS in single-trajectory evaluation and an outstanding 93 .3

EPDMS under best-of-six oracle selection. NativeDrive-C achieves competitive scores of 88 .1 and 91 .0 EPDMS under the same settings (Table 2). 

4.3 Ablation Studies 

We conduct ablation studies on the navtest set to evaluate discrete action representations, joint discrete– continuous policy training, and visual supervision. All settings remain fixed except the investigated factor. 

Action Tokenization. Table 3 compares four discrete action representations under the same backbone and training settings. Cluster uses motion prototypes [41], FAST uses frequency-domain tokens [22], and Text XY represents waypoint coordinates as text [33]. Learned VQ achieves the highest PDMS of 88 .11 , outperforming the others. Across the four representations, gains in reconstruction fidelity do not always translate into comparable gains in PDMS. To further examine the effects of token budget and decoding strategy, we compare AR and BAR at 4 and 16 

tokens/s and include a VQ+RVQ configuration at 4 + 4 tokens/s following ActionCodec [9]. As shown in Fig. 6, BAR outperforms AR by 0.48 and 4.24 PDMS points at the two budgets, respectively. The larger gain at 16 tokens/s suggests that parallel prediction within each block helps limit error accumulation in longer sequences. With BAR, increasing the budget from 4 to 16 tokens/s substantially raises reconstruction error and lowers PDMS by 0.42 points. Adding RVQ improves reconstruction but lowers PDMS to 84 .10 , suggesting that single-level VQ already provides sufficient fidelity and that predicting an extra block may hurt planning. ## 5 Conclusion 

We presented NativeDrive, a driving framework transferring native multimodal understanding and generation to planning. Its Native Action Interface supports learned discrete action tokens and a continuous policy initialized from the generation expert, while Driving Semantic Editing provides structure-focused visual supervision during training. NAVSIM ablations demonstrate the benefits of learned action representations, blockwise prediction, joint policy training, and current-frame semantic supervision. ## A Continuous Action Expert Adaptation 

We retain the generation expert’s Transformer depth and attention configuration while reducing the hidden width from 4096 to 768 and the feed-forward intermediate width from 12288 to 3072. We replace its visual interfaces with an action input projection ( 3 → 768 ) and a velocity output head ( 768 → 3) for three-dimensional motion increments. Pretrained weights are adapted independently within each corresponding Transformer layer through sequential one-dimensional linear interpolation along the output and input axes, skipping unchanged dimensions, while the action input projection and output head are randomly initialized. The same continuous-expert architecture is used in joint discrete–continuous training; NativeDrive-C additionally uses discrete-policy checkpoint initialization (Table 4).

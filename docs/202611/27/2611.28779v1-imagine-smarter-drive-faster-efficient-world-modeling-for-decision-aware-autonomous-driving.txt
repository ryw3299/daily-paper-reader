Abstract
World models hold great promise for autonomous driv-
ing planning, yet their ability to imagine the future often
comes at a formidable computational cost. Efficiency ef-
forts, such as knowledge distillation and domain-specific
pre-training, often shift this burden to costly teachers
or training pipelines, leaving lightweight world model-
ing an open challenge.
We introduce FastWorld, a na-
tively lightweight world-model framework for efficient au-
tonomous driving planning. FastWorld reduces computa-
tion through compact scene representations, short-horizon
action-conditioned prediction, and selective candidate ver-
ification and score refinement. These designs support effi-
cient future-informed planning without teacher distillation
or additional domain-specific pre-training. On NAVSIMv1,
FastWorld achieves 92.2 PDMS at 132 FPS on a single

1. Introduction
Autonomous driving has evolved from directly map-
ping sensor inputs to driving actions toward anticipatory
decision-making that predicts environmental dynamics and
evaluates action consequences. World models provide an
important foundation for this transition [10, 11, 14, 19,
45, 55, 61].
Recent world-model-based planning meth-
ods for autonomous driving have shown substantial per-
formance potential, but their computational efficiency still
limits practical deployment [19].
A common consensus
is that rich future imagination often incurs high computa-
tional costs. Explicit modeling approaches [13, 25, 56, 57]

typically generate high-dimensional images, occupancy, or
explicit scene representations. Although implicit modeling
approaches [12, 20, 28, 39, 60] avoid some reconstruction
overhead, they may still require complex state transitions
and candidate evaluation. These costs further increase when
future prediction extends to multiple candidate actions, cre-
ating a pronounced trade-off between planning performance
and efficiency.
To address this trade-off, some works use knowledge
distillation or pretrained knowledge transfer to balance
performance and deployment efficiency.
For example,
WPT [22] transfers a world-model-guided teacher policy to
a lightweight student policy through policy distillation and
world reward distillation. Drive-JEPA [47] combines a pre-
trained video encoder [1] with additional driving videos to
learn predictive representations. These methods offer ef-
fective practical solutions, but their capabilities necessarily
rely on strong teacher supervision, costly feature extractors,
or additional training data. Efficiency gains at inference do
not directly imply lower overall learning costs. Thus, these
approaches fundamentally retain their dependence on ex-
pensive representations. Parameter-efficient adaptation also
offers a complementary route [15, 23].
However, does high-quality planning necessarily de-
pend on expensive representations?
To re-examine this
question, we selected several representative autonomous
driving approaches [31, 34, 47] and analyzed the average
quality of both their candidate trajectories and final selected
trajectories across a subset of NAVSIM [9], as illustrated
in Fig. 1(a). A key observation is that the trajectories ulti-
mately selected by all models often fail to realize the full po-
tential of their candidate sets. Moreover, Drive-JEPA [47]
achieves similar candidate trajectory quality with feature
extractors of different sizes. This suggests that represen-
tation quality alone does not determine the performance
bottleneck. For autonomous driving world models, what
matters more is understanding the futures associated with
different candidates and making accurate selections accord-
ingly. Therefore, improving planning performance requires
not only richer future imagination but also more effective
use of limited future information for candidate evaluation.
The above analysis motivates us to explore a more direct
pipeline: learning future information that is truly useful
for planning under a limited computation budget via na-
tively lightweight architectural design that constrains the
cost of future state construction and trajectory evaluation.
As shown in Fig. 1(b), we propose FastWorld, a lightweight
world model framework for efficient autonomous driving
planning. First, FastWorld constructs a compact world state
that preserves camera identities and coarse spatial layouts.
Second, FastWorld generates a candidate trajectory set and
preliminarily filters a small subset of top candidates for
low-cost visual verification, confining expensive cross-view
feature interactions to limited candidates while preserving
shared candidate context. Built upon this, the lightweight
state transition network predicts compact future world states
conditioned on the current state, ego motion, and candidate
actions. Finally, a small MLP reads out future utility and
corrects the initial score with bounded residuals. In this
way, FastWorld allocates limited computation to both ‘pre-
dicting the futures’ and ‘assessing the value of these futures
for decision-making’, avoiding full high-dimensional scene
generation or complex future feature decoding for each can-
didate trajectory. Meanwhile, FastWorld does not rely on
teacher distillation or extra domain-specific pre-training to
achieve lightweight capacity. On the NAVSIM [4, 9] bench-
mark, FastWorld achieves an inference speed of 132 FPS
with planning performance comparable to state-of-the-art
methods (92.2 PDMS and 89.4 EPDMS), see Fig. 1(c). On
the nuScenes [2] benchmark, FastWorld achieves a 3s aver-
age L2 of 0.40 and a 3s average CR of 0.31, at 17 FPS. The
competitive inference latency demonstrates FastWorld’s po-
tential to attain a better balance between planning quality
and computational efficiency.
In summary, our main contributions are as follows:
• We identify the candidate trajectory selection bottle-
neck in autonomous driving models. We observe a sub-
stantial gap between the latent performance of the candi-
date set and the final selection in most models. There-
fore, enhancing candidate understanding and evaluation
is a promising direction for improving efficient planning.
• We propose FastWorld, a natively lightweight world
model framework. With compact state representation,
shared state transition, and selective candidate verifica-
tion, FastWorld structurally controls the computational
cost of future prediction and trajectory evaluation.
• FastWorld achieves a competitive balance between
performance and efficiency. Without teacher distillation
or extra domain-specific pre-training, FastWorld achieves
92.2 PDMS at 132 FPS on NAVSIMv1 [9], 89.4 EPDMS
on NAVSIMv2 [4], and 0.40 m average L2 error at 17 FPS
on nuScenes [2], offering an efficient world-model-based
solution for real-time autonomous driving.
2. Related Work
End-to-End Autonomous Driving.
End-to-end au-
tonomous driving aims to directly map raw sensor inputs to
planned trajectories. Recent research has increasingly fo-
cused on multi-modal trajectory generation [36, 41, 42, 59]
and closed-loop robustness [40, 59].
DIVER [42] com-
bines diffusion-based multi-modal trajectory generation
with reinforcement learning. GuideFlow [36] models multi-
modal trajectory distributions through constraint-guided
flow matching. Diffusion Planner [59] uses a DiT to jointly
model ego-vehicle planning and other-agent prediction, em-
ploying classifier guidance to generate multi-modal driving
2


behaviors and demonstrating closed-loop robustness on nu-
Plan [3]. These methods improve planning capabilities in
complex driving scenarios, but temporal interactions and
iterative trajectory generation also pose challenges to in-
ference efficiency [43]. To address these challenges, ex-
isting approaches adopt strategies such as task paralleliza-
tion (e.g., PARA-Drive [50] and SparseDrive [44]), sparse
scene representations (e.g., SSR [27], SparseDrive [44],
and DriveTransformer [21]), and fewer trajectory genera-
tion steps (e.g., DiffusionDrive [34] and GoalFlow [52]),
reducing computational overhead and supporting progress
toward safe real-world deployment.
Efficient World Modeling for Autonomous Driving.
World models for autonomous driving increasingly in-
tegrate future scene generation with trajectory planning.
However, explicit scene generation often incurs substantial
computational overhead [5, 49, 56]. Some studies model
future scenes in latent space [20, 29–31].
For example,
DriveWorld-VLA [20] connects world modeling and action
planning through shared latent states, avoiding costly pixel-
level future rollouts. Although latent-space modeling re-
duces visual reconstruction costs, rich imagination of fu-
ture worlds may still involve multi-step state prediction and
action-conditioned interactions. Other works further prior-
itize efficiency in world model design [8, 48, 53, 54]. For
example, SparseWorld [8] uses sparse scene representations
to reduce future prediction costs. Latent-WAM [48] com-
presses multi-view images into compact scene tokens using
learnable queries. Building on compact latent-space mod-
eling, FastWorld further reduces inference latency through
compact scene representations, short-horizon future predic-
tion, and selective trajectory refinement.
3. Method
FastWorld supports the planning task through compact,
action-conditioned future prediction, as shown in Fig. 2.
FastWorld comprises three main components: Observation
Encoding (Sec. 3.1), Action-Conditioned World Predic-
tion (Sec. 3.2, ACWP), and Future-Aware Utility Scoring
(Sec. 3.3, FAUS). The visual encoder constructs the scene
state and provides trajectory-aligned evidence for candidate
trajectory generation under a computational budget. ACWP
predicts compact future states conditioned on the current
scene and candidate actions, while FAUS uses these predic-
tions to refine candidate trajectory scores.

4. Experiments
4.1. Details
Dataset.
We evaluate FastWorld on NAVSIMv1 [9],
NAVSIMv2 [4] and nuScenes [2]. NAVSIMv1 is an au-
tonomous driving planning benchmark built on the Open-
Scene [7] dataset, providing 120 hours of driving data with
multi-camera inputs and fused LiDAR point clouds. The
core metric Predictive Driver Model Score (PDMS) is de-
fined as the product of a penalty term and a weighted av-
erage score.
NAVSIMv2 proposes a two-stage pseudo-
simulation evaluation.
Its core metric is called the Ex-
tended Predictive Driver Model Score (EPDMS). For open-
loop planning, nuScenes is a large-scale outdoor driving
dataset with 1000 multi-modal scenes. Each scene lasts 20
s, is annotated at 2 Hz, and includes 6 synchronized camera
images and LiDAR point clouds. We use L2 and Collision
Rate (CR) as evaluation metrics. See the Supplementary
Material for more information about the datasets.
Implementation.
For NAVSIM, we take synchronized
images from four cameras (CAM F0, CAM B0, CAM L0,
CAM R0) as input. A shared ResNet-34 [16] with FPN [35]
outputs a 14 × 24, 256-channel feature map per view. For
world prediction, each view is pooled to a 2 × 2 grid and
projected to 64 channels, compressing visual memory from
1,344 spatial tokens to 16 scene tokens. The planner gener-
ates 64 trajectories; we keep 16 candidates for visual verifi-
cation and predict a compact 1-second future state for each.
Training uses AdamW with a global batch size of 128. The
model for Tab. 1 and Tab. 2 is trained for 30 epochs on 8
RTX 4090 GPUs. For nuScenes, we use six surround-
view cameras. A Swin-T [38] encoder processes the current
frame and a frame from roughly 1 second prior. The utility
readout employs 36 tokens (width 64) per prediction step
to rerank 16 out of 64 proposals over a 3-second horizon.
The model for Tab. 3 is trained for 95 epochs on the same
hardware. See the Supplementary Material for additional
implementation details.

4.3. Performance-Efficiency Analysis
Proposal Potential and Selection Effectiveness. To fur-
ther investigate the trajectory selection bottleneck identified
in Fig. 1, we analyze the quality of candidate and selected
trajectories across representative planning methods.
As
shown in Fig. 3, we sample 100 scenes from the navtest
split and report the average PDMS of the best candidate tra-
jectory (red dashed line) and the finally selected trajectory
(green dashed line) for each method, together with the dis-
tribution of selected trajectory scores (purple dashed line
from bar).
A key observation is that high-quality trajectory gener-
ation does not necessarily translate into effective trajectory
selection. Despite generating promising candidates, most
methods exhibit substantial gaps between their best achiev-
able and actual selected scores, reaching 11.3, 10.5, and 8.6
PDMS points for WoTE [31], DriveWorld-VLA [20], and
DiffusionDrive [34], respectively. Moreover, the selected
trajectory scores of DiffusionDrive [34] and WoTE [31] are
relatively dispersed, with only 21 and 24 scenes falling into
the highest PDMS interval (99–100), respectively. In con-
trast, several other methods exhibit greater concentrations
of high scores.
Notably, FastWorld achieves a remarkably small selec-
tion gap of only 4.2 PDMS (96.2 vs. 92.0), the smallest
among world-model-based methods and close to the over-
all minimum of 4.0 PDMS (99.3 vs.
95.3) attained by
DrivoR [24]. These results empirically support our core
premise: effectively leveraging limited future-related infor-
mation can be more valuable than simply increasing the
complexity of imagined futures.

5. Conclusion
In this study, we introduce FastWorld, an efficient au-
tonomous driving framework that integrates compact world
prediction into trajectory selection.
Specifically, Action-
8


Conditioned World Prediction forecasts compact future rep-
resentations for candidate actions in parallel, while Future-
Aware Utility Scoring uses these predictions to refine candi-
date rankings. By directly predicting future states in a com-
pact latent space, FastWorld incorporates future scene infor-
mation into decision-making with minimal computational
overhead. Evaluations on NAVSIM and nuScenes demon-
strate that FastWorld achieves leading inference speed while
maintaining competitive planning performance, offering
practical insights into the efficient deployment of world
models for autonomous driving.

S2. More Details about Dataset
NAVSIMv1. NAVSIMv1 [9] is built on OpenScene [7],
a 2Hz redistribution of nuPlan [3], which contains ap-
proximately 120 hours of urban driving data. Each sam-
ple provides eight camera views and a fused LiDAR point
cloud from five sensors. The benchmark provides filtered
navtrain and navtest splits and evaluates a predicted
four-second trajectory in a non-reactive simulation: the ego
vehicle follows the plan using an LQR controller and a
kinematic bicycle model, while other actors replay their
recorded future motion.
The primary metric Predictive
Driver Model Score (PDMS) is:
PDMS = NC · DAC · 5 EP + 5 TTC + 2 C
12
(S8)
where NC is No At-Fault Collision, DAC is Drivable Area
Compliance, EP is Ego Progress, TTC is Time to Collision,
12


and C is Comfort. NC and DAC act as multiplicative safety
penalties. EP measures progress along the route relative to a
safe-progress reference, TTC penalizes a predicted time to
collision below the safety threshold, and C evaluates trajec-
tory comfort from kinematic quantities such as acceleration
and jerk. All component scores are normalized to [0, 1].
PDMS is averaged over evaluated frames and reported on a
0–100 scale.

evaluation. The primary metric Extended Predictive Driver

S3. More Details about Implementation
NAVSIM. The ResNet-34 [16] FPN outputs a 256×14×24
feature map per camera at stride 32. We flatten the spatial
grid into 336 feature vectors per view and preserve the four
views for image cross-attention. For NAVSIMv1, we jointly
fine-tune the planner and the world model for 30 epochs on
8 NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 GPUs with a global batch
size of 128. The approximate wall-clock training time is 75
hours. The planner uses a lower learning rate than the future
prediction module.
nuScenes. We project the 768-channel Swin-T [38] fea-
tures to 256 channels and use six learned queries per cam-
era view to obtain a 36 × 256 scene representation for each
frame. We jointly train the model on images for 15 epochs,
then continue training for 80 epochs using cached scene rep-
resentations and trajectory proposals, with an overall wall-
clock training time of approximately 20 hours. Retrieving
the cached features by timestamp avoids repeated image
encoding and proposal generation. During the final three
epochs, we keep the world dynamics fixed and update the
trajectory scorer.

ples include 2 row 2 col in Fig. S1, 3 row 4 col in Fig. S1, 1

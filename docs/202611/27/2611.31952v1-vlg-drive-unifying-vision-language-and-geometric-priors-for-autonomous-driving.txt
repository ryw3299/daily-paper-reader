## Abstract 

A driving planner needs both scene semantics, which vision-language models (VLMs) provide, and spatial structure, which geometric foundation models recover from visual his-tory. We present VLG-D RIVE , which keeps both models frozen and trains only a fusion interface and a planner from trajectory labels. At each camera-grid cell, the inter-face fuses the paired VLM and geometry features into one joint token through a geometry-anchored residual, and a command-conditioned planner reads these tokens to predict the ego trajectory. VLG-D RIVE achieves 31 .78 EPDMS on NavHard under perturbed initial states, the highest among the compared methods, and 88 .27 PDMS on NavTest from cameras alone. Pointwise fusion outperforms independent VLM and geometry streams on both benchmarks. In closed-loop driving on 420 HUGSIM scenarios, VLG-D RIVE out-performs all compared planners. These results indicate that a lightweight planner trained on fused features of frozen vision-language and geometric foundation models is an ef-fective alternative to adapting either model for planning. 

## 1. Introduction 

Driving decisions depend on both what a scene means and what motion its physical structure permits. Recognizing a merging vehicle must be related to its position and the available space; interpreting a turn must account for the road layout. Vision-language models (VLMs) connect vi-sual observations with semantic and task context [2, 25, 27], while geometric foundation models learn spatial represen-tations through reconstruction [34, 38]. How can a driv-ing planner make joint use of these different sources? Re-cent systems answer this question in different ways (Fig-1arXiv:2611.31952v1 [cs.CV] 24 Nov 2026 ure 1). UniDriveVLA decouples semantics and geometry into an understanding expert and a sparse-geometry per-ception expert [20], VGGDrive injects dense 3D geometry into a VLM-based planner [36], and DVGT-2 plans with a geometry-centric model that has no semantic stream [49]. A simpler alternative would give the planner both feature streams and let its attention combine them. Both features, however, describe the same image locations, so their corre-spondence is known in advance, yet this design leaves the planner to rediscover it from limited trajectory labels. What matters is therefore not only access to both sources but also the interface that combines them. Motivated by these observations, we propose VLG-DRIVE , which binds VLM and geometry features that share a camera-grid address before the planner reads them. The key idea is to let trajectory supervision shape how the two priors interact, so that scene meaning and spatial struc-ture inform ego motion together. A residual interface an-chored on the geometry feature combines each dense VLM– geometry pair into one joint token, which replaces the pair in the planner memory. Fusion is local while planning is global, so the interface decides how the two priors describe each location, and a separate, command-conditioned de-coder decides which locations matter for the requested ma-neuver. Both encoders stay frozen, and trajectories are de-coded by a dedicated planner rather than a language model. We evaluate VLG-D RIVE primarily on NavHard, be-cause its perturbed initial states require the planner to re-spond to a changed spatial relationship between the ego ve-hicle and its surroundings [5]. On NavHard, VLG-D RIVE 

reaches 31 .78 EPDMS, the highest among the compared methods. On NavTest, which evaluates recorded initial states [8], it reaches 88 .27 PDMS from camera input alone. Pointwise fusion outperforms independent streams at every tested label fraction on both benchmarks, with the largest NavTest margin at 10% of the labels ( 82 .23 versus 79 .21 

PDMS). The same interface improves a second frozen ge-ometry backbone, DVGT-2, on both benchmarks. In closed-loop driving on 420 HUGSIM scenarios with reactive traf-fic [47], VLG-D RIVE scores higher than LTF and well above VGGDrive and DVGT-2. A component-level anal-ysis shows that vision-language context mainly improves drivable-area compliance after the perturbation with both geometry backbones, whereas collision avoidance is al-ready supported by the geometric representation. The contributions are threefold: (i) VLG-D RIVE , a geometry-anchored residual interface that fuses frozen VLM and geometry features into joint tokens for a dedi-cated trajectory planner; (ii) evidence that the interface, not access to both priors alone, drives the gain: pointwise fu-sion outperforms independent streams at every tested label fraction and improves two geometry backbones; and (iii) 

the highest NavHard EPDMS among the compared meth-ods, the highest closed-loop HD-Score among the compared planners on HUGSIM, and strong three-camera NavTest re-sults with frozen foundation models, together with an anal-ysis of where vision-language context helps. 

## 2. Related Work 

Vision-language models for driving and planning. End-to-end planners connect scene representations to action through bird’s-eye-view aggregation [21], task queries [10], vectorized scenes [13], sensor fusion [7], and the design of perception–planning connections [39]. Foundation models add pretrained knowledge to this design space. Image-text alignment, visual instruction tuning, and the Qwen-VL fam-ily provide representations for language-conditioned scene understanding [2, 3, 25, 27]. Flamingo and BLIP-2 learn interfaces between pretrained visual and language compo-nents [1, 17], while OpenVLA adapts pretrained visual features to robotic action [15]. Driving methods built on VLMs connect these capabilities to planning in sev-eral ways. LMDrive uses language-conditioned closed-loop control [28]; DriveGPT4 combines explanations and control prediction [43]; DriveLM and DriveVLM orga-nize reasoning through structured questions or hierarchical plans [29, 32]. EMMA represents scene elements and tra-jectories in a common language space [11], and AutoVLA generates discrete trajectory actions autoregressively [48]. ReCogDrive connects VLM driving priors to a continu-ous diffusion planner [19]. Closest to the representation question studied here, UniDriveVLA coordinates under-standing, sparse perception, and action through specialized experts [20]. These VLM-based planners read the scene mainly through 2D image features, which leave the metric position of semantic cues implicit. VLG-D RIVE instead combines dense geometry features with VLM features be-fore a dedicated trajectory decoder reads them, so that each semantic feature is tied to the 3D structure at its location and the planner knows where that evidence lies in space. 

Geometric foundation models. Reconstruction provides supervision for reusable spatial features. DUSt3R pre-dicts point maps without requiring known camera calibra-tion or poses [38], and MASt3R adds descriptors for im-age matching [16]. Spann3R and CUT3R accumulate scene information sequentially [33, 37]; Fast3R processes multi-ple views jointly [44], and MonST3R addresses dynamic scenes [45]. VGGT jointly estimates cameras, depth, point maps, and tracks [34], and VGGT-Ω extends geometric pre-training across static and dynamic scenes and studies trans-fer to vision-language-action models [35]. By reconstruct-ing depth, camera poses, and point maps accurately from images alone, these models let an agent locate scene ele-ments at concrete positions in 3D space rather than inferring their location from appearance. DVGT-2 brings this capa-bility to driving by jointly predicting geometry and trajec-2tories in a streaming architecture without an explicit VLM branch [49]. VGGDrive instead incorporates dense geomet-ric features into VLM states and lets the VLM generate the trajectory [36]. VLG-D RIVE combines frozen geometric and VLM representations cell by cell on a shared camera grid in a trajectory-supervised interface and plans without reconstructing an explicit scene. 

Geometry priors in multimodal language models. Sev-eral recent models inject geometric features into mul-timodal language models. Spatial-MLLM builds on Qwen2.5-VL [3] and merges its 2D visual features with those of a spatial encoder initialized from VGGT [34] through a lightweight MLP connector [41]. VG LLM, a video–3D geometry large language model, adds VGGT ge-ometry features to the visual tokens of Qwen2.5-VL be-fore the language model [46]. VLM-3R fuses reconstruc-tion tokens from CUT3R [37] with 2D appearance fea-tures through cross-attention for spatial and temporal rea-soning [9]. In all of these models, the fused features are routed back into the language model, which is tuned with instruction-style supervision and produces its output as text. Most of the VLM-based driving planners discussed above follow the same paradigm. VLG-D RIVE departs from it. It fuses geometric and vision-language features outside the language model and learns a dedicated planner directly on the fused features, which regresses continuous ego motion from trajectory labels. Neither source model is fine-tuned, and producing a plan requires no language decoding. 

## 3. VLG-Drive: Unifying Vision-Language and Geometric Priors 

3.1. Planning from complementary priors 

Well-pretrained foundation models already capture much of what a driving planner needs. A driving-adapted VLM reads the semantics of a scene and the task it implies, and a geometric foundation model recovers the scene’s spatial structure from visual history. Both have been trained at a scale that downstream driving data cannot match. Rec-ognizing this potential, we ask whether a planner can be learned from their features alone while both foundation models stay frozen. VLG-D RIVE answers this question by unifying the two priors at the representation level. Let It denote the current multi-camera observation, Ht

the visual history including the current observation, ut the route command, and mt the recent ego-motion history in-cluded in the VLM prompt. Frozen encoders produce VLM features Vt and geometry features Gt, a trainable interface 

Fθ combines them into a joint planning representation Zt,and a dedicated planner Pϕ predicts the future trajectory:

The route com-mand conditions the VLM prompt and the planner, and mo-tion history enters only through the prompt. This factoriza-tion separates representation formation, which decides how the two priors describe each part of the scene, from trajec-tory decoding, which decides which parts matter for the re-quested maneuver. Pointwise residual fusion. With both sources aligned at every cell, the remaining question is how to combine them. Passing the two features to the planner as separate tokens would leave it to discover, from trajectory labels alone, which VLM feature belongs to which geometric ev-idence. Since the grid address already specifies this corre-spondence, we combine each pair at its address before the planner reads the scene. The combination takes a residual form, with the geometry feature, which carries the metric structure of the cell, as the anchor and a learned update that injects vision-language context: 

ri = fθ ([LN g (gi); LN v (vi)]) , zi = gi + ri, (5) where fθ is a nonlinear projection shared across locations and [·; ·] denotes channel-wise concatenation. The residual depends on both inputs and thus learns a cross-source inter-action. We call the anchoring stream the carrier ; the VLM carrier swaps the roles of gi and vi (Section 4.4). Zero-initializing the final projection gives zi = gi at the start of training, so optimization begins from the geometric part.

## 4. Experiments 

4.1. Experimental protocol 

We train on NAVSIM v1 NavTrain and evaluate on two benchmarks. NavTest contains 12 ,146 scenarios scored by PDMS under the official four-second non-reactive proto-col [8]; PDMS multiplies no-at-fault collision and drivable-area compliance terms with a weighted average of ego progress, time to collision, and comfort. NavHard con-tains 5,912 observations scored by the official two-stage EPDMS [5]. Stage 1 scores the plan from 450 recorded scenes; Stage 2 scores plans from 5,462 synthetic start states rendered around the human driver’s position four sec-onds later, each weighted by its proximity to the endpoint of the Stage-1 plan, and EPDMS multiplies the two stage scores of each scene. VLG-D RIVE builds its frozen feature branches on a driving-adapted 2B Qwen3-VL model, the Stage-1 checkpoint released with UniDriveVLA [20]. The VLM receives the current front-left, front, and front-right images, and VGGT-Ω receives three timestamps per camera (Appendix B.1). Only the interface and planner are trained, 

106 .1M parameters in total. The planner is a three-layer decoder of width 1024 with eight attention heads and eight planning queries, trained from scratch for 24 ,000 updates with batch size 128, AdamW, a learning rate of 2.5 × 10 −4,and 4,000 warmup updates on cached encoder outputs. Pub-lished baseline scores come from the cited sources, and we evaluate VGGDrive and DVGT-2 from their official check-points (Appendix C.1); Appendix A lists all recipes, and Appendix C.3 reports training and inference cost. Each en-try is one training run; Appendix B.3 reports the variation across runs. 

4.2. Overall planning performance 

Perturbed-state planning. NavHard tests whether a plan-ner produces useful motion when the ego state departs from the recorded trajectory. Table 1 reports every component at both stages and compares VLG-D RIVE with published baselines and with the two closest alternatives to our de-sign, VGGDrive, which feeds geometry into a VLM that generates the plan, and DVGT-2, which plans from geome-try alone. VLG-D RIVE reaches 31 .78 EPDMS, 2.98 points above the strongest baseline, DVGT-2. The gain comes from Stage 2, which scores the plans made after perturba-tion. Its stage score of 41 .0 is the highest reported, driven by higher NC and TTC than DVGT-2 at a small cost in ego progress. 

Standard-distribution planning. NavTest evaluates the same planner from recorded initial states, measuring its driving quality under the standard distribution (Table 2). VLG-D RIVE reaches 88 .27 PDMS from three cameras, above DiffusionDrive, which additionally uses LiDAR, and 

4.25 points above VGGDrive, which generates trajectories with its VLM. DVGT-2 trains all parameters of its geom-etry backbone and planner on large-scale driving data and reaches 88 .60 PDMS, and DVGT-2-NAVSIM further fine-tunes the full model on NAVSIM with eight cameras to reach 90 .30 . VLG-D RIVE keeps both foundation mod-els frozen, with a geometry backbone not trained for driv-ing, and trains only 106 .1M parameters in about 58 GPU-hours, against about ten days on 64 GPUs for DVGT-2 (Ap-pendix C.3); it comes within 0.33 PDMS of DVGT-2 and exceeds the same checkpoint by 2.98 EPDMS on NavHard, which makes a lightweight planner on general-purpose pri-ors a promising route for driving. 

4.3. Closed-loop driving on HUGSIM 

NAVSIM scores a plan without letting it change the scene. We therefore also drive the NAVSIM-trained planners in closed loop on HUGSIM [47], which renders reconstructed scenes from nuScenes [4], Waymo [31], KITTI-360 [24], and PandaSet [42] with reactive inserted vehicles. The ego vehicle follows each plan for 0.25 s per step, for up to 400 steps. Table 3 reports the HUGSIM metrics, and Appendix C.2 lists the inputs of each planner. HD-Score combines route completion with per-step collision, drivable-area, time-to-collision, and comfort terms, and we give its 95% scenario-bootstrap interval. Paired over all 420 scenarios, VLG-D RIVE exceeds LTF by 4.7 HD-Score points ( 95% interval [1 .1, 8.4] ), VGGDrive by 15 .2

([12 .3, 18 .2] ), and DVGT-2 by 9.8 ([6 .3, 13 .4] ). VLG-DRIVE has the highest collision, drivable-area, time-to-collision, and route-completion scores of all compared planners; only DVGT-2 scores higher on comfort. ## 5. Conclusion and Limitations 

VLG-D RIVE turns frozen vision-language and geometry features into a trajectory-supervised joint planning repre-sentation. It reaches 31 .78 EPDMS on NavHard, the high-est among the compared methods, and 88 .27 PDMS on NavTest from camera input alone. Pointwise fusion outper-forms independent streams at every tested label fraction and improves two different geometry backbones on both bench-marks. In closed-loop driving on HUGSIM, VLG-D RIVE 

8outperforms all compared planners. Vision-language con-text mainly improves drivable-area compliance after pertur-bation with both geometry backbones, while the geomet-ric representation already supports collision avoidance. A lightweight planner trained on the fused features of frozen vision-language and geometric foundation models is there-fore an effective alternative to adapting either model for planning. Because VLG-D RIVE keeps both foundation models frozen and trains only a lightweight interface and planner, fully fine-tuning the geometry model on larger driving datasets, as DVGT-2 does, could further improve the results given sufficient compute. Closed-loop evalua-tion on further benchmarks such as nuPlan [14] and further pretrained encoder combinations will test how broadly joint representation learning benefits driving planners.

11 A. Implementation Details 

Sources of vision-language and geometric priors. The vision-language branch uses a driving-adapted Qwen3-VL model with 2B parameters, the publicly released Stage-1 nuScenes checkpoint of UniDriveVLA [20]. According to its released training configuration, it is fine-tuned from Qwen3-VL-2B-Instruct [2] on driving question-answering and planning corpora built on nuScenes [4] and on BDD-X, DRAMA, LingoQA, CODA-LM, Talk2Car, SUTD-TrafficQA, and MAPLM, together with a general visual question-answering subset. None of these sources is derived from nuPlan [14], from which the NAVSIM logs are drawn, so no NAVSIM training or test log reaches the frozen VLM during its adaptation. The geometry branch uses VGGT-

Ω. Both encoders are frozen during downstream training. VLM features correspond to the current front-left, front, and front-right images. The main geometry features are ex-tracted per camera with visual history, and the current-frame grid is passed to the planner. 

Dense layout and spatial indexing. Both branches sup-ply a 17 × 30 grid per camera. Qwen3-VL encodes each 

1920 × 1080 view at its native resolution, which its proces-sor rounds to 1920 × 1088 ; with 16 -pixel patches and 2 × 2

token merging, each view yields a 34 × 60 grid of merged visual tokens. We take the final hidden states of the lan-guage model at these image-token positions, restore their two-dimensional layout from the processor’s grid size, and average-pool each 2 × 2 block, so every cell of the 17 × 30 

grid summarizes a fixed 64 ×64 -pixel image region. VGGT-

Ω features of the same view are pooled to the same grid. In an earlier front-camera, geometry-only study, a coarser 

8 × 11 grid scored 86 .74 NavTest PDMS against 87 .37 for 

17 × 30 . Pooling and camera ordering define correspond-ing row-major addresses, so correspondence is defined at the grid level. Source-specific adapters project the features to the decoder width. Ray directions are computed at cell centers from the recorded intrinsics and camera-to-ego ex-trinsics, and the Pl¨ ucker encoding of Equation 4 is applied to both sources. The geometry adapter adds the encoding to its projected feature. The VLM adapter uses a second encoding of the same form with its own random matrix, maps it through a zero-initialized linear layer, and adds it to Vi before projection, so the VLM features are unchanged at initialization. The geometry adapter additionally adds a learned camera embedding and a fixed two-dimensional si-nusoidal encoding of the grid position. 

Fusion and memory. The pointwise fusion module is a two-layer MLP with GELU activation, source-specific Lay-erNorm, and a zero-initialized output layer. Concatenat-ing independent streams retains 1,530 dense VLM tokens and 1,530 dense geometry tokens, together with context, for 3,114 memory tokens. Pointwise fusion retains 1,530 

joint tokens, three camera tokens, 48 register tokens, a null token, and two global VLM tokens (the final-layer hidden state averaged over the non-image prompt tokens and the hidden state at the last prompt position), totaling 1,584 de-coder memory tokens. 

Training configurations. The main system and the data-efficiency, backbone, feature-level, and history-length stud-ies use the main recipe; Sec. B.5 lists the recipe of each fusion-study row. Table S1 compares the two recipes. Both have three decoder layers, eight attention heads, and a feed-forward expansion of four. The main recipe has 106.1M trainable parameters outside the frozen encoders. When the source representation is fixed, frozen-encoder outputs are cached once and reused during downstream training; exper-iments that vary internal feature levels extract the relevant features online.

Planning queries and losses. Eight planning queries repre-sent future poses at 0.5-second intervals over four seconds. A learned four-way command embedding (left, straight, right, or unknown) conditions these queries. Each decoder layer applies query self-attention, memory cross-attention, and a feed-forward block. The model predicts normalized per-step (∆ x, ∆y, ∆ψ) in the NAVSIM ego frame and is trained with the objective of Equation 7. Outputs are de-normalized per channel and cumulatively summed. The ego-state, ego-pose, and per-camera queries share the de-coder layers with the planning queries, and their prediction heads serve only the training objective. Lstate is an L1 loss on the normalized current velocity and acceleration. Lpose 

is a Smooth-L1 loss on the ego motion since the previous frame, with the heading change represented by its sine and cosine. Lcam is a Smooth-L1 consistency loss between the predicted camera-pose encodings and those of the frozen geometry encoder. 

Prompt and causal inputs. The VLM prompt contains the native NAVSIM command and a recent history-trajectory string in the convention expected by the driving model. Mo-tion history reaches the model only through this prompt and the geometry history. The planner receives no ego-state in-put; ego velocity and acceleration serve only as auxiliary targets. Sec. C.3. Computational cost 

Table S7 summarizes training and inference cost. VLG-DRIVE trains only its interface and planner on cached en-coder outputs; the caches are computed once per source and reused by all runs that share it. Inference runs both frozen encoders online for every step, and the 2B VLM dominates its latency: it accounts for 65 of the 87 TFLOPs per step and the geometry encoder for 22 , so the geometry-only planner needs a quarter of the compute and 40% of the latency. C.5. Attention and spatial interventions 

Where the planner reads. Planning queries place 99 .94% 

of their cross-attention on the fused dense grid, which holds 

96 .6% of the memory tokens. Within the grid, attention concentrates on the region the vehicle will traverse, as a projected reference-path tube occupies 2.19% of the dense cells but receives 12 .89% of the attention mass. Splitting the dense attention by camera and averaging within each command group shows that the reading follows the route command. Left-command scenes place 36 .9% of the atten-tion on the front-left camera and 23 .6% on the front-right camera; right-command scenes reverse this ordering, with 

15 .1% and 32 .8% . Straight-command scenes concentrate 

59 .5% of the attention on the front camera, and scenes with-out a known command spread it nearly evenly across the three views. The same fused memory is therefore read dif-ferently according to the requested maneuver. 

Attended locations drive the prediction. To test whether this attention reflects reliance [12, 40], we replace selected features with the mean dense feature of the same camera in every decoder layer and recompute the trajectories. Replac-ing the top 5% of attended cells shifts waypoints by 0.717 

m, compared with 0.066 m for equal-size random cells and 

0.0009 m for the bottom 5% . Replacing the path tube it-self shifts waypoints by 0.414 m, compared with 0.043 mfor an equal-size random control. The planner’s predictions therefore depend on a small set of localized memory cells concentrated along the driving path. 

Protocol. The analysis uses the VLG-D RIVE model re-ported in Tables 1 and 2. The diagnostic set is drawn from the 3,373 validation samples by taking, within each com-mand group (left, straight, right, and unknown), one scene from each of the low, middle, and high trajectory-error strata, so that it spans every route command and a range of difficulty. Cross-attention is recovered for all decoder layers, atten-tion heads, and planning queries by replaying the observed attention calls with their masks; the recomputed outputs match the original calls to within 1.91 × 10 −5. Quantita-tive measurements use the native attention grid. In every decoder layer, selected dense key/value inputs are replaced by the mean dense feature from the same cam-era. The top and bottom 5% of attended cells are compared with three random selections of the same size. A second in-tervention targets a one-cell-radius neighborhood of valid, visible projected reference waypoints, using equal-size ran-dom controls. Projection uses (x, y, 0) and calibrated cam-era transforms, since the trajectory’s third channel is head-ing. Invisible reference nodes are omitted, and the reference trajectory serves only to define the diagnostic region. The reported shift is the mean distance between the per-turbed and original predicted waypoints. Averaged over scenes, the path tube receives 6.74 × its share of cells in 16 attention mass.

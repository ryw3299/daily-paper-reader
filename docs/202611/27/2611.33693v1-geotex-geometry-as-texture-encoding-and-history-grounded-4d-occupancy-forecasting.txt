## Abstract 

4D occupancy forecasting models 3D scene evolution to support future prediction and scenario generation in autonomous-driving world models. Semantic occupancy abstracts image color and texture into discrete labels, leav-ing 3D voxel arrangements as the primary cues for distin-guishing local shapes within each class. Existing meth-ods borrow image-encoding frameworks to obtain com-pact latent representations through height-channel encod-ing or plane factorization, but do not sufficiently model within-column height patterns and cross-column relations jointly before compression, weakening these geometric cues. Forecasting also underutilizes complementary his-tory and struggles to retain reliable observation evidence. We therefore propose GeoTex, an occupancy world model combining geometry-as-texture encoding with history-grounded forecasting. GeoTex Representation jointly en-codes within-column height patterns and cross-column re-lations into compact continuous latent representations and restores 3D structure through content-dependent height unfolding and geometric interaction. GeoTex Prediction retains initial evidence throughout rollout in fixed cross-observation relational memory and incorporates comple-mentary recent history through target-referenced residual updates. After prediction training, decoder-only adap-tation improves semantic decoding of predicted states. GeoTex improves average forecasting mIoU and IoU by 

9.79% and 11.91% relative to GenieDrive while reducing parameters from 3.47M to 2.33M, with gains increasing at longer horizons.

To efficiently model scene evolution, these models encode historical occupancy into compact latent states and predict their changes. Forecasting quality therefore depends both on the scene structure preserved in latent 1arXiv:2611.33693v1 [cs.CV] 25 Nov 2026 representations and on how history is used during predic-tion. To construct these states, existing OWMs primar-ily adopt VAE-series architectures from image genera-tion [8, 13, 14, 23, 25], but their encoding designs do not fully account for how occupancy expresses local shape. In Fig. 1(a–b), image color and texture provide cues for distinguishing tree structures [10, 47, 48], whereas se-mantic occupancy abstracts these variations into a shared vegetation label, making local shape discrimination de-pend on 3D voxel arrangements. Continuation, gaps, and termination along vertical columns define height patterns, while variations and connections across columns describe surfaces, boundaries, and spatial extent. Together, these relations form the geometric texture of occupancy scenes. Height-channel encoding [18, 46] and tri-plane factoriza-tion [6, 41] produce compact representations but insuffi-ciently model these relations jointly before compression, weakening the geometric cues that distinguish local shape (Fig. 1(c)). This mismatch between encoding design and how occupancy expresses structure motivates encoding centered on occupancy geometry. Forecasting also requires effective use of historical ev-idence. Existing methods learn scene evolution through autoregressive prediction [18, 41, 46] or diffusion gener-ation [11, 24, 29], and some further combine geometric propagation and content refinement [7, 19, 34] to exploit spatial correspondence for forecasting. However, histor-ical feature aggregation does not explicitly distinguish what each frame adds relative to the current target; even with spatial correspondence, observations still differ in content due to visibility and sampling. Meanwhile, dur-ing autoregressive rollout with sliding history, real obser-vations gradually leave the window, limiting continued access to initial observation evidence. Moreover, a de-coder trained only on states encoded from ground-truth occupancy faces a distribution shift when decoding autore-gressively generated states, affecting semantic decoding quality. To address these issues, we propose GeoTex , an occu-pancy world model that combines geometry-as-texture encoding with history-grounded forecasting. At the rep-resentation stage, GeoTex Representation alternates height-wise and horizontal interactions after local 3D encoding, linking within-column shape to surrounding structures before height-ordered aggregation into com-pact continuous latent states. During reconstruction, the decoder unfolds content-dependent height features and restores occupancy through geometric interactions and 3D upsampling. In this continuous latent space, GeoTex Prediction 

retains initial observation evidence throughout rollout in fixed relational memory and tracks recent states through sliding history. At each prediction step, ego-motion lo-cates historical regions corresponding to target positions, and current target features guide content retrieval. To ex-ploit complementary information from recent history, we introduce a target-referenced historical residual update 

that subtracts current target features from retrieved his-torical features, weights the residuals across frames, and writes them into the target state. After prediction train-ing, we freeze the other modules and adapt the decoder using encoded and predicted states to improve semantic decoding. Our contributions are summarized as follows: • We introduce GeoTex Representation, which jointly organizes height patterns and cross-column relations during compression and reconstruction to preserve geo-metric texture in occupancy scenes. • We propose GeoTex Prediction, retaining initial scene evidence in relational memory and extracting comple-mentary updates through target-referenced historical residuals for history-grounded forecasting. • With only 2.33M parameters, GeoTex improves aver-age forecasting mIoU and IoU by 9.79% and 11.91% 

relative to leading methods, better preserves scene ge-ometry, and widens its advantage at longer horizons. 

## 2 Related Work 

2.1 3D Scene Representation and Latent Encoding 

3D scene representation research organizes scene ge-ometry through height information and spatial context. BEVDepth [16] and BEVHeight [38] connect image features to 3D space using depth and ground-relative height, respectively, while 3D occupancy perception meth-ods further recover scene structure through spatial con-text [5, 17, 30, 45]. To efficiently forecast scene evolution, occupancy world models draw on image and video tok-enizers [8, 33, 42, 43] to construct latent states through voxel encoding [37], temporal reconstruction [15], and compact planar representations [3, 18, 41, 46]. However, existing encoders insufficiently organize within-column height patterns and cross-column relations jointly, weak-ening local shape cues. GeoTex Representation jointly organizes height-wise and horizontal relations before compression and restores 3D structure through content-dependent height unfolding during decoding, preserving geometric texture throughout encoding and reconstruc-tion.

## 3 Proposed Method 

3.1 Overall Architecture 

As shown in Fig. 2, GeoTex consists of GeoTex Repre-sentation and GeoTex Prediction, combining geometry-as-texture encoding with history-grounded forecasting. Given L historical occupancy frames O−L+1:0 , Geo-Tex forecasts T future semantic occupancy frames bO1: T .Each frame is Ot ∈ { 0, . . . , K − 1}H×W ×D, where 

H, W, D are the grid dimensions and K is the num-ber of labels. GeoTex Representation jointly organizes height patterns and cross-column relations, mapping each scene through the encoder to a continuous latent state 

Zt = Enc( Ot) ∈ Rh×w×c, where h, w are the latent planar resolutions and c is the channel dimension. In this latent space, the prediction module F combines ego-motion conditions, fixed relational memory built from initial observations, and recent sliding history to autore-gressively generate future states bZ1: T . The decoder Dec 

then reconstructs future occupancy from these states. Training first learns GeoTex Representation through occupancy reconstruction to preserve geometric texture during compression and reconstruction; its encoder and decoder are detailed in Sec. 3.2. We then freeze the repre-sentation module and train GeoTex Prediction to model scene evolution conditioned on history in latent space. Finally, we freeze the encoder and prediction module and adapt only the decoder to improve semantic decoding of predicted states. The prediction module and decoder adaptation are described in Sec. 3.3. 

3.2 GeoTex Representation 

Semantic occupancy explicitly describes 3D positions and spatial occupancy, but abstracts continuous appearance into discrete classes. Voxels with the same semantic label may play different structural roles, such as the interior, outer contour, and top termination of a tree canopy. These roles are revealed by voxel relations: vertical continua-3tion, gaps, and termination characterize within-column shape, while changes and connections between neighbor-ing columns describe surfaces, boundaries, and spatial extent. We refer to the discriminative cues jointly formed by these height patterns and their horizontal organization as geometric texture .To preserve these geometric relations in a compact representation, GeoTex Representation organizes 3D geo-metric features before height compression within a con-tinuous VAE framework. 3.3 GeoTex Prediction 

In this continuous latent space, GeoTex Prediction learns how scene states evolve over time. History-grounded fore-casting requires both a persistent reference to observed structure and complementary information about recent evolution. We therefore organize initial observations into fixed relational memory M and extract target-referenced residual updates from the sliding history Ht. At autore-gressive step t, the prediction module combines these two sources with ego-motion conditions to generate the next latent state:

We present GeoTex, an occupancy world model combin-ing geometry-as-texture encoding with history-grounded 4D occupancy forecasting. GeoTex Representation jointly organizes height patterns and cross-column relations as geometric texture during compression and reconstruction. This preserves local shape distinctions within semantic classes. GeoTex Prediction retains initial observation evi-dence in fixed relational memory and updates future states through target-referenced historical residuals. Decoder-only adaptation then improves the decoding of these states into 3D semantic occupancy. Experiments demonstrate improved scene geometry and state-of-the-art forecasting with a compact model, and the gains increase at longer horizons.

## F Dynamic Future Ambiguity and Evalua-tion Limitations 

Figure A4 shows a divergence between GeoTex and GT in the evolution of a surrounding vehicle. The yellow vehicle continues along the road in GT, whereas GeoTex predicts a gradual turn toward a side road. GeoTex Predic-tion updates scene content using historical evidence after geometric alignment, so object evolution is not restricted to rigid propagation. In this scene, the road branch pro-vides spatial scope for an alternative maneuver. Limited historical observations may not uniquely determine a ve-hicle’s future intent, so predictions that differ from the observed trajectory also need to be assessed against scene and motion constraints. This example illustrates a limitation of evaluation 15 against a single observed future. Both mIoU and IoU mea-sure voxel overlap with GT. For relatively stable structures such as roads and buildings, they directly reflect geomet-ric and semantic agreement. Dynamic objects, however, may admit several plausible future behaviors. Even a tra-jectory that follows the road layout and maintains motion continuity is penalized when its position or orientation dif-fers from GT. These metrics therefore measure fidelity to the observed future, but cannot independently distinguish implausible motion from a plausible alternative. Our future work will explore evaluation protocols that account for both prediction accuracy and generative plau-sibility. Alongside voxel-level geometry and semantics, these protocols should consider motion continuity, road constraints, interaction consistency, and coverage of mul-tiple possible futures, providing a more complete assess-ment of scene evolution in occupancy world models. 16

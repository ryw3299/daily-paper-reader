## Abstract 

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models introduce language reasoning into end-to-end autonomous driving. They out-put a language decision together with a trajectory, which makes the planner more interpretable. However, in exist-ing methods the language decision is either an independent output or an index that selects among similar candidate tra-jectories, and its annotations are not verified against the trajectory. As a result, the language decision and the trajec-tory are often inconsistent in closed-loop driving. To address

this, we propose Commit2Drive, a framework that imposes the language decision as a consistency constraint on trajec-tory planning. The decision is a commitment, a maneuver class with a continuous parameter that one deterministic rule reads from any trajectory, and it is grounded in facts reported by the perception of the model itself. A Maneu-ver Bank maps the commitment to a code with a kinematic meaning, and consistency losses train the planner to execute every admissible decision of a scene, so that the trajectory follows the decision. The code is a lookup that involves no hidden state of the language model, so Com-mit2Drive is extensible to rule-based decisions, which the same planner executes without retraining. Commit2Drive uses a 0.5B language model and runs closed-loop inference online on a single consumer graphics card. On Bench2Drive, Commit2Drive reaches a Driving Score (DS) of 78.50 and a Success Rate (SR) of 55.91% with a lightweight model, which is competitive with the state of the art, and greatly reduces the language-action inconsistency. ## 1. Introduction 

Autonomous driving requires a model to understand com-plex traffic scenes and to plan safe trajectories. Traditional End-to-End (E2E) methods [3, 15, 23, 24] integrate percep-tion, prediction and planning into one differentiable network and map sensor inputs directly to trajectories. These meth-ods have made great progress, but they work as black boxes and give no explanation for the trajectory they output. With the rapid progress of Vision-Language Models (VLMs) in visual understanding and reasoning, recent work extends them to Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models for driv-ing [9, 10, 18, 39, 46, 64]. A VLA model outputs chain-of-thought reasoning or a language decision together with the trajectory. This paradigm improves generalization with the world knowledge of the VLM, and it lets human drivers understand the decisions of the model. Most current VLA models produce the two outputs in parallel (Fig. 1c). OmniDrive [48] lets the language head output both the language decision and the trajectory as text [18, 35, 46, 55]. Text tokens are discrete and carry no notion of numerical distance, so waypoints generated token by token are not precise enough [9]. ORION [9] improves on this design and predicts the waypoints with a separate nu-merical planner [32, 39, 50]. Combined with Reinforcement Learning (RL) post-training on the trajectory [10, 32], this design alleviates the above problem to some extent. How-ever, neither design constrains the two outputs, which are often inconsistent [34, 37, 59]. On Bench2Drive [22], the decision stated by MindDrive contradicts its own planned trajectory on the vast majority of the frames (Fig. 1a and Tab. 2). This is because the language annotations of the training data are not checked against the expert trajectory, and no explicit constraint links the decision to the trajec-tory. The trajectory is usually the more accurate of the two (Sec. 4.2), because subsequent training, including RL post-training, mainly rewards the trajectory and neglects the language decision. As a result, the language decision becomes redundant, and the interpretability that the VLA paradigm promises is greatly limited. Recent methods such as MindDrive [10] and DriveMA [62] attempt to solve this problem, but they align the two outputs only in form and do not effectively constrain their consistency. The decision token of MindDrive selects among candidate trajectories that fall into the same maneuver class on most frames, so it is an index without a meaning (Fig. 1b). DriveMA considers the future motion of the expert only in the labels of the language decision and does not resolve the contradictions inside the chain-of-thought reasoning. A data pipeline that enforces the consistency of the whole annotation is still missing. To address this challenge, we propose Commit2Drive, a VLA framework that imposes the language decision as a consistency constraint on trajectory planning (Fig. 1c). Specifically, the language decision is stated as a commitment ,which consists of a maneuver class, such as slow down, and a target value, such as the speed to reach. Facts reported by the perception of the model rule out the decisions that the scene does not allow. A Maneuver Bank then maps the commitment to a code with a kinematic meaning, and the planner generates the trajectory conditioned on this code. To make the planner follow the decision, we design consis-tency losses that supervise every admissible decision of a scene, with the expert trajectory for the decision of the ex-pert and a counterfactual execution for each alternative. To provide the supervision, we build an automatic data pipeline in which rule-based functions and a VLM generate, review and refine the language annotations. In addition, the map-ping takes only a decision as input, so the framework is extensible to mature rule-based decision modules, which can be plugged in without modification. Extensive experi-ments on the closed-loop Bench2Drive benchmark show that Commit2Drive, with a lightweight 0.5B language model, is competitive with the state of the art, reaching a Driving Score (DS) of 78.50 and a Success Rate (SR) of 55.91%, and reduces the inconsistency between the language decision and the planned trajectory to 1.8%. Our main contributions are summarized as follows: • We propose Commit2Drive, a language-constrained plan-ning framework for VLA autonomous driving models. By introducing a Maneuver Bank and consistency losses, Commit2Drive turns the language decision from a mode in-dex into a semantic constraint that the trajectory is trained to satisfy, substantially improving the consistency between language decisions and planned trajectories. • We introduce an automatic data pipeline that combines rule-based functions with a VLM to generate, review and refine language annotations, reducing their reasoning con-tradictions. To the best of our knowledge, it is the first to ground counterfactual reasoning in the facts of visual perception and to train the language decision and the tra-jectory on it together, so that the two outputs of the model stay consistent. 2• Extensive experiments demonstrate the effectiveness of Commit2Drive, achieving 78.50 DS and 55.91% SR on the Bench2Drive benchmark with a lightweight 0.5B language model. The lookup of the Maneuver Bank also decouples the language decision from the hidden states of the lan-guage model, which makes the framework extensible to rule-based decisions. 

## 2. Related Work 

We review E2E driving and language-action consistency. 

2.1. End-to-End Autonomous Driving 

E2E autonomous driving learns perception, prediction and planning in one differentiable network that maps sensor in-puts to trajectories [3, 13, 15, 24, 51]. UniAD [15] and VAD [24] unify several tasks on bird’s-eye-view represen-tations, and GenAD [61] and DiffAD [49] model planning as a generative problem. However, these models lack the reasoning ability and common sense that complex scenar-ios require, so recent methods build driving models on VLMs [30, 33, 43, 63]. DriveGPT4 [55], EMMA [18] and OmniDrive [48] output control signals or trajectories as text. Senna [25] and DriveVLM [46] pair a VLM with a separate planner, and ORION [9] links the VLM to a generative plan-ner with a planning token. AutoVLA [64], ReCogDrive [32] and AlphaDrive [26] are further optimized with RL. Mind-Drive [10] uses a Decision Expert and an Action Expert that share one language model, and optimizes its language decisions with trajectory-level rewards in online RL. 

2.2. Language-Action Consistency 

The explanation of a model need not reflect the computation behind its prediction [19, 29, 47]. In driving, prior works align textual explanations or reasoning with the predicted actions [17, 27, 28]. SimLingo [39] trains the planner on simulated alternative futures to follow language instructions. Alpamayo-R1 [50] rewards the consistency between reason-ing and action in RL post-training, and CF-VLA [38] revises its meta-actions by counterfactual reflection. DriveMA [62] labels meta-actions from the future motion of the expert, generates the meta-action before the trajectory and rewards their agreement. Audits find the reasoning of driving VLMs and VLA models often ungrounded or weakly coupled to the trajectory [34, 37, 53], and run-time monitors flag or reject such mismatches [52, 59]. These methods measure or en-courage consistency through data, losses and rewards, while guaranteed constraints rely mainly on rule-based executors and shields outside VLA planners [1, 5, 14, 40, 41]. 

## 3. Method 

In this section, we present Commit2Drive in detail. As illus-trated in Fig. 2, following MindDrive [10], Commit2Drive comprises a Decision Expert and an Action Expert, which share one VLM and differ only in their Low-Rank Adapta-tion (LoRA) [12] parameters. The Decision Expert serves as the language decision head and states the driving decision, while the Action Expert serves as the trajectory execution head and generates the trajectory. We first formulate tra-jectory planning under a language consistency constraint (Sec. 3.1). We then introduce the consistent language-action mapping, which maps the language decision to a code with a kinematic meaning through a Maneuver Bank and trains the Action Expert with consistency losses to follow it (Sec. 3.2). Next, the counterfactual decision-action data pipeline pairs every admissible decision of a scene with a trajectory, and reviews and refines the language annotations (Sec. 3.3). Finally, the mapping makes Commit2Drive extensible to rule-based decisions, which drive the same Action Expert (Sec. 3.4).

3.2. Consistent Language-Action Mapping 

To satisfy Eq. (3), the language decision and the action must be consistent, and the language decision must constrain the action directly. This requires the language decision to carry semantics that the action can follow. However, in existing methods the decision reaches the action only as an index, which carries no semantics. We therefore introduce a Ma-neuver Bank, which gives the language decision a kinematic meaning and maps it to the action, and consistency losses, which train the Action Expert to follow it. 

Maneuver Bank. The Decision Expert implements the selection policy πd. Its prompt carries a set of perception facts F , short sentences rendered from the detection heads of the model, which define the admissible set DF ⊆ D of the decisions that are safe and legal in the scene. At a special token <commit> , the Decision Expert states the decision as a sentence and outputs it as a commitment c = ( d, θ ), a parameterized action [11, 36], where d ∈ D F is one of four speed decisions, STOP , SLOW , KEEP and ACCEL , and θ is its target value, the stopping distance for STOP and the end speed otherwise. Both forms are supervised by the same label, which is read from the expert trajectory. The Maneuver Bank then maps the commitment to the action. It stores one learnable entry for each decision, and the decision d looks up its entry. The target value θ and the current speed v0 are encoded with Fourier features [45], passed through ψ together with d and added to the entry, which forms the commitment code z. A grounding head decodes z into a nominal speed profile ˆw, and both are fused into the speed branch of the Action Expert, which implements the generation policy πg . 3.3. Counterfactual Decision-Action Data Pipeline 

The mapping above needs supervision that existing datasets do not provide. Their language annotations are not clearly tied to the action. A decision label often does not describe what the expert does next, and the reasoning text can con-tradict the scene or itself. Moreover, counterfactual data has not yet been used to train the language decision and the action together. To fill this gap, we build a counterfactual decision-action data pipeline. As shown in Fig. 3, rule-based functions generate the records of every frame, and a VLM reviews and refines the language annotations. The pipeline runs automatically on the raw logs of Bench2Drive [22]. 

Generate. With one decision and one trajectory per scene, a model can produce both from the scene alone, without the trajectory following the decision. We therefore pair every admissible decision of a scene with a trajectory, and rule-based functions write four records for every frame. (i) 

Decision label : the commitment ( ¯d, ¯θ) is read from the next 3 s of the expert trajectory and rendered as the decision sentence, so the label and the trajectory agree by construction. (ii) Counterfactual executions : for every other decision k,the path of the expert is re-timed with a speed profile of decision k, which gives a trajectory ¯ak with Φ(¯ ak) = k. It is rolled out against the logged motion of the other agents on an occupancy grid [13] and marked infeasible if it collides. (iii) Admissible set : DF keeps the decisions whose execution is feasible and that no traffic rule forbids. KEEP and ACCEL 

are forbidden when the stop line of a red light or of a stop sign is within reach. (iv) Perception facts : the fact sentences, rendered from the logged scene by the templates that are used at inference. With these records, the admissible set tells the Decision Expert which decisions are admissible, and the counterfactual executions tell the Action Expert what each of them looks like (Eq. (5)). 

Review and refine. A language annotation is a short chain of reasoning, and its contradictions are also learned by the model. A VLM [2] therefore takes two roles. As a blind reviewer, it judges whether a decision sentence contradicts the scene, whether a fact sentence invents or omits an object, and whether an alternative decision is safe and legal. As an editor, it revises reasoning text that contradicts itself. Errors that originate in a rule or a template are corrected at their source, and the records are regenerated and reviewed again. Finally, human experts check a random sample of the records. The resulting dataset covers the base set of Bench2Drive (Sec. C of the supplementary material). 

3.4. Extensibility to Rule-Based Decisions 

The mapping of Sec. 3.2 connects a coarse-grained decision to a fine-grained action. The Action Expert reads the deci-sion only through the code of Eq. (4), which involves no hidden state of the language model. The language decision and the action are therefore separable, and the Action Ex-pert does not depend on the source of the decision. Mature rule-based decision modules, such as traffic-rule logic, safety rules [41] and rule-based planners [6], already decide at this granularity. ## 4. Experiments 

We evaluate Commit2Drive and ablate its components. 

4.1. Experimental Settings 

We detail the dataset, metrics, model and training. 

Dataset. We train and evaluate Commit2Drive on Bench2Drive [22], a closed-loop benchmark for end-to-end autonomous driving based on the CARLA simulator [7]. We adopt the official base set of 1000 clips, with 950 for training and 50 for open-loop validation. The clips are annotated by our data pipeline (Sec. 3.3), which yields 234,769 training and 12,806 validation frames. Closed-loop evaluation uses the official 220 short routes across 44 interactive scenarios. 

Metrics. For closed-loop evaluation, we adopt DS, SR and Multi-Ability. Following Bench2Drive [22], SR is the pro-portion of routes completed within the time limit, DS is the route completion penalized by traffic infractions, and Multi-Ability evaluates five advanced urban driving skills. Model Setting. We set the number of speed waypoints to 

Ns = 6 , a future 3 s trajectory sampled at 2 Hz. The decision space of the Decision Expert includes 4 speed decisions, each with a continuous target value. Φ reads a trajectory as STOP 

if its end speed is below 0.5 m/s, as SLOW or ACCEL if its speed changes by more than 1 m/s, and as KEEP otherwise. The Maneuver Bank has 4 entries of dimension 64 and uses Fourier features with 4 frequencies, and its grounding head decodes Ns speed values. The fusion MLP has a hidden size of 512 and a zero-initialized output layer. In the consistency losses, we set m = 0 .3 m/s and λp = 3 .

Training Process. All models are trained on 4 NVIDIA A100 graphics cards. We employ the pretrained EVA-02-L [8] as the vision encoder and the lightweight Qwen2-0.5B [56] as the base Large Language Model (LLM). Com-mit2Drive is trained in two stages, Imitation Learning (IL) and online RL, both following MindDrive [10]. Only the Action Expert is initialized from the released checkpoint of MindDrive, which gives the trajectory generation a prior. This Action Expert has been trained by IL alone, as the on-line RL of MindDrive updates only its Decision Expert and value network. In the IL stage, the Decision Expert is fine-tuned with LoRA to state the decision, and the Maneuver Bank, the fusion MLP and the speed branch are then trained with Lcons , while all other modules are frozen. Closed-loop evaluation runs online on a single RTX 4090, and every measured result is the median over multiple random seeds. 

4.2. Main Results 

We compare Commit2Drive with representative methods of the traditional E2E paradigm and the VLA paradigm on Bench2Drive. Tab. 1 lists the results, and we find that: 

1) Commit2Drive delivers competitive closed-loop and open-loop performance with a lightweight model. With the Qwen2-0.5B language model, Commit2Drive achieves 76.50 DS and 50.00% SR after IL, and 78.50 DS and 55.91% SR after online RL. Among the VLA models with the same lightweight LLM, it surpasses ORION [9] (72.89 DS, 45.83% SR) and is comparable to MindDrive [10], which reports 75.85 DS after IL and 78.04 DS after online RL. Sim-Lingo [39] reaches 85.07 DS with a model of similar size, but it is trained on its own dataset and not on the base set. Re-produced on our RTX 4090 ( ‡ in Tab. 1), MindDrive reaches 73.24 DS and 46.59% SR, and Commit2Drive improves over it by 5.26 DS and 9.32% SR. In Multi-Ability, it achieves a mean of 59.48%. In open loop, Commit2Drive obtains an average L2 error of 0.685 m after IL, close to MindDrive (0.69 m) and ORION with the same LLM (0.67 m), and 0.60 m after online RL. 

2) The language decision of Commit2Drive is consis-tent with its trajectory. Tab. 2 compares the say-do gap of Eq. (8). MindDrive and ORION are evaluated from their released checkpoints, and their stated decisions are mapped to D (Sec. C of the supplementary material). The decision stated by MindDrive differs from its planned trajec-tory on 83.5% of the frames and from the executed trajectory on 69.6%. On the open-loop validation frames, where the expert is available, its say-do gap is 66.4%, and the lan-guage decision is wrong in 71% of these conflicts and the trajectory in 13%. In contrast, Commit2Drive reduces the say-do gap to 1.8% in closed loop on Dev10, a ten-route subset of Bench2Drive, and to 0.4% on the open-loop val-idation frames. With the decision forced to another value, the trajectory of Commit2Drive follows it on at least 99% of the frames, against 27% to 30% for MindDrive (Fig. 1b), which shows that the decision determines the trajectory. The say-exec gap of Commit2Drive is 17.2 points below that of MindDrive but remains 52.4%. 4.3. Qualitative Results 

Fig. 4 compares MindDrive and Commit2Drive on a pedestrian-crossing scenario of the Bench2Drive closed-loop evaluation. MindDrive states that it maintains a slow or mod-erate speed in all five frames, while its speed waypoints accelerate, and it hits the pedestrian 0.4 s after the last frame. We further examine the semantics of the decision on a subset of 3,434 validation frames (Fig. 1b). The seven candidate trajectories behind the speed tokens of MindDrive are nearly identical. On 86% of the frames they fall into the same deci-sion, two candidates end only 0.51 m apart on average, and the output follows its token on fewer than one third of them. In Commit2Drive, the four decisions of a scene yield distinct trajectories, 8.38 m apart on average, and the output follows the stated decision on at least 99% of the frames.

Extensibility to rule-based decisions. We test the extensi-bility of Sec. 3.4 by plugging two rule-based functions into Commit2Drive without retraining. A traffic-light rule de-cides STOP or SLOW at a red light that governs the ego lane, and a collision-avoidance rule decides STOP when a hazard is within the time-to-collision threshold. A triggered rule issues the decision in place of the Decision Expert (Eq. (7)), and the Action Expert is unchanged. On Dev10, the DS changes from 53.09 to 51.57 with the rules active. In Tab. 4, a rule-issued decision takes 5.4 ms instead of 35.5 ms, and perception accounts for most of the latency of every model. 

## 5. Conclusion 

In this paper, we introduce Commit2Drive, a VLA frame-work that imposes the language decision as a consistency constraint on trajectory planning. A Maneuver Bank maps the language decision to a code with a kinematic mean-ing, and consistency losses train the Action Expert to fol-low it. An automatic data pipeline, in which rule-based functions and a VLM generate, review and refine the an-notations, supplies the supervision. Extensive experiments on Bench2Drive demonstrate that Commit2Drive, with a lightweight 0.5B language model, is competitive with the state of the art and greatly reduces the inconsistency be-tween the language decision and the planned trajectory. The framework is also extensible to existing rule-based meth-ods, whose decisions drive the same Action Expert without retraining. 

Limitations and Future Work. This work addresses only the say-do gap inside the model, and its driving performance exceeds the baseline by a small margin. The say-exec gap, which lies between the model and the physical execution of 8its plan, remains unsolved and is left to future work. We hope that it brings new inspiration to the autonomous driving community on the consistency between language decisions and trajectories.

## B. Questions 

Q1. What are the differences between Commit2Drive and MindDrive? 

Commit2Drive adopts the architecture of MindDrive [10], in which a Decision Expert and an Action Expert share one VLM, and initializes its Action Expert from the released MindDrive checkpoint (Sec. D.2). In MindDrive, the deci-sion is a speed token that selects one of several candidate trajectories (Eq. (2)). The token is an index, and no term requires the selected candidate to follow it. The candidates of MindDrive are nearly identical on most frames, and its output follows a forced token on a minority of the frames (Sec. E.4). In Commit2Drive, the decision is a commitment 

(d, θ ) that the deterministic rule Φ reads from any trajectory. The Maneuver Bank maps the commitment to a code with a kinematic meaning, and the consistency losses supervise every admissible decision of a scene with a trajectory that realizes it. The decisions of a scene then yield distinct tra-jectories, and the trajectory follows a forced decision on nearly all frames (Tab. A11). The supervision also differs: the decision labels of Commit2Drive are read from the future motion of the expert, so the label and the trajectory agree by construction, and the records are reviewed (Sec. C). 

Q2. Is the consistency between the decision and the trajec-tory guaranteed at inference? 

It is learned and not guaranteed. We relax the indicator of Eq. (3) into the consistency losses of Eq. (5) and apply no projection or rejection at inference. We therefore measure the consistency in two ways: with the say-do gap in closed loop and in open loop (Tab. 2 and Sec. E.2) and with interven-tions that force the decision to another value (Sec. E.4). In both, the trajectory departs from the decision on few frames. Since Φ is a deterministic rule on the planned trajectory, a remaining violation can be detected at run time. We do not use such a check in any reported result. 

Q3. Why is conditioning the Action Expert on the decision not sufficient? 

On expert data, every scene comes with one decision, and this decision is a function of the scene. A planner can then reproduce the expert from its hidden state ha alone, and the imitation loss does not constrain its output under any other decision. We state this formally in Sec. D.3. This agrees with what we observe for MindDrive, whose output follows a forced speed token on a minority of the frames (Fig. 1b). The counterfactual executions add every admissible decision of a scene to the training data, each with its own target, so only the decision tells the targets apart. Without them, the say-do gap rises (Tab. 3), the trajectories of different decisions move closer together, and the trajectory follows a forced decision far less often (Tab. A10). 

Q4. How does the counterfactual data differ from the Action Dreaming of SimLingo? 

SimLingo [39] simulates alternative futures to train the planner to follow external language instructions. These fu-tures are paired with instructions, and not with the deci-sion that the model itself states in its commentary. In our interventions on its released checkpoint, the trajectory fol-lows an explicit instruction on nearly all frames, but it fol-lows the commentary far less often when the commentary is forced to another decision (Tab. A12). In Commit2Drive, the counterfactual executions are paired with the decision of the model itself. The same records supervise the Decision Expert, through the admissible set, and the Action Expert, through the counterfactual executions. The admissible set is computed from the logged scene, so the alternatives are limited to the decisions that pass its collision check and its traffic rules (Sec. C.1). 

Q5. Why does the say-exec gap remain large when the say-do gap is small? 

The two gaps compare the stated decision with different trajectories: the say-do gap with the trajectory that is planned at the same step, which the consistency constraint covers, and the say-exec gap with the trajectory that the vehicle executes afterward. Since the model re-plans at every step, the executed trajectory results from many consecutive plans, and a decision that is stated at one step can be replaced at the next. The planned and the executed trajectory also differ for the baselines, as their do-exec gap shows (Tab. A13). Commit2Drive lowers the say-exec gap, but a large part of it remains (Tab. 2). This remainder concerns the consistency 12 of the decisions of consecutive steps, which the per-step constraint of Eq. (3) does not cover, and we leave it to future work. 

Q6. Why does the decision space contain only speed deci-sions? 

The Action Expert has a path branch and a speed branch (Fig. 2). In the annotations of MindDrive [10], the model on which Commit2Drive is built, the path token is the nav-igation command of Bench2Drive, so the decision that the model makes by itself is the speed decision. The released checkpoint confirms this: its path token equals the naviga-tion command on the validation frames, and its open-loop error comes almost entirely from the speed along the path (Sec. D.1). We therefore keep the path branch frozen and constrain the speed decision. The formulation does not de-pend on this choice: any decision that a deterministic rule can read from a trajectory can be stated as a commitment. We leave path decisions to future work. 

Q7. What is the role of the VLM in the data pipeline? 

The VLM does not produce the labels. Rule-based func-tions generate all four records of a frame from the raw log. The decision label is read from the future motion of the ex-pert, and the counterfactual executions, the admissible set and the perception facts are computed from the logged scene (Sec. C.1). The VLM [2] then acts in two roles, as a blind reviewer that judges the records without knowing their origin or the verdict of the rule-based functions, and as an editor that revises reasoning text. When the reviewer disagrees because of an error that originates in a rule or a template, we correct it at its source and regenerate the records, so the correction applies to every frame. In the example of Fig. 3, the reviewer judges that keeping the current speed toward a red light is illegal, and the rule that builds the admissible set is corrected to forbid KEEP when the stop line is within reach (Eq. (A5)). Fig. A5 shows a record of this kind before and after the correction.

The reviewer rejected KEEP when a red light was a short distance ahead, and it rejected decisions that take the vehicle past a stop sign. The rule on the reach of a red light and the stop-sign rule were added, which changed the admissible set of 3.4% of the labeled training frames and of 2.7% of the labeled validation frames. Finally, human experts check a random sample of the records. 

C.2. Dataset Statistics 

The resulting dataset covers the base set of Bench2Drive, with 950 clips and 234,769 frames for training and 50 clips and 12,806 frames for open-loop validation (Tab. A3). A label exists for 196,769 training frames and 10,806 valida-tion frames. Every labeled frame carries four records: the decision label, the counterfactual executions of the other de-cisions, the admissible set and the perception facts. STOP is the most frequent decision, with 37.5% of the training labels, because the vehicle is at rest in many frames. ACCEL follows with 29.1%, KEEP with 20.7% and SLOW with 12.7%. For the training frames, 422,141 counterfactual executions are generated, of which 334,315 are admissible. The labeled training frames have 2.67 admissible decisions on average, and 77.5% of them have at least two, so that the Action Ex-pert sees most scenes under more than one decision. A red light governs the ego lane on 18.3% of the labeled training frames, and a traffic rule forbids the decision of the expert on 2.4%. D.2. Training Details 

Commit2Drive is trained in two stages, IL and online RL, both following MindDrive [10]. The IL stage consists of decision learning and mapping learning. 

Decision learning. The Action Expert is initialized from the released checkpoint of MindDrive, in which it has been trained by IL alone. The vocabulary is extended by 62 new tokens: <commit> , the four decision tokens and the 57 value tokens of Tab. A1. In decision learning, the LoRA parameters of the Decision Expert, the commitment head and the embeddings of the new tokens are trained, and all other parameters are frozen, so ha and the path are unchanged. The Decision Expert is trained with three losses on the labeled frames. The language modeling loss supervises the decision sentence. The commitment loss supervises the scores q =softmax( W LN( hc) + b) of the commitment head, which implement the selection policy πd, with the label ¯d and with the admissible set:

If the decision of the expert is not admissible, because the expert keeps its speed or accelerates where a traffic rule forbids it, the label is replaced by STOP with the target value of its counterfactual execution. This affects 2.4% of the labeled training frames. If no feasible STOP exists either, only the second term of Eq. (A9) is applied. 

Mapping learning. The Maneuver Bank, the fusion MLP and the speed branch are then trained with the consistency loss Lcons of Eq. (6), while all other modules are frozen. The VLM is among the frozen modules, so the hidden states 

ha are computed once and cached. The speed branch of the reported results is trained on every third training clip, 317 clips with 78,078 frames, of which 65,398 carry a label, while the Decision Expert is trained on frames of all training clips. Alg. A2 gives one training step. For every admissible decision k of a frame, the commitment (k, θ k) is read from the target ¯ak, which is the expert trajectory for the decision of the expert and a counterfactual execution otherwise, with one exception that is given below. One frame contributes up to four targets, 2.7 on average, which differ only in the decision. The Maneuver Bank is fitted alone beforehand, with Lground and Lsem on the 618,896 valid executions of the labeled training frames, expert and counterfactual. The target value that the Maneuver Bank receives is perturbed by Gaussian noise with a standard deviation of 1.5 m for STOP 

and of 2.0, 0.5 and 1.0 m/s for SLOW , KEEP and ACCEL ,and clipped to the range of its decision. This imitates the error of the Decision Expert, and Lground uses the value without noise. The exception concerns the trajectory that the pretrained speed branch generates without a commitment. If Φ reads this trajectory as a decision other than that of the expert, it replaces the counterfactual execution of that decision as the target, and the pair is left out of Lground and 

Lsem . The trajectory term of Lexec is the L1 error of the positions plus half the L1 error of the displacements between consecutive waypoints. 

Online RL. The second stage follows the online RL of MindDrive [10]: rollouts are collected in CARLA [7], and the Decision Expert is updated by proximal policy optimiza-tion (PPO) on the collected steps. The RL action is the decision of the commitment. It is sampled from the scores of the commitment head over DF , its target value is decoded as in Eq. (A6), and the frozen Action Expert executes it, so the stated decision is also the one that the Action Expert executes during exploration. The reward is that of MindDrive, −1 for a penalty event, +1 for a completed route and 0 otherwise. We add a progress reward that is paid in proportion to the route completion and amounts to 1 for a completed route. A penalty event ends the episode. The seven penalty events are a collision with a pedestrian, with a vehicle or with a static object; a traffic-light or a stop-sign infraction; a deviation from the route; and leaving the lanes of the route. As in MindDrive, PPO updates only the LoRA parameters of the Decision Expert and the output layer of the value network, which we initialize to zero. 19 D.3. E.3. More Ablation Studies 

Tab. 3 removes the Maneuver Bank, the counterfactual data or the consistency losses as a whole. Tab. A10 complements it in open loop with measures that Tab. 3 does not report: the collision rate, the distance between the trajectories of two decisions and the follow rate. Every variant retrains the Maneuver Bank, the fusion MLP and, unless it is frozen, the speed branch on the same cached hidden states (Sec. D.2), 23 and the commitment is either the label of the frame or the output of a Decision Expert. The counterfactual data matters most. With supervision on the decision of the expert only, the trajectories of two decisions end 2.65 m apart instead of 8.39 m, the trajectory follows every forced decision on 32.4% of the frames instead of 99.0%, and the collision rate under a label commitment rises from 0.50% to 1.08%. Removing Lground and Lsem 

leaves these measures almost unchanged, since Lexec still supervises the generated trajectory and requires it to be read back as the decision. The two losses supervise the code of the Maneuver Bank, which these measures do not capture. Without them, the grounding head receives no supervision, and the nominal speed profile ˆw that it decodes from the code no longer matches the decision. On the training frames, it deviates from the speed profile of its target by 4.77 m/s on average, compared with 0.22 m/s with the two losses. Freezing the speed branch raises the collision rate under a label commitment from 0.50% to 0.60%. 

E.4. Decision Interventions 

MindDrive and Commit2Drive. Tab. A11 gives the follow rate of Eq. (A12) for every decision. A decision is evaluated on the frames on which an execution of it exists, between 2,509 and 3,429 of the 3,434 frames of the subset. On the validation subset, the trajectory of MindDrive follows a forced speed token on 27% to 30% of the frames. Its seven candidate trajectories fall into one decision on 86% of the frames, and the candidates of two speed tokens that name different decisions end only 0.51 m apart on average. The token is an index without a kinematic meaning. The trajectory of Commit2Drive follows a forced decision on at least 99% of the frames for each of the four decisions, and the trajectories of two decisions end 8.38 m apart on average, close to the 8.49 m of the executions that supervise them. On the full validation set (10,806 labeled frames), the trajectory of MindDrive follows a forced speed token on 25% to 36% of the frames, and that of Commit2Drive again on at least 99%. 

SimLingo. Tab. A12 lists the interventions on SimLingo [39]. Forcing the commentary to a decision moves the trajectory into this decision on 32% of the frames on av-erage, whereas an explicit instruction, the task that Action Dreaming trains, succeeds on 94% to 100%. The trajectory therefore depends much less on the decision that the model itself states than on an external instruction. The official agent of SimLingo keeps the dropout of its adapters active at inference, and we run the interventions with and without it. 

E.5. Consistency across Scene Types 

Frames are assigned to scene types from the privileged sim-ulator state and the scenario of the route. Model outputs are not used, so the scene types are the same for all models. A frame belongs to C1 if the route turns by more than 15 ◦

within the distance that the ego vehicle covers in 3 s, at least 15 m; C2 if a pedestrian or cyclist is within 20 m or inside the ego corridor; C3 if a vehicle has a time to collision below 3 s or cuts into the corridor; C4 if the ego vehicle is slower than 1 m/s, a red light or a stop sign of the ego lane is within 30 m, or a stationary vehicle blocks the corridor within 20 m; 

C5 if a static obstacle is inside the corridor; C6 at night or in rain; and C7 if at least three moving agents are within 30 m and a vehicle is in the corridor. A frame also belongs to C1, C2, C3, C5 or C7 if the scenario of its route is of this type. A frame is simple if none of the conditions on the simulator state holds and the ego vehicle drives at more than 3 m/s, and 

complex if it belongs to at least one scene type and is not simple. Tab. A13 gives the gaps and the infraction rate of each scene type. For MindDrive and ORION, the say-exec gap grows from 13.2% and 9.1% on simple frames to 63.3% and 69.1% on complex ones. It peaks at 82.2% and 87.8% on stopped or low-speed frames (C4), the scene type with the highest infraction rate. SimLingo shows the opposite order for the say-exec gap, with 28.1% on simple frames and 15.8% on complex ones, while its say-do gap more than doubles. 

E.6. Perception versus Language 

Commit2Drive grounds the decision in facts that the percep-tion of the model reports. Tab. A14 motivates this design with ORION [9], whose language head can be asked about the facts that its perception heads report. We compare two facts in closed loop, a hazard and a red light, and list the two conditions of the red light separately. A hazard is re-ported when a vehicle or a vulnerable road user, rolled out at constant velocity, meets the front of the ego vehicle within 3 s. A red light is reported when a traffic light governs the ego lane and is red. Each disagreement is checked against the privileged simulator state with a tolerance band, and frames inside the band are not scored. The answers of the language head contradict the perception of the model itself most often for the hazard, on 12.9% of the frames on which this fact is scored and 14.8% of the complex ones, and the language head is wrong in 84% of the scored disagreements. On the open-loop validation frames, the same analysis gives a see-say gap of 0.4% on simple frames and 14.9% on com-plex ones on average over the facts, with the language head wrong in 94% of the scored disagreements.

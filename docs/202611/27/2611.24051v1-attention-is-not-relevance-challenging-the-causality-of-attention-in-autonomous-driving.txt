Markdown Content:
Attention has become one of the most widely adopted solutions for estimating the relevance of surrounding objects and interpreting an autonomous vehicle’s driving decisions. However, its ability to capture the causal impact of individual input elements on an AI model’s output has long been discussed in the literature, particularly in the Natural Language Processing (NLP) domain. This work brings this discussion into the autonomous driving domain by analyzing the accuracy of attention in estimating the impact of an input object on an autonomous vehicle’s driving, i.e., its relevance. To this end, we compare different attention-based relevance estimation methods against a causal reference of object relevance obtained through counterfactual analysis. We perform our analysis on more than 80,000 synthetic driving scenes using an autonomous vehicle based on PlanT 2.0, a state-of-the-art Transformer-based model for object-level planning. Results show that attention provides a correlational rather than causal indication of object relevance. Attention-based relevance estimation methods do not consistently identify all relevant objects and tend to incorrectly classify as relevant objects with little or no impact on the vehicle’s driving. In particular, results show that attention can effectively identify relevant objects in simple single-lane, straight-road scenarios, but its accuracy degrades in more complex settings, such as multi-lane roads and intersections. These findings highlight the limitations of attention as a proxy for object relevance, calling for novel methods able to accurately capture the causal impact of individual objects on the vehicle’s driving and distinguish it from correlational associations. 

Introduction 

Autonomous vehicles operate in complex environments populated by heterogeneous and dynamically interacting objects, including other vehicles, motorcycles, pedestrians, and cyclists. While an autonomous vehicle may detect and track a large number of surrounding objects through its onboard sensors, in practice, only a subset of the detected objects is actually relevant to its driving. In [1] and [2], object relevance is defined from a safety-oriented perspective: an object is considered relevant if its missed detection can have safety-critical implications for the ego-vehicle. Similarly, the authors of [3] link object relevance to collision avoidance and consider an object as relevant if its missed detection can lead to a collision. In other works, object relevance is associated with related concepts such as object importance and object risk. For example, [4] uses object importance to refer to objects that the ego-vehicle should consider to make safe driving decisions, while [5] uses the term more broadly to refer to the objects that are relevant to a human driver’s decisions. Object risk is instead used in [6], where the authors define risk objects as those that cause a human driver to stop or change lane. 

We define the relevance of an object as the impact that the object has on the autonomous vehicle’s driving. This definition is consistent with the eXplainable AI (XAI) literature, where the relevance of an input feature (the object) is generally associated with its impact on the AI model’s output (the vehicle’s driving) [7]. In addition, it is deliberately framed from a broader driving perspective, so that it can be specialized to more specific safety-related definitions like the ones in [1]-[3] if required by a particular downstream application, while being applicable to a variety of use cases. For example, object relevance could be used to focus or prioritize detection and tracking efforts on objects likely to influence the vehicle’s driving, thereby enabling a more efficient allocation of computational resources across the autonomous driving stack. It could also be used to identify the objects that influenced a particular maneuver, making the behavior of an autonomous vehicle easier to inspect and diagnose, enhancing the interpretability of AI-based autonomous driving models. 

Attention: A Proxy for Relevance? 

The attention mechanism is one of the most popular and widely adopted solutions for estimating the relevance of surrounding objects. Attention is a fundamental component of modern AI architectures. It enables a deep neural network to selectively focus on different parts of the input by learning attention weights that quantify the relative contribution of each input feature to the model’s output. Early end-to-end autonomous driving models based on Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) leveraged pixel-level attention maps to highlight regions of the input image containing objects that may have influenced the ego-vehicle’s driving decisions [8]. An object-level approach to visualize attention in CNN-based models was later proposed in [9], where the authors introduced an additional pooling layer to focus the learned attention weights on individual objects rather

While early studies interpreted attention weights as explanations or proxies for the casual impact of individual input elements on a model’s output, studies in the Natural Language Processing (NLP) domain have shown that larger attention weights do not always correlate with greater impact or relevance [11]. In the autonomous driving domain, [8] provided preliminary qualitative evidence challenging the capability of the attention mechanism to identify relevant objects. The authors showed that pixel-level attention maps frequently highlight regions of the input image that do not contain any objects and are, therefore, likely irrelevant to the ego-vehicle’s driving. In contrast, [3] and [4] showed that attention can be an accurate proxy for a safety-oriented definition of object relevance considering an autonomous vehicle based on PlanT. In [3], the authors proposed labelling the object receiving the highest attention from PlanT as relevant. However, the accuracy of the proposed approach was indirectly evaluated by analyzing the collision-avoidance capability of a rule-based expert algorithm that, at each timestep, controls the ego-vehicle using only the object labelled as relevant, rather than by directly measuring the relevant object’s impact on PlanT’s output. In [4], the same approach was evaluated against a human-annotated dataset in which an object is labelled as relevant if the annotator considers it potentially safety-critical to the ego-vehicle’s driving. The existing literature provides mixed evidence on the accuracy of attention as a proxy for object relevance. In addition, it highlights the need for a systematic and direct analysis of attention-based relevance estimation methods against the true impact of each individual object on the output of AI-based models such as PlanT, which cannot be reliably determined from rule-based expert algorithms or human annotations. To address these gaps, this work directly analyses the accuracy of multiple attention-based relevance estimation methods against a causal reference of object relevance determined through counterfactual analysis. 

Counterfactual Analysis: A Causal Reference 

Counterfactual analysis is a fundamental causal learning technique to determine the causal impact of each input feature on a model’s output.  It involves examining how a model’s output changes in response to a controlled modification of one input feature, while keeping all other inputs fixed. This allows the impact of the modified input feature to be isolated from potentially spurious input-output correlations [12], making counterfactual analysis a particularly suitable tool for establishing a causal reference of object relevance against which attention-based methods can be directly evaluated. In autonomous driving, counterfactual analysis has been used, for example, to identify the elements of the driving scene that influence the planned trajectory of an autonomous vehicle [13]. In this work, we perform counterfactual analysis by evaluating an autonomous vehicle’s driving under alternative counterfactual scenarios generated by iteratively removing each individual object from the driving scene. For each removed object, we measure the resulting changes in driving behavior and use them as a causal reference for the object’s impact on the autonomous vehicle’s driving, i.e., its relevance. We perform our analysis in a CARLA-based simulation environment, using an autonomous vehicle controlled by PlanT 2.0 [14], a state-of-the-art, open-source, object-level planning Transformer. CARLA provides the explicit and full control over the driving environment required to remove individual objects while keeping the rest of the driving scene unchanged, enabling the systematic generation of controlled counterfactual scenarios. PlanT 2.0’s object-level input representation enables precise counterfactual interventions on individual objects (i.e., object removal), while its attention weights allow the implementation of the attention-based relevance estimation methods considered in this work.

PlanT 2.0 

PlanT 2.0 is a Transformer-based model for object-level planning with state-of-the-art performance on popular autonomous driving benchmarks such as Bench2Drive and CARLA’s leaderboard 2.0 validation routes at the time of this work. It employs an encoder-only Transformer architecture with 41.4M parameters, 𝐿𝐿 = 8 layers, 𝐻𝐻 = 8 attention heads per layer, and 𝑑𝑑𝑚𝑚𝑚𝑚𝑚𝑚𝑚𝑚𝑚𝑚 = 512 hidden size trained via imitation learning on a dataset of 500k driving scenes collected using the PDM-lite expert driver [14]. PDM-lite is a privileged, rule-based expert planner that uses the kinematic bicycle model to identify potential future collisions and the Intelligent Driver Model (IDM) to compute continuous target velocities based on the distance and relative speed with respect to the object ahead. PDM-lite is widely used to train AI-based autonomous driving models due to its computational efficiency and strong performance on CARLA leaderboard 2.0 scenarios. Further details about PlanT 2.0 training methodology and benchmark performance are available in [14]. 

Fig. 1 illustrates the architecture of PlanT 2.0. As shown in Fig. 1, PlanT 2.0’s input includes multiple types of tokens describing the ego-vehicle’s surrounding environment. The first input token represents a Bird Eye View (BEV) map of the road layout centered on the ego-vehicle and is encoded using a ResNet-18 encoder followed by a linear embedding layer. The BEV token is complemented by two learned embedding tokens representing the speed limit and the ego-vehicle’s route, respectively. The route is defined by 20 points, sampled every 1 meter, that guide the ego-vehicle’s long-term driving behavior towards its goal destination. The remaining input tokens represent surrounding objects. Each object is represented as an oriented 3D bounding box containing its relative position, heading, and class (e.g., vehicle, pedestrian), and is encoded using a class-dependent linear projection layer. PlanT 2.0’s output consists of 8 temporally equidistant waypoint tokens sampled at 4 Hz and a sequence of 20 spatially equidistant path tokens sampled every 1 meter. Methodology 

We analyze attention’s accuracy as a proxy for object relevance using synthetic driving scenes generated through custom CARLA-SUMO co-simulations. A driving scene consists of a snapshot (or frame) taken from a CARLA simulation including the ego-vehicle, controlled by PlanT, and surrounding objects whose mobility is controlled by SUMO. In each scene, we first identify the relevant objects that influence the ego-vehicle’s driving by analyzing their relevance, determined through counterfactual analysis. We then assess the accuracy of attention-based relevance estimation methods by evaluating their ability to correctly classify these objects as relevant. In each driving scene, our analysis considers all PlanT input objects, i.e., all objects within the ego-vehicle’s perception range. As in PlanT’s original work [14], the perception range is defined by a 50 m-radius circular region behind the ego-vehicle and a 50 m-wide, 100 m-long elliptical region stretching in front of it.

In each driving scene, we use the attention scores to identify the objects estimated to be relevant. For each attention-based relevance estimation method, we rank the input objects in decreasing order of their attention score 𝐴𝐴 𝑚𝑚 , and compute the ratio between each object’s attention score and that of the next ranked object. Then, we identify the first ranked object for which this ratio exceeds a pre-defined threshold 𝛽𝛽 . This object, and all higher-ranked ones, are classified as relevant. Intuitively, this approach assumes that relevant objects will receive higher attention scores than irrelevant ones from PlanT, and that this difference manifests as a drop-off in their attention scores. Unlike existing methods that always classify the highest ranked object as relevant [3], our approach is more flexible: it can identify a single relevant object when the attention score drops after the first ranked object, or multiple relevant objects when the attention score remains high across a group of objects before dropping. In addition, it doesn’t necessarily classify an object as relevant when all objects have similar attention scores.

In contrast, relevance assigns negligible values to irrelevant objects by design and provides a more discriminative and interpretable measure of the impact that each input object has on PlanT’s output and, ultimately, the ego-vehicle’s driving.

However, strongly focusing on the object ahead can reduce precision, since object relevance depends not only on the relative position between the ego-vehicle and the object but also on other factors, such as their relative speed. Indeed, precision is only 27.9% in single-lane straight scenarios when 𝑀𝑀 = 1 .

This tendency to focus on the object ahead becomes less effective in more complex scenarios where the ego-vehicle’s driving can be influenced by richer interactions with multiple surrounding objects. In multi-lane straight scenarios, recall decreases to 90.5% for 𝑁𝑁 = 1 and further drops to 48.6% for 

𝑁𝑁 = 2 , highlighting the limited ability of attention-based methods to identify multiple relevant objects in the same scene. Fig. 4(a) illustrates this limitation with an example in which attention identifies only one of the two relevant objects in front of the ego-vehicle. In the figure, the ego-vehicle is highlighted by a red circle, relevant objects by light blue dashed boxes, and the object classified as relevant by the Sum method by a green box. Arrows indicate the direction of the ego-vehicle’s planned path and the objects’ trajectories, which are not used by PlanT as input. In Fig. 4(a), both objects ahead of the ego-vehicle are relevant since they are moving at lower speed and blocking both lanes, forcing the ego-vehicle to decelerate. However, attention correctly classifies as relevant only the object in the ego-vehicle’s current lane.

Attention’s limited ability to identify relevant objects in complex settings is further highlighted by the analysis of intersection scenarios. Fig. 4(b) shows a representative example in which attention focuses on the irrelevant object directly ahead of the ego-vehicle (in the green box), which is leaving the intersection, while failing to classify as relevant the potentially safety-critical object (in the light blue box) crossing the ego-vehicle’s path. In both single- and multi-lane intersection scenarios, recall drops to approximately 85% when 𝑁𝑁 = 1 , and decreases further in scenarios with two relevant objects ( 𝑁𝑁 = 2 ). 

The similar performance in single- and multi-lane intersection scenarios can be explained by the fact that they are approximately equivalent from the ego-vehicle’s driving perspective, since the ego-vehicle is allowed to cross multi-lane intersections only on the innermost lane. Table 1 also shows that precision is generally higher in intersections compared to straight scenarios. This can be explained by the richer interactions and greater number of potential conflicts at intersections, which increases the likelihood that an object classified as relevant is actually affecting PlanT’s output and, hence, the ego-vehicle’s driving. 

Conclusions 

This work analyzed attention’s accuracy in estimating the relevance of surrounding objects in autonomous driving. To this end, we analyzed more than 80k synthetic driving scenes with 

Ego  Attention  Relevance an autonomous vehicle based on PlanT 2.0, a state-of-the-art, object-level planning Transformer. Our analysis shows that attention is a correlational rather than causal measure of object relevance, corroborating preliminary evidence from the NLP domain. In particular, the obtained results show that attention tends to rely on learned correlations stemming from the autonomous vehicle’s training methodology, rather than 

accurately capturing each object’s causal impact on the vehicle’s driving . While attention-based relevance estimation methods can identify relevant objects in simple straight-road scenarios, they do not generalize to more complex scenarios with multiple lanes per driving direction and richer interactions, such as intersections. Furthermore, they tend to incorrectly classify as relevant objects with little or no impact on the vehicle’s driving. Results also show that attention-based relevance estimation methods are not able to strongly discriminate between relevant and irrelevant objects, and exhibit an inherent tradeoff between sensitivity (recall) and selectivity (precision). Our findings highlight the limitations of attention as a proxy for object relevance in autonomous driving, underscoring the need for more accurate proxies able to capture the causal impact of individual objects on the vehicle’s driving and distinguish genuine decision-making influence from correlational associations.

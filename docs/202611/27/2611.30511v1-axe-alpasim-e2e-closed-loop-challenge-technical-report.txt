Abstract 

This report presents a comprehensive pipeline for developing an end-to-end (E2E) autonomous driving model for the Physical AI AV Track. The framework con-sists of data curation, HD map auto-labelling, data-driven imitation learning, and simulator-based reinforcement learning. To ensure safety in the closed-loop set-ting, we introduce a selection-based E2E planning approach that leverages ob-ject detection and BEV segmentation to filter out trajectory candidates likely to cause collisions or off-road driving. Trained on NVIDIA NuRec and the NVIDIA PAI-AV dataset, our method scores a fixed vocabulary of trajectory candidates for collision, drivable-area compliance, and progress learned from simulated rollouts, and selects the best candidate that passes the filter. This design highlights the ef-fectiveness of combining E2E planning with perception-based trajectory filtering for safe and reliable closed-loop autonomous driving.

2 Policy description 

2.1 Data Curation 

We curate complementary driving clips to address scenario imbalance and model failures while lim-iting map-refinement effort. Starting from approximately 2K NuRec clips, we select additional data from a labeled 10K-clip subset of NVIDIA Physical AI (PAI), which contains approximately 300K clips overall. We retain the relatively accurate PAI object annotations and refine map annotations for selected clips before training. To select informative clips for downstream policy training, we apply three complementary selection strategies and select a total of 1,400 PAI-AV clips. 

Scenario-based Selection. We characterize clips using longitudinal and lateral ego motion and the distribution of surrounding objects to identify scenarios underrepresented in NuRec. We initially select clips to improve scenario coverage, including rare lane-change maneuvers. After initial model training, we select clips resembling low-scoring or failed scenarios, including turning maneuvers and high-speed straight driving. 

VLM-guided Scene Selection. To capture context beyond predefined scenario labels, we use a pretrained VLM to generate scene descriptions and tags. These guide the selection of clips with contexts similar to model failures, while visual inspection helps identify rare cases for fine-tuning. 

Scene-embedding-based Selection. We further select clips using distances between spatiotem-poral scene embeddings extracted by SparseDrive. A hierarchical scene encoder aggregates sparse object and map tokens within each frame and models their temporal evolution to obtain clip-level embeddings. These embeddings guide selection based on novelty relative to existing training data and similarity to failure scenarios. 

2.2 HD Map Auto-labeling 

We extend PseudoMapTrainer [1] and have developed our own auto-labeling framework to generate HD map annotations for downstream driving model training. 

Semantic Road Surface Reconstruction. PseudoMapTrainer only optimizes road surface Gaus-sians with perspective view RGB and semantic label images. This may result in inconsistent re-construction due to inconsistencies across the multi-view images. To address such limitations, we leverage auxiliary supervision from static point cloud reconstruction using LiDAR observations. In addition, we introduce DINOv3 [2] latent embedding to each Gaussian representation to boost multi-view consistency. 

Semantic HD Map Pseudo-Label Generation. The resulting semantically enhanced road Gaus-sian representation is rendered into ego-centric semantic Bird’s-Eye-View (BEV) maps, which serve as pseudo-labels for downstream driving model training. 22.3 End-to-End Autonomous Driving Model 

Our method formulates trajectory planning as selection from a fixed vocabulary of 8,192 candidate trajectories. The model encodes multi-view temporal observations into a bird’s-eye-view (BEV) representation and leverages perception outputs to filter infeasible candidates based on drivable-area constraints and object collisions, promoting safe driving. The remaining candidates are ranked through a coarse-to-fine scoring pipeline [3] to select a single trajectory for closed-loop execution. 

Inputs and BEV Encoding. The model takes three temporal frames ( t − 1.0, t − 0.5, and t) from three front and front-side cameras ( 512 × 256 ), the current frame from the two rear cameras ( 512 ×

256 each), along with ego state and 20 route waypoints. A DINOv3-pretrained ViT-S backbone [2] and a three-layer BEVFormer encoder [4] produce a 112 ×112 BEV grid, where ego-motion-aligned temporal features are fused via convolution. The two rear views are encoded separately by the same ViT-S, without temporal fusion or BEV projection, and pooled into 256 rear tokens. 

Trajectory Scoring and Selection. A three-layer transformer decoder scores 8,192 candidate tra-jectories from a fixed k-means trajectory vocabulary using 56 × 56 pooled BEV tokens, the 256 rear tokens, route features, and ego state. The scoring heads predict no-fault collision (NC), drivable-area compliance (DAC), ego progress (EP), their product (the overall scene score), and imitation similarity. A coarse-to-fine pipeline [3] ranks all candidates and re-scores the top 256 with a sep-arate refinement decoder over the same pooled BEV features and rear tokens. A perception-based feasibility gate, derived from perception heads operating on the same BEV representation, penalizes candidates that violate drivable-area constraints or overlap with detected objects. The highest-ranked candidate is output as a sequence of 40 poses (x, y, θ ) at 0.1 s intervals over a 4 s horizon and tracked by an MPC controller. 

2.4 Data-driven Imitation Learning 

During the imitation learning stage, the E2E model is trained on real-world driving logs using joint perception and planning objectives to learn scene understanding and trajectory selection. Focal loss [5] is used for 3D object classification, L1 loss with Hungarian matching [6] for bounding-box regression, and class-weighted cross-entropy for BEV segmentation. Soft cross-entropy is used to imitate recorded trajectories, while auxiliary losses predict simulator-generated NC, DAC, and EP labels and their product. Both planning losses are applied to all 8,192 candidates at the coarse stage and the top 256 at the fine stage. Further details are provided in Appendix A.4. 

2.5 Simulator-based Reinforcement Learning 

For online reinforcement learning (RL), we utilize the 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS)-based simu-lator, NVIDIA AlpaSim. To mitigate the computational overhead of 3DGS rendering and alleviate long-horizon credit assignment challenges, the RL agent interacts with the environment at a down-sampled frequency of 2 Hz, derived from the native 10 Hz simulation rate. We employ Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) [7] for online policy optimization. The episode reward decomposes into a step-wise function rt = RC t · LD t − Cor t − Tt, where RC t ∈ [0 , 1] and LD t ∈ [0 , 1] denote route completion and normalized lateral alignment, respectively. Cor t ∈ [0 , 1] is the corridor penalty for lateral distances between 1 m and 4 m. Tt ∈ { 0, 1} is a hard constraint penalty triggered by safety violations (e.g., collisions, off-road driving, or boundary cross-overs), which terminates the episode immediately upon violation. 

3 Resources and training 

3.1 Data Curation 

For additional training data, we use a labeled subset of approximately 10K clips from the NVIDIA PAI-AV dataset, which contains approximately 300K clips in total. We apply three complementary selection paradigms across four curation rounds: scenario-based coverage and failure-aware selec-tion, semantic retrieval using Qwen-based scene metadata [8], and feature-space selection using temporal scene representations extracted by SparseDrive [9]. The SparseDrive model used for fea-ture extraction was trained on an approximately 1K-clip subset of the labeled PAI data. Following 3deduplication across the curation rounds, 1,400 unique PAI-AV clips were retained. Their map an-notations were generated using the HD Map Auto-labeling pipeline described in Sec. 2.2 and 3.2, while the available object annotations were retained. The resulting labeled PAI-AV clips were com-bined with the NuRec clips for downstream end-to-end policy training. The selected datasets and models were used according to their respective usage terms and are listed in the references. 

3.2 HD Map Auto-labeling 

We generate HD map annotations on the curated NVIDIA PAI-AV clips using multi-view camera images, LiDAR point clouds, and ego poses. Our pipeline builds upon PseudoMapTrainer [1] for road surface reconstruction using Gaussian representation. For semantic label for Gaussian opti-mization, we employ off-the-shelf Mask2Former [10], trained on MapillaryVistasV2 [11] dataset, to extract semantic label predictions. We additionally use pretrained DINOv3 [2] ViT-L model to provide rich feature optimization. 

3.3 End-to-End Autonomous Driving Model 

The image backbone uses publicly available pre-trained DINOv3 weights [2], the BEV encoder follows the BEVFormer architecture [4], and trajectory scoring is based on DriveSuprim [3]. 

3.4 Data-driven Imitation Learning 

We train on NVIDIA NuRec 26.01 and 26.04 together with clips from the NVIDIA PAI-AV dataset. For NuRec, 3D bounding boxes and BEV drivable-area labels come from the provided annotations and maps. For PAI-AV, 3D bounding boxes are obtained by auto-labeling, and we additionally auto-label a BEV drivable-area raster for every frame, which serves both as the segmentation target and as the drivable area for DAC scoring. Planner labels are generated by tracking each of the 8,192 vocabulary trajectories with an MPC controller and scoring the rollout with NC, DAC, EP, and their product, with EP set to zero for lateral deviations exceeding 4 m from the GT trajectory. Training proceeds in three stages: 20 epochs of perception-only training, 5 epochs with frozen backbone and BEV encoder, and 5 epochs of joint training. We use AdamW [12] with a learning rate of 10 −4 and weight decay of 0.01; each stage starts with a 500-iteration linear warmup, followed by a cosine decay to 10 −6 scheduled over 30 epochs and truncated at the end of the stage. Training uses a batch size of 64 across four NVIDIA H200 GPUs. Further details are provided in Appendix A.4. 

3.5 Simulator-based Reinforcement Learning 

Closed-loop RL fine-tuning is conducted on AlpaSim using 1,761 training and 441 validation NuRec scenes across 48 parallel simulator sessions at a 2 Hz control frequency. Keeping the pretrained IL policy (48.3M parameters) fully frozen, we train only 0.54M LoRA [13] parameters placed on plan-ner’s transformer decoder layers and a separate 1.78M critic network. We fine-tune using PPO [7] with an exact KL penalty ( β = 0 .02 ) relative to the SFT policy, optimized via AdamW [12] at a constant learning rate of 1 × 10 −4 and a rollout buffer batch size of 2,048 steps (minibatch size 512) for 17 epochs across 8× NVIDIA H100 GPUs. 

4 Limitations and safety considerations 

Failure Modes. The planner selects from a fixed set of 8,192 trajectories clustered from recorded driving, so maneuvers outside this set (e.g., tight U-turns or multi-point turns) cannot be produced, and rare situations are covered by fewer nearby candidates. 

Limitations. Trajectories are scored and selected using collision avoidance, drivable-area compli-ance, ego progress, and imitation. Ride comfort (e.g., acceleration, jerk, and yaw rate) is not part of the selection criteria, so the chosen trajectories can be less smooth than human driving. 

Safety Considerations. The candidate filter relies on the model’s own front-view BEV perception, so missed detections and rear agents, seen by the planner only through rear-camera tokens, can pass it. Traffic-light state is not part of the planner labels, so the planner can proceed through a red light. 4Acknowledgments

A.1.3 Spatiotemporal Feature Diversity 

Traditional rule-based scenario classification is limited by its inability to distinguish between dis-crete clips assigned to the same semantic class (e.g., straight driving, accelerating, or environments containing only vehicles). While suitable for initial data curation, this approach necessitates expan-sion into a continuous latent space for finer granularity. To address this, we use a scene encoder to extract clip-level spatiotemporal embeddings, which enable the identification and selection of novel and diverse driving clips. By leveraging the indices of previously trained and failed evaluation cases, we use the latent space distance to identify new samples that exhibit novel features and can potentially mitigate known failure modes. A.2 HD Map Auto-labeling 

To automatically generate HD map annotations on the curated PAI-AV data, we adopt a PMT-based auto-labeling pipeline that employs RoGS [14] for scene-level road surface reconstruction. We en-hance its semantic representation by incorporating LiDAR-derived semantic supervision and DI-NOv3 [2] feature distillation. The overall auto-labeling pipeline is illustrated in Figure 8. 

Semantic Road Surface Reconstruction. RoGS [14] relies on perspective-view RGB and se-mantic supervision, which may lead to inconsistent road surface reconstruction due to discrepancies across camera views. To mitigate this issue, we incorporate additional 3D semantic supervision from a globally aggregated static LiDAR point cloud, with semantic consistency improved through multi-view majority voting. Specifically, we project LiDAR points onto multiple camera images and assign semantic labels using Mask2Former [10] predictions, resolving conflicting labels through majority voting across views. After filtering out dynamic objects and retaining static road-related classes, we transform the labeled points into the global coordinate frame and aggregate them across frames. Semantic HD Map Pseudo-Label Generation. Following the PMT auto-labeling pipeline, we generate BEV drivable area pseudo-labels by rendering the reconstructed Gaussian representation within an ego-centric region. Specifically, we define the BEV region to cover 5 m behind and 160 m ahead of the ego vehicle, with a lateral extent of 40 m on each side. Unlike PMT, which considers multiple semantic map categories, we adopt a binary semantic representation that distinguishes only between drivable and non-drivable areas. The resulting BEV segmentation maps serve as pseudo-labels for training the End-to-End Autonomous Driving model. Additionally, we apply rule-based post-processing to the BEV segmentation maps to generate vectorized HD map annotations com-prising dividers, road boundaries, and pedestrian crossings. Qualitative examples of the generated BEV segmentation and vector map annotations are presented in Figure 9. 

A.3 End-to-End Autonomous Driving Model Model Input. Along with the camera images, the model takes a projection matrix for each front and front-side camera image, which the BEV encoder uses to map BEV locations to image pixels. It also takes the current frames of the rear-left and rear-right cameras ( 512 × 256 each), which are not projected into the BEV. The ego state s ∈ R4 consists of the current velocity (vx, v y ) and acceleration (ax, a y ); the driving command is not used, as the route replaces it. The model also receives the poses of the two past frames relative to the current rear-axle frame, which are used to align past BEV features with the current frame. 11 Figure 9: Visualization of automatically generated BEV segmentation and vector HD map anno-tations. The binary segmentation distin-guishes drivable and non-drivable areas, while rule-based post-processing generates vectorized map elements comprising dividers (blue), road boundaries (red), and pedestrian crossings (yellow). Feature Extractor. The DINOv3 [2] ViT-S/16 image backbone encodes each camera image into 

32 ×16 patch features at stride 16, which are upsampled and downsampled into a three-level pyramid (strides 8, 16 and 32) with 256 channels per level. In the BEVFormer [4] encoder, the grid covers 0–56 m ahead of the vehicle and ±28 m to each side at 0.5 m resolution, and each cell is a learnable query that gathers features from this pyramid around its projected locations in each camera that sees it. To save computation, the two past frames pass through only the first encoder layer, without gradients during training, and the encoder outputs a BEV feature map F ∈ R256 ×112 ×112 that is shared by the perception heads and the planner. The drivable-area head applies two ConvNeXt-V2 [15] blocks to F to predict whether each BEV cell is drivable. The detection head also reads F

and follows the DETR-style BEVFormer head [6, 4], with 600 object queries that predict scores for seven classes (vehicle, pedestrian, bicycle, traffic cone, barrier, construction-zone sign and generic object) and a 3D box with position, size, orientation and velocity. The two rear images pass through the same ViT-S one at a time; their 32 × 16 patch maps are placed side by side, projected to 256 channels by a 1 × 1 convolution with GroupNorm and GELU, average-pooled to 32 × 8 = 256 

tokens, and given learned positional embeddings. They use only the current frame, bypass the BEV encoder, and in the final training stage send no gradient to the shared backbone. 

Trajectory Vocabulary. The vocabulary is built from the 4 s future trajectory at every frame with a full 4 s future in the NuRec and PAI-AV clips, sampled as eight poses (x, y, θ ) at 0.5 s intervals. We first remove trajectories that the MPC controller cannot track. Because turns and lane changes are rare, we add their left–right mirror images, which stay within the controller limits. The trajectories are then interpolated to 40 steps at 0.1 s and clustered with bisecting k-means, splitting a cluster only if both halves keep at least five trajectories, so every entry averages at least five recorded or mirrored trajectories and satisfies the controller limits. 

Feasibility Gate. The gate checks each candidate against the predicted drivable area and detec-tions and returns a binary mask (Figure 10). The ego footprint is the clip’s rig bounding box (length and width scaled by 0.98), placed ahead of the rear-axle pose by the rear-axle-to-center offset and enlarged by 1.1. A candidate fails if, at any of its 40 poses, a footprint corner inside the BEV range falls on a cell with drivable probability below 0.6, or if, within its first 10 poses (1 s), the footprint overlaps a detection with confidence above 0.4 under the separating axis theorem; detections are en-larged by 2.0 for pedestrians and bicycles and 1.1 otherwise, and moved at their predicted velocity with fixed heading. Planner. In the coarse stage, each vocabulary trajectory (40 poses (x, y, θ )) is flattened, embedded by an MLP, and passed through one self-attention layer over all candidates to form the decoder queries. The keys and values are the pooled BEV tokens with learned positional embeddings and the 256 rear tokens, concatenated with 20 route tokens (one per waypoint, with empty slots masked). The ego state and a route summary are added to each candidate’s decoder output, from which MLP heads predict NC, DAC, EP, the overall score and imitation. The coarse score is the product of the predicted NC, DAC and EP plus the imitation score normalized to [0 , 1] , all with weight 1.0; the overall-score head is trained but not used for ranking. Candidates failing the feasibility gate are demoted by the score range plus one, and the top 256 are kept. In the fine stage, their decoder outputs serve as queries of a three-layer refinement decoder with its own heads, attending to the pooled BEV tokens, the rear tokens and its own route tokens. All three layers are supervised, and the final trajectory is the candidate with the highest last-layer score, computed as in the coarse stage but with an imitation weight of 0.02. 

Controller. Planner labels are generated with a batched re-implementation of the AlpaSim lin-ear MPC controller, so they reflect how the simulator would drive each candidate. The vehicle is a dynamic bicycle model (kinematic below 5 m/s) with a 2.85 m wheelbase, whose steering and accel-eration follow their commands through first-order lags of 0.1 s. Every 0.1 s, the controller linearizes the model about the current state and solves a quadratic program over a 2 s horizon, penalizing lon-gitudinal, lateral and heading errors (weights 2.0, 1.0 and 1.0), the acceleration state (0.1), and the steering and acceleration commands (5.0 and 1.0), with tracking applied only from 1 s ahead. The states are bounded by a heading change of at most 90 ◦, 0 ≤ vx ≤ 35 m/s, a steering angle of at most 

45 ◦, and −8 ≤ a ≤ 6 m/s 2. Each of the 8,192 vocabulary trajectories is simulated for 4 s from the recorded ego state, re-solving the MPC at every step. 

A.4 Data-driven Imitation Learning Data Filtering. We remove clips with unreliable labels or duplicated drives. For NuRec, these are clips with reverse driving that the MPC cannot follow, missing or inconsistent map or route 13 geometry, or object boxes unsupported by any camera frame. For PAI-AV, these are clips in which the ego vehicle leaves the annotated drivable area, along with clips that duplicate a NuRec drive. 

Perception Loss. BEV segmentation uses class-weighted cross-entropy with inverse-frequency class weights and a loss weight of 4.5. Detection follows the BEVFormer head [4]: a sigmoid focal loss [5] ( γ = 2 , α = 0 .25 ) for classification with weight 2.0 and an L1 loss on the 3D box parameters with weight 0.25. Queries are assigned by Hungarian matching [6] on the same weighted sum of the focal classification cost and the L1 box cost, and every decoder layer is supervised. 

Planning Loss. The imitation and score losses follow DriveSuprim [3]. The imitation target is a softmax over negative squared distances to the recorded trajectory at eight timestamps (0.5 s apart), with temperature σ = 0 .5. The loss weights are 1.0 for imitation and 3.0 (NC), 3.0 (DAC), 2.0 (EP), and 1.0 (their product) for the score heads. Scenes with identical labels for all candidates, mostly beyond the MPC speed limit, are excluded from the score losses. 

Training Stage. Stage 1 initializes the image backbone from DINOv3 [2] and disables the plan-ning losses; each later stage initializes from the previous one with a fresh optimizer and schedule. We keep DriveSuprim’s [3] EMA teacher, which is used at inference, and student-only photometric augmentation, with momentum rising from 0.992 to 0.998 over the first three epochs, but do not use its rotation-augmented views. The feasibility gate is also applied during Stages 2 and 3 using predicted detections and drivable area rather than ground truth, so the fine stage is trained on the can-didate distribution it receives at inference. Training uses bf16 mixed precision and gradient clipping at a global norm of 35. 

A.5 Simulator-based Reinforcement Learning Policy Architecture and Optimization. We train our policy using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) [7] and Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE) [16]. The actor is parameterized via Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA), while the critic is trained jointly. Both networks are optimized using AdamW ( β1 = 0 .9, β2 = 0 .999 , ϵ = 10 −5) with a constant learning rate of 10 −4. Weight decay is set to 0.01 for LoRA parameters. 

Experience Rollout and Distributed Training. Each update collects a buffer of about 2,048 de-cision steps from 48 concurrent simulation sessions. Each update runs up to 3 PPO epochs over 4 minibatches of 512 steps, i.e. up to 12 optimizer steps. Training uses 8× NVIDIA H100 (80 GB) GPUs with data parallelism. Each GPU hosts one learner rank, which also serves policy inference for the sessions it owns, together with seven neural-rendering instances and a physics service. Each rank processes about 64 steps per minibatch with a micro-batch size of 8 via gradient accumulation. 

RL Objectives and Regularization. To align with the 10 Hz decision frequency, we set discount 

γ = 0 .951 (0.99 5) and GAE λ = 0 .774 (0.95 5). Policy clip ϵ, value clip threshold, and value loss coefficient are set to 0.1, 0.1, and 0.5, respectively. To prevent deviation from the SFT baseline, an exact KL penalty ( βKL = 0 .02 ) is computed over 256 candidates, and training early-stops if approximate KL exceeds 1.5 × 0.03 . Gradients are clipped independently at norm 0.5. Advantage normalization is executed per minibatch across all GPUs. Policy entropy is regulated via a sampling temperature targeting 2.5 nats without explicit entropy loss. 

Implementation Details. The complete training run consists of two stages. The first stage spans 

12 epochs over 2,202 scenes, comprising 26 ,424 episodes and 391 PPO updates, and completes in approximately 29 .5 hours. The second stage continues from this checkpoint for 5 additional epochs on the 894 scenes with mixed success and failure outcomes, adding 4,470 episodes and 69 PPO updates in approximately 5 hours. In total, training comprises 30 ,894 episodes and 460 PPO updates over approximately 34 .5 hours of wall-clock time on the 8× H100 setup.
